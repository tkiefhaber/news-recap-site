Houthis seize Mocha, tightening their grip on the Bab el-Mandeb strait

Facts

  • Iran-aligned Houthi forces took the Red Sea port city of Mocha on Thursday and pushed toward high ground overlooking the Bab el-Mandeb strait, about 50 miles away.
  • The corridor carries roughly 10% of global trade and by most estimates 5–7% of seaborne oil. It had become the main workaround for Gulf crude after the Strait of Hormuz was effectively closed to routine commercial traffic in July.
  • The Houthis have declared a blockade aimed at Saudi-linked shipping and said the strait is closed to specified vessels.
  • Brent traded above $107 Thursday before easing; Hormuz traffic is down from more than 100 vessels a day before the war to roughly five, with Gulf crude exports off about 47% from 2025 levels.

Left view

MSNBC's opinion desk calls the seizure a much bigger deal than the administration is letting on and argues the Iran campaign has produced exactly the escalation critics warned about. NPR and CNN emphasize that roughly 45 days of U.S. strikes on the Houthis last year did not degrade the group's ability to project force, and that the White House has no stated plan beyond more bombing.

Right view

RedState and the Washington Times frame it as proof that Iran's proxy network survives because Washington keeps stopping short — the argument being that limited strikes invite this. The Wall Street Journal editorial line has been that freedom of navigation requires a standing naval commitment, not episodic retaliation. Reason and other libertarian-leaning outlets take the opposite tack on the right, noting the Houthi campaign has already cost taxpayers north of $1 billion with little to show.

Watch for

Energy analysts see Brent staying in a $100–110 band while both chokepoints are contested; some forecasters have Brent averaging around $106 for 2026. Watch war-risk insurance premiums on Red Sea transits, Suez Canal transit volumes, and whether Saudi Arabia leans harder on the East–West pipeline to Yanbu. Bank of America has estimated sustained oil above $100 shaves more than 60 basis points off U.S. GDP growth. A U.S. or Saudi move to retake Mocha is the tail risk that would spike prices further.

Supreme Court blocks Missouri's Republican-drawn House map for the second time this week

Facts

  • The justices on Thursday paused a federal judge's order that would have required Missouri to use a new 7–1 Republican-favoring congressional map in November.
  • The existing 6–2 map drawn after the 2020 census stays in effect for the general election while litigation continues.
  • Last week the Missouri Supreme Court unanimously blocked the Trump-backed map and ordered the question put to a statewide referendum, setting up the conflict between state and federal rulings.
  • The fight sits downstream of Louisiana v. Callais, the 6–3 decision in April narrowing when the Voting Rights Act can compel additional majority-minority districts.

Left view

The Washington Post and Democracy Docket present this as a rare speed bump in a redistricting wave the Court itself opened, with tallies suggesting Republicans could net well into double digits in House seats from post-Callais map redraws. NBC News reports on mounting criticism that the Court is intervening in election cases too close to voting.

Right view

Conservative legal commentators, including at National Review and The Dispatch, argue the ruling is procedurally correct rather than a defeat — the Purcell principle counsels against changing rules near an election, and it cuts both ways. Fox News coverage stresses that Missouri Republicans were following a legislature's prerogative and that Democratic states including California and Virginia have run the same play.

Watch for

Election forecasters were already treating House control as close to a coin flip; holding Missouri at 6–2 removes one seat from the GOP column. Watch the Missouri referendum timing, whether Georgia and Tennessee maps draw similar emergency applications, and whether the Court takes the Missouri case on the merits after November.

Trump's $5,000 "dividend" pledge draws bipartisan pushback

Facts

  • Closing the first-ever Republican midterm convention in Dallas Wednesday night, Trump promised every U.S. adult a $5,000 payment if the GOP holds both chambers in November.
  • He did not specify a funding source. Applied to roughly 270 million adults, the cost would exceed $1.3 trillion.
  • Sen. Bernie Moreno (R-Ohio) said he would draft legislation after the November 3 election. Former Rep. Bob Good (R-Va.) called it a socialist vote-buying scheme.
  • Trump also predicted at the convention that the war with Iran would end shortly after the election.

Left view

CNBC and NBC News lead on the fiscal math and on legal questions about conditioning payments on an election outcome; Rep. Robert Garcia (D-Calif.) said nobody believes the checks are coming. The Guardian and Vox frame the pledge as an admission that affordability is the GOP's weakest issue heading into November.

Right view

Fox News reports open division inside the party, with fiscal conservatives objecting to a new entitlement layered on the debt. National Review and The Dispatch have been dismissive on the same grounds. The supportive case, made by Moreno and parts of the populist right, is that tariff revenue should be rebated to households rather than absorbed by Washington.

Watch for

Economists across the spectrum flag the inflation risk of a $1.3 trillion transfer with CPI already near 3.4% and oil above $100. Watch whether any actual bill text appears before Election Day, how the Congressional Budget Office scores it if so, and whether Treasury yields — the 10-year hit its highest level since 2023 on Thursday — keep pricing in looser fiscal policy.

Protections for about 200,000 Salvadorans left in limbo as DHS delays a decision

Facts

  • Temporary Protected Status for Salvadorans was set to expire September 9. DHS instead said the status remains in effect until further notice, without formally extending it.
  • Roughly 200,000 people are covered, many of them in the U.S. for two decades or more.
  • Since January 2025 the administration has ended or moved to end TPS for 13 countries, affecting more than a million people, including Haitians and Syrians.
  • Reporting points to the relationship between Trump and Salvadoran President Nayib Bukele — who has accepted U.S. deportees — as a distinguishing factor.

Left view

The Washington Post and NBC News center the human cost of indefinite limbo: work authorization that could lapse without notice, mortgages, and U.S.-citizen children. The Guardian and Mother Jones argue the selective treatment shows TPS decisions are being driven by diplomatic favor-trading rather than the statutory country-conditions test.

Right view

The Washington Times and the New York Post note that "temporary" status lasting 25 years is the anomaly the administration is trying to unwind, and that Congress, not DHS, should decide permanent legal status. The Free Press and Reason split from that: both have argued that abruptly stripping status from long-settled workers is bad policy on economic grounds regardless of the original designation.

Watch for

A Federal Register notice is the trigger to watch — it would set the wind-down clock. Also watch litigation in the Ninth Circuit, where earlier TPS terminations were challenged, and whether the delay holds past November. Construction and hospitality labor markets in Texas, Virginia, Maryland and California are the most exposed.

IAEA says North Korea has built a second enrichment plant at Yongbyon

Facts

  • The IAEA reported a new two-story building at the Yongbyon complex capable of housing up to 28 centrifuge cascades, identified by matching state-media photos of a June Kim Jong Un visit against satellite imagery.
  • An expanded annex at the Kangson site near Pyongyang also appears to be operating.
  • Construction started in mid-to-late 2024; the shell was finished by mid-2025 with internal installation continuing.
  • The agency has had no inspectors in the country since 2009. Director General Rafael Grossi called the expansion of weapons-grade material production a serious concern and said it violates U.N. Security Council resolutions.

Left view

NPR and The Atlantic treat this as evidence that summit-driven diplomacy without verification produced nothing durable, and argue the absence of inspectors since 2009 is the core failure. Reuters and CNN coverage stresses that Pyongyang's program has expanded through three U.S. administrations.

Right view

National Review and the Washington Examiner point to the report as an argument against sanctions relief in advance of verified dismantlement, and press for tighter enforcement on Chinese entities facilitating trade. Some on the right also note the U.S. is stretched across Iran and the Red Sea, leaving little bandwidth for the Korean peninsula.

Watch for

Analysts expect this to feed South Korea's domestic debate over its own deterrent and to strengthen the case in Tokyo for higher defense spending. Watch the IAEA Board of Governors meeting response, any U.S. force-posture signaling in the region, and whether Beijing acknowledges the findings at all.

Twenty-five years on from 9/11, a divided country marks the anniversary

Facts

  • The commemoration at the National September 11 Memorial began at 8:30 a.m. ET with an in-person reading of the names by family members and the first moment of silence at 8:46 a.m.
  • Seven moments of silence mark each tower strike and collapse, the Pentagon attack, the Flight 93 crash, and those who have died since from 9/11-related illness.
  • Ceremonies are also being held at the Pentagon in Arlington and at the Flight 93 memorial in Shanksville, Pennsylvania.
  • A live broadcast of the Tribute in Light from Lower Manhattan airs at 8 p.m. ET. The anniversary also falls a day after the one-year mark of Charlie Kirk's assassination; his accused killer, Tyler Robinson, has pleaded not guilty to aggravated murder and faces trial.

Left view

NPR's framing is that a country that briefly unified in 2001 now gathers to remember while split on nearly everything else, including what the post-9/11 wars accomplished. The Atlantic and Vox have used the quarter-century mark to revisit the surveillance and detention architecture built after the attacks and how much of it remains.

Right view

The Washington Times and New York Post emphasize the first responders, the compensation fund, and the continuing toll of 9/11-related illness. National Review's anniversary coverage argues the counterterrorism apparatus is the reason there has been no comparable attack on U.S. soil, and warns against dismantling it.

Watch for

Practical items rather than predictions: the World Trade Center Health Program's funding position, the pace of Guantanamo military-commission proceedings for the accused plotters, and how much airtime the anniversary shares with the Red Sea and midterm news cycles.
