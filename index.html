US and Iran trade fire again: Larak Island strike, then missiles at bases in Jordan

Facts

  • US forces struck two Iranian launchers on Larak Island after observing IRGC units preparing to fire rockets carrying sea mines into the Strait of Hormuz, US officials said. It was the first strike on Iranian territory in roughly a month.
  • Early Monday the IRGC said its Aerospace Force hit the King Hussein and Azraq airbases in Jordan in a combined missile-and-drone operation, and warned that further US attacks would draw a stronger response. Al Jazeera reported Iran also claimed strikes on a site in the UAE.
  • Jordan's armed forces said they intercepted eight missiles that entered Jordanian airspace. Iran said the US strike killed and wounded both fighters and civilians and called it a grave blunder.
  • The war began in February. A 60-day ceasefire framework lapsed earlier this month, and Trump has said no talks with Tehran are scheduled. He maintains Hormuz is open and demined; CENTCOM has not confirmed that, referring questions to the White House.
  • Congress has twice rebuked the administration on war powers — the House passed a resolution 214–208 in July with four Republicans joining Democrats, while a companion Senate measure failed 47–49.

Left view

NPR frames this as a war that has outrun its own promises: a swift victory was pledged in February, and six months on the conflict has battered the global economy, strained Gulf allies and drawn down US munitions stocks. Al Jazeera and left-leaning commentators emphasize that each round of tit-for-tat now lands on third countries — Jordan intercepting missiles aimed at American bases on Jordanian soil — and argue that with no negotiating track, escalation is the only remaining policy. Democrats who backed the war powers resolutions, led in the House by Rep. Pramila Jayapal, say the campaign has no defined mission or endpoint and that Congress was never consulted.

Right view

Fox News coverage stresses the operational trigger: Iranian forces were caught in the act of preparing to lay mines in a waterway the US has committed to keeping open, making the strike preemptive rather than escalatory. Fox contributor Lara Logan cautioned that Iran treats retaliation as a long game and would be unlikely to stop here. The right is not unified, though — Sens. Rand Paul, Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski and Bill Cassidy have crossed party lines on Iran war powers, and House Republicans Thomas Massie and Warren Davidson among others have voted to constrain the campaign.

Watch for

CNBC's read is that the strike reflects Washington's frustration that sanctions are working too slowly, which implies more kinetic action rather than less. Energy analysts see the risk premium re-entering crude: Brent is near $88 after peaking above $110 in May, and BloombergNEF has sketched a path toward $91 in late 2026 on sustained Iranian disruption. EIA now assumes Hormuz constraints persist into early 2027 with roughly 0.6 million barrels a day of lasting disruption. Watch whether Gulf states quietly distance themselves from US basing, whether Jordan's government faces domestic pressure, and whether war-risk insurance rates move again — that is usually the first place a genuine escalation shows up.

Venezuela oil deal draws fire from both parties — including the WSJ editorial board

Facts

  • Trump announced an agreement giving the United States a stake in Venezuela's oil reserves — the largest proven reserves in the world — following the January raid that captured Nicolás Maduro and his extradition to New York on federal drug-trafficking charges.
  • The White House has released few specifics. Drilling timelines, funding structure, and how revenue is split remain unclear, as does whether Congress has any role. Trump called it the biggest oil deal in world history.
  • The Wall Street Journal reported Chevron and Halliburton are in talks to invest billions in Venezuelan fields; Bloomberg confirmed Chevron is nearing a deal to expand there.
  • Fortune noted the unusual structure: the US federal government would effectively control a company sitting on the world's second-largest reserves, something it has no domestic equivalent for.
  • Venezuelans surveyed by Fortune were largely critical of their own government's role in handing over the reserves.

Left view

Democrats say the deal retroactively exposes the motive for the Maduro operation. Sen. Tim Kaine (D-Va.) called it corruption at epic scale and argued gas prices will not fall by anything close to what the Iran war added to them. Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.) told The Hill this is not a win and said service members were put at risk to secure oil for well-connected firms. House Oversight Democrats have an open investigation into administration communications with oil companies during the Venezuela takeover.

Right view

Sen. Bernie Moreno (R-Ohio) defends it as historic and mutually beneficial, arguing that the alternative was Maduro in power and Venezuelan crude flowing to China at a discount. But the right is split in a notable way: the Wall Street Journal editorial board ran a piece titled "Trump Takes the Oil in Venezuela," comparing the arrangement to a scene from The Godfather Part II, questioning its legality and calling it crony statism. That is a free-market conservative objection, not a progressive one — the complaint is about the state owning an oil company, not about intervention.

Watch for

Energy analysts note Venezuelan heavy crude needs years of capital and infrastructure before it moves global prices, so anyone promising near-term pump relief is overselling. The near-term market question is whether Chevron and Halliburton actually sign and what indemnities they demand against future US or Venezuelan legal challenge. Watch for the legal architecture to surface — whether this runs through an executive agreement, a sanctions carve-out, or something requiring Senate action — and whether the WSJ editorial line spreads to Senate Republicans, which is the signal that would make this a real intraparty fight rather than a one-off.

Fed's Warsh signals rate hikes are back on the table — ten weeks before the midterms

Facts

  • In his Jackson Hole keynote Friday, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said the central bank has "work to do" if it is not confident inflation is returning to 2% clearly and quickly — read across markets as an explicit opening for hikes.
  • CPI rose 3.4% over the twelve months through July; the Fed's preferred PCE measure ran 3.7% over the same period.
  • Warsh said he was impressed by the economy's overall strength but troubled that underlying inflation trends have not improved. He recommitted to the 2% PCE target and declined to offer forward guidance or a reaction function.
  • Markets repriced immediately: the S&P 500 fell 0.25% Friday to 7,711.76 and the Nasdaq dropped 0.52%, though the S&P still closed the week higher.
  • Trump, who appointed Warsh in January, has said he would not have chosen him if he wanted rate hikes, and has blamed the Fed's board for what he calls political bias.

Left view

NPR's framing is that the inflation Warsh is fighting is substantially self-inflicted — an energy shock produced by a war the administration chose and a tariff regime it expanded — which makes attacks on the Fed a deflection. Democrats point to the same polling Republicans are seeing and argue voters are correctly attributing $4 gas and 3.4% CPI to policy rather than to the central bank.

Right view

The Washington Examiner reports that market expectations of a hike "haunt Warsh and the GOP," acknowledging the political problem directly: a pre-election hike would spotlight mortgage and auto loan costs for households already stretched. The Hill reports Republicans are divided over a pre-election stimulus push in response. Hawkish conservatives and the Fed-independence wing counter that credibility is the whole asset — that a chair who blinks under presidential pressure gets neither lower inflation nor lower long rates.

Watch for

The next CPI and PCE prints are the whole ballgame; another 3-handle makes a hike hard to avoid and a September or October move increasingly live. Economists flag the collision course between the White House and a chair Trump himself picked, and the possibility Trump escalates rhetorically against the board rather than the chair. For markets, watch the long end — the 30-year already hit a 19-year high this month — and rate-sensitive sectors. Politically, watch whether GOP leadership converges on a stimulus or tax-relief package as a hedge.

Russian drone strike on Kyiv-area depot kills 38 — the war's deadliest attack this year

Facts

  • A Russian drone hit a warehouse in Myla, about three miles west of Kyiv, at roughly 8:10 p.m. local time Friday. Ukrainian officials say the site stored shells, mines and other ammunition.
  • The secondary detonations set off fires that swept through dozens of nearby homes and buildings. The toll rose to 38 by Sunday, per Zelenskyy — the deadliest single attack of 2026.
  • Emergency crews were still extinguishing fires Sunday and bomb disposal teams were clearing unexploded ordnance from the site.
  • Russia hit Kyiv for a third consecutive night on Aug. 29–30, with repeated air raid alerts and fires reported in the capital.
  • Zelenskyy said Russia launched nearly 2,000 attack drones, more than 1,600 glide bombs and 31 missiles — including North Korean ballistic missiles — at Ukraine in the past week alone.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian's coverage centers the civilian cost and the pattern: near-continuous drone saturation designed to exhaust air defenses and civilian endurance rather than to take ground. The presence of North Korean ballistic missiles in the salvo is treated as evidence that sanctions have failed to isolate Moscow's supply chain, and as an argument for accelerating Western air defense transfers rather than conditioning them.

Right view

Coverage on the right is comparatively thin today — Ukraine has been crowded out by Iran and Venezuela across right-leaning outlets. Where it appears, the framing splits: hawkish voices at National Review treat the North Korean missiles as proof of an axis requiring a harder line, while restraint-minded outlets argue a US already fighting Iran and reworking Venezuela cannot sustain a third theater and should push toward a negotiated end. Worth flagging: the depot detail — that the target was an ammunition store — is being used by some commentators to contest the "civilian massacre" framing, though the deaths were overwhelmingly in surrounding homes.

Watch for

Analysts expect the drone-volume trend to continue climbing, which puts interceptor supply, not political will, as the binding constraint through autumn. Watch European air defense pledges in September, whether the North Korean missile evidence triggers new secondary sanctions, and whether the Kyiv strike shifts the administration's posture — Ukraine policy has been on the back burner while Iran consumes attention and munitions inventory, and those two theaters compete for the same interceptors.

Nepal–Tibet flood toll passes 900 with more than 4,000 still missing

Facts

  • Flash floods on Aug. 26 tore through dozens of settlements along a 72-km stretch of the Trishuli River in Nepal.
  • Confirmed figures now stand at roughly 903 dead and 4,247 missing in Nepal, plus 16 dead and 546 missing in Tibet — a sharp rise from the 750 reported a day earlier.
  • The trigger is believed to be a glacier collapse on Langtang Lirung, which produced a tremor of magnitude Ms 5.2 registered by seismometers worldwide.
  • At least 261 foreigners are among the missing, including 18 US citizens. Earlier NPR reporting put the number of missing Americans as high as 90 before identifications narrowed it.
  • Floodwaters carried some victims' bodies as far as 240 km downstream into India. Poor weather continues to hamper rescue and recovery.

Left view

The Guardian and NPR tie the glacier collapse directly to Himalayan warming and accelerating glacial lake outburst risk, arguing this is the category of disaster climate scientists have warned about for a decade and that early-warning infrastructure in the region is badly underfunded. Coverage also notes the aid response is arriving late and thin relative to the scale.

Right view

Right-leaning outlets are covering this primarily as a consular story — the missing Americans and the State Department's response — rather than as a climate story, with some commentators cautioning against attributing a single glacier collapse to warming without attribution analysis. The Free Press and similar outlets have focused on rescue logistics and the limits of aid in remote high-altitude terrain. Mainstream and center coverage is doing most of the work here; there is not much of a partisan argument yet.

Watch for

Disaster researchers expect the missing count to convert largely into confirmed deaths, which would place this among the deadliest Himalayan events on record. Watch for a formal glaciological assessment of the Langtang Lirung collapse, pressure on Nepal and China to build out GLOF early-warning systems, and the State Department's consular accounting of the 18 Americans. Downstream, monsoon-season debris flows in the same drainage remain a live risk for weeks.

NASA's Roman Space Telescope launches on a Falcon Heavy

Facts

  • The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope lifted off at 7:26 a.m. EDT Sunday from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy.
  • Roman separated from the rocket on schedule at 7:57 a.m. EDT, 31 minutes after launch, and NASA concluded launch coverage with the vehicle healthy.
  • Next steps are solar panel deployment and a mid-course correction burn en route to the L2 Lagrange point, about one million miles from Earth on the anti-sun side.
  • The $4 billion observatory's science program targets dark energy and dark matter, plus a wide-field exoplanet survey — its field of view is roughly 100 times Hubble's at comparable resolution.
  • Trump called in live during NASA's post-launch press conference.

Left view

Left-leaning science coverage frames Roman as a vindication of long-horizon federal science funding — a mission conceived over a decade ago, repeatedly threatened with cancellation in budget cycles, that survived to launch. The implicit argument is a caution against the deeper NASA science cuts proposed in recent budget requests.

Right view

Coverage on the right emphasizes the public-private model: a NASA payload on a commercial Falcon Heavy at a fraction of the historical launch cost, offered as evidence that competitive contracting outperforms legacy government-built launch systems. This is one of the rare stories today where both sides are essentially pleased and are simply crediting different things.

Watch for

Roman needs roughly a month to reach L2, followed by a commissioning period before first light. The first cosmology data releases are where the actual stakes sit — Roman is designed to test whether dark energy's equation of state is constant, and a deviation would be the most consequential physics result in a generation. Nearer term, watch the mid-course correction burn confirmation and the instrument checkout milestones.
