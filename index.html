US destroys five Iranian tankers; Tehran hits Jordan base as the “tanker war” widens

Facts

  • CENTCOM says US forces struck five Iranian oil tankers in the latest round of tit-for-tat attacks, after Iran's Revolutionary Guard fired ballistic missiles toward a US carrier and destroyer near the Strait of Hormuz. No US ships were hit and no servicemembers injured, per the Pentagon.
  • Iran retaliated with strikes on a US base in Jordan and on commercial vessels it says are US-linked, according to Al Jazeera and ABC News. Iranian officials report at least 11 dead and dozens wounded from US strikes in southern Iran.
  • The exchanges follow a “tanker for tanker” policy Trump approved in early September, first reported by Axios. Secretary of State Marco Rubio framed it as a standing rule: Iranian attacks on US ships cost Iran tankers.
  • The war began Feb. 28 with joint US–Israeli strikes; Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz in response. An April 7 ceasefire lapsed. Hormuz traffic is down from roughly 100 vessels a day to about five, per Al Jazeera's shipping analysis.
  • Gulf crude exports have fallen roughly 47%, from about 17 million barrels a day in 2025 to roughly 9 million bpd as of August.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian keep the focus on legal authority: seven months in, there is still no congressional authorization, and the White House's argument that the War Powers Resolution's 60-day clock stopped at the April ceasefire is doing a lot of work. Schiff, Kaine and Schumer are pressing another War Powers vote. Just Security frames the tanker exchanges as evidence the “hostilities ended” theory has collapsed. The broader critique: a war of attrition with no defined end state, escalating by reflex.

Right view

Notably, the sharpest skepticism is coming from the right, not just the left. National Review has run pieces arguing the Iran war is “eating Trump's presidency” and that his adversaries have concluded he is bluffing — questioning whether he has the patience for the economic-pressure campaign he announced. Washington Examiner coverage ties voter fatigue to the midterms. Fox News and administration allies counter that proportional retaliation is the only language Tehran responds to, and that backing off now would invite more attacks on US ships. Al Jazeera's opinion page has carried the case that the strategy is starting to pay off.

Watch for

Analysts at Sparta and the Stimson Center warn that a Hormuz disruption sustained through Q3 puts roughly 12 million bpd of export supply at risk and could drive a shallow global recession in the second half of 2026. Near-term markers: whether Iran strikes a US-flagged vessel (a likely escalation trigger), whether Gulf states permit further use of their airspace, and whether the next War Powers vote draws Republican defectors. Freight and war-risk premiums on Gulf routes are the cleanest real-time gauge of how the market reads the risk.

Watch the September FOMC decision, the next CPI print, and whether SPR releases or an OPEC+ response materialize. Canada's counter-tariffs take effect on roughly $20B of American goods

Facts

  • Canada's retaliatory tariffs took effect just after midnight ET Tuesday, covering hundreds of product categories. Ottawa's own accounting puts the figure at $27.6 billion CAD; US reporting generally cites roughly $20 billion.
  • Rates run from 15% to 50%. At 50%: American milk, perfume, video game consoles, golf clubs, fishing rods, steel, aluminum, jackets and T-shirts. At 25%: cheese, carpets, stoves and air conditioners. At 15%: forklifts and industrial molds.
  • The measures answer the Trump administration's 50% duties on about $20 billion of Canadian goods, imposed after trade talks broke down in August.
  • Canada also announced a $7.5 billion support package for affected businesses and workers.
  • Context: the Supreme Court's 6–3 ruling in Learning Resources v. Trump (February) held that IEEPA does not authorize presidential tariffs, invalidating that category. Section 232 tariffs — the authority behind the steel and aluminum duties — survived and are what's in play here.

Left view

The Guardian and Vox treat this as the predictable end of a trade strategy built on leverage the courts partly took away: after IEEPA was struck down, the administration narrowed to Section 232, allies retaliated, and American consumers pay twice. The Tax Policy Center's analysis is cited to argue the relief from the Supreme Court ruling is smaller than advertised because the remaining tariffs still bite.

Right view

Reason ran the story straight and critically — libertarian outlets have been the most consistent conservative opponents of the tariffs throughout. The Tax Foundation, which leans free-market, welcomed the IEEPA ruling as a rebuke of executive overreach and estimates the invalidated tariffs would have shrunk long-run GDP by 0.3%. Trade-hawk voices at the Washington Examiner and OANN frame Canadian retaliation as proof Ottawa was never bargaining in good faith and argue holding firm is the only way to reset terms.

Watch for

PwC and Norton Rose Fulbright estimate up to $175 billion in potential IEEPA refunds working through the system — a real fiscal item. Watch whether the administration reaches for other trade authorities to rebuild what the Court took down, whether Canadian retaliation broadens to energy or procurement, and how the 50% duties land on Midwest agriculture and appliance manufacturing heading into November.

Twelve countries move to ban Israeli settlement goods; Israel closes the UK consulate in East Jerusalem

Facts

  • The UK, France and Canada announced Tuesday they will ban imports of goods produced in Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank and Jewish neighborhoods of East Jerusalem. Goods from Israel proper are not affected.
  • Nine more countries — Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Ireland, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Spain and Sweden — said they will introduce national restrictions, back EU-level measures, or actively consider them. Three of the twelve are G7 members.
  • The joint foreign ministers' statement cited unprecedented settler violence and continued settlement expansion as undermining a two-state solution.
  • Israel condemned the move and announced retaliation, including closing the British consulate in East Jerusalem. Foreign Minister Gideon Sa'ar announced the countermeasures; President Isaac Herzog also objected, as did US Ambassador Mike Huckabee.
  • France is separately pushing for an EU-wide ban.

Left view

NPR led with the UK's language accusing Israeli settlers of ethnic cleansing in the West Bank. The Guardian and Chatham House frame the bans as long overdue — the point being that decades of statements condemning settlements as illegal under international law finally carry an economic cost. Supporters emphasize the narrow scope: this targets settlements, not Israel.

Right view

The Free Press, National Review and the NY Post treat the distinction between “settlements” and “Israel” as thinner than advertised, and read the timing — with Israel's principal ally at war with Iran — as European governments responding to domestic political pressure rather than events on the ground. Israeli right-wing politicians and Huckabee argue the bans reward intransigence and remove any incentive for Palestinian negotiation. The Jewish Telegraphic Agency's coverage documents the reaction across the Israeli political spectrum, where opposition was broad.

Watch for

Whether the EU adopts a bloc-wide measure, which would change the scale materially — settlement exports to the UK alone are small, in the low tens of millions annually, so the near-term economic bite is symbolic. Watch for further Israeli countermeasures beyond the consulate closure, whether Washington pressures allies to reverse, and whether more countries join the twelve.

NTSB interviews crew of Amazon cargo jet that overran a Miami runway, killing five

Facts

  • Prime Air Flight 7598, a Boeing 767-300 from San Juan, overran the runway at Miami International around 2 p.m. Sunday, tore through a perimeter fence and struck vehicles on NW 67th Avenue. Five people died, five were injured.
  • All the victims were in vehicles on the road, not aboard the aircraft, the NTSB said.
  • The aircraft came to rest roughly 1,300 feet past the paved runway. Video appears to show it remaining airborne well down the runway before touchdown.
  • Investigators are examining touchdown point, speed, braking, weather and wind, and the aircraft's mechanical condition. Crew interviews began this week, along with interviews of controllers and dispatchers.
  • Miami International does not have engineered materials arrestor beds — crushable pads designed to stop overrunning aircraft.

Left view

NPR and coverage in left-leaning outlets center the arrestor-bed gap and the broader question of aviation infrastructure funding, alongside scrutiny of contracted air-cargo operations and crew scheduling pressure at Amazon's air network. The framing: a known, engineerable risk that money would have mitigated.

Right view

Right-leaning coverage at the NY Post and Fox has stayed largely on the reporting rather than the policy, with commentary cautioning against drawing regulatory conclusions before the NTSB establishes cause — noting that early video suggesting a long landing points to flight-crew factors rather than infrastructure. Some conservative aviation commentators fold this into ongoing criticism of FAA staffing and air-traffic modernization delays.

Watch for

The NTSB preliminary report, typically within 30 days, which should establish touchdown point and whether the aircraft was configured and braking normally. If a long landing is confirmed, expect renewed pressure on the FAA over runway safety areas at airports where geography makes standard overrun zones impossible — Miami among them. Amazon Air's operational model and its contract carriers are likely to draw congressional attention regardless of cause.
