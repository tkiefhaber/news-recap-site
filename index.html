The Big Stories Today

US and Iran trade strikes again; Trump threatens a harder blow

Facts

  • Two oil tankers were hit by projectiles off the coast of Oman — one owned by Saudi Aramco, one a Liberian-flagged supertanker — reigniting the Hormuz fight after roughly a month of relative quiet.
  • CENTCOM said US forces struck two IRGC rocket launchers on Larak Island around noon ET Tuesday, describing the targets as launchers preparing to fire rockets carrying sea mines into the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Hours later the IRGC launched a missile-and-drone operation it called “Punishment of the Aggressor” against the King Hussein and Al Azraq bases in Jordan. Jordan said it intercepted eight missiles; no injuries were reported at either base. Kuwait and Bahrain also reported drone activity.
  • Trump said Iran would be hit “much harder” if it responds again, and claimed the US has near-total control of the strait. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian said Tehran would return to the June Islamabad Memorandum if Washington does the same.
  • Shipping through Hormuz has been largely blocked since late February, when the US and Israel opened an air campaign against Iran. Brent pushed above $95 a barrel on the news.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian frame this as the predictable arithmetic of an open-ended war — each “limited, precise” strike buys days, not an endgame. Thomas Wright, writing in The Atlantic, argues the off-ramps are closing and that a war of choice is hardening into a war of necessity. Democratic leaders, echoed by MSNBC, return to the process objection they have made since February: the administration keeps expanding a conflict Congress never authorized, and the Jordan strikes show how fast US troops in third countries become targets.

Right view

Fox News and the New York Post treat the Larak strike as textbook deterrence: Iran was staging sea mines for a civilian waterway, and the US removed the launchers before they fired. National Review and The Free Press argue the freedom of the seas is a core US interest that predates Trump, and that Tehran's failure to inflict casualties in Jordan is evidence the pressure campaign is working. Reason and parts of the MAGA coalition dissent sharply, warning that a seven-month air war with no defined objective is exactly the entanglement Trump campaigned against — the split Rand Paul and Thomas Massie have pressed all year.

Watch for

Energy desks are the tell. Goldman Sachs has said Brent averages north of $100 through 2026 if Hormuz stays mostly shut another month, and $115–120 in the back half if the disruption drags. Analysts at StoneX note futures markets have swung from pricing two Fed cuts this year to pricing one — and in some scenarios a hike. Morgan Stanley is the counterweight, arguing limited pass-through to core inflation lets the Fed look through an energy shock. Also watch whether the Massie–Khanna war powers resolution gets a floor vote now that Congress has cleared funding, and whether the tanker attacks push marine insurance rates high enough to keep ships out regardless of naval escorts.

Congress punts the funding fight past the midterms

Facts

  • The House passed a continuing resolution 370–48 on Tuesday, extending current funding levels through December 11. It had already cleared the Senate and now goes to Trump's desk.
  • Opposition was bipartisan and small: 19 Republicans and 29 Democrats voted no.
  • The timing is unusual — Congress rarely finishes a CR nearly a month before the September 30 deadline. It removes an October 1 shutdown from the pre-election calendar entirely.
  • The bill temporarily blocks an OMB rule that would route federal grant funding through political appointees, and bars the administration from transferring money out of other programs into Border Patrol.
  • The House has passed three of twelve FY2027 appropriations bills; the Senate has passed none.

Left view

The Washington Post and NOTUS report that Democratic negotiators traded an early, clean CR for the two guardrails — the OMB grant-review block and the Border Patrol transfer ban — treating them as concrete limits on impoundment-style maneuvers that a shutdown fight would not have produced. Vox and Mother Jones voices on the left flank are less satisfied, arguing leadership gave up its only real leverage of the cycle for provisions that expire in December, and that a party running against executive overreach just funded it for three more months.

Right view

House Appropriations Republicans cast the vote as restoring regular order: fund the government on time, then do the twelve bills properly. The Wall Street Journal editorial board and The Dispatch note the political logic is unmistakable — no shutdown means no October chaos to blame on the majority, and both parties preferred that. Spending hawks at Reason and in the Freedom Caucus register the standard complaint that another flat-line extension locks in a baseline nobody voted for, which is roughly where the 19 GOP no votes came from.

Watch for

December 11 lands in a lame-duck session whose leverage depends entirely on the November 3 result, so expect both sides to slow-walk FY2027 bills until they know the map. Watch whether the Senate moves any appropriations bills in September, and whether the Iran supplemental — the Pentagon has signaled it wants roughly $200 billion more for the war — gets attached to the December package rather than voted on its own, which would put war funding and shutdown risk in the same bill.

Judge sends Tyler Robinson to trial in Charlie Kirk's killing; death penalty on the table

Facts

  • Utah District Judge Tony Graf ruled Tuesday there is probable cause for Robinson to stand trial on all counts: aggravated murder, felony discharge of a firearm causing serious bodily injury, obstruction of justice, witness tampering, and a violent offense committed in the presence of a child.
  • Robinson entered a not guilty plea. The ruling clears prosecutors to pursue the death penalty.
  • Prosecutors told the court they have what they described as a mountain of evidence that Robinson shot Kirk at Utah Valley University in September 2025 in front of thousands of students.
  • This was a preliminary hearing, not the trial. Opening statements have not been scheduled.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian keep the emphasis on the legal mechanics — a probable-cause finding is a low bar, not a verdict — and on capital punishment itself, noting Utah is one of the few states that still authorizes firing squad and that death-penalty prosecutions routinely run a decade or more. The Atlantic's coverage has focused on the year since the assassination and the argument that political violence has become bipartisan in its targets, resisting the framing that any one movement owns it.

Right view

Fox News, the New York Post and National Review center Kirk's family and the anniversary, and treat the death-penalty ruling as the baseline answer to a political assassination carried out in front of a crowd of students. Commentary on the right also revisits the campus-speech context — that Kirk was killed while speaking — and argues the case should be tried as an attack on public debate, not only as a homicide.

Watch for

Two procedural fights ahead: a likely defense motion to strike the death notice, and a venue challenge given saturation coverage in Utah County. Capital cases in Utah typically take years to reach a jury, so a 2027 trial date would be fast. The trial's calendar will also collide with the midterm campaign, which both parties have reason to be wary of.

Tropical Storm Edouard floods Texas and Louisiana after Gulf landfall

Facts

  • Edouard came ashore near Johnson Bayou, Louisiana, at about 2:20 p.m. CDT Tuesday with 60 mph sustained winds, right at the Texas–Louisiana border.
  • A 90 mph gust was recorded at Port Arthur. Houston is in line for 3–6 inches of rain with locally up to 9 inches.
  • Flash flooding is the main threat, extending into the lower Mississippi Valley and southern Plains; isolated tornadoes are possible on the northwest side of the circulation.
  • The system has weakened to a tropical depression and is forecast to dissipate by Wednesday night.

Left view

CNN's coverage placed the storm in a climate frame — warmer Gulf water feeding rapid intensification in the final hours before landfall, and rainfall totals that outrun drainage systems built for a different century. The Guardian and Mother Jones extend that to FEMA capacity and to who absorbs repeat flooding in Harris and Jefferson counties, where the same neighborhoods have flooded in successive storms.

Right view

Fox Weather and local outlets keep it operational: evacuation compliance, power restoration timelines, refinery and petrochemical status along the Sabine–Neches corridor. The Wall Street Journal editorial page's recurring argument on storms like this is that flood-insurance subsidies and permissive floodplain development, not emissions policy, are what determine the damage bill.

Watch for

Gulf Coast refining is the market link — the Port Arthur and Beaumont complexes sit almost exactly where the storm came ashore, and any extended outage there compounds an already tight products market with Hormuz disrupted. Analysts have noted geopolitical disruptions already have close to 10% of global refinery capacity offline. Retail gasoline is the transmission channel to watch over the next week.

IAEA confirms Assad-era Syria hid a nuclear reactor program

Facts

  • A confidential IAEA report circulated to member states concludes Syria under Bashar al-Assad failed to declare nuclear material, facilities and activities tied to an unfinished reactor at Deir ez-Zor.
  • Inspectors verified roughly 73 metric tons of natural uranium metal, much of it in fuel rods, at a previously undeclared location. It is now under IAEA monitoring and containment.
  • The al-Kibar reactor, destroyed by an Israeli airstrike in 2007, was linked to a fuel-fabrication plant and to sites used for storage and planned reprocessing. It was widely believed to have been built with North Korean help.
  • The findings follow new inspector access to sites and records after Assad's ouster in 2024; Syria's current government opened the program to UN inspection last month and agreed in August to hand over the material.

Left view

The Guardian and NPR read this as vindication of the inspections regime rather than of the 2007 strike — the definitive accounting came from access and records, not from bombs, and it took the collapse of the regime to get it. Al Jazeera's framing highlights how long the file sat unresolved and what that says about the IAEA's leverage when a state simply refuses cooperation.

Right view

The Washington Times, the Foundation for Defense of Democracies and the Wall Street Journal editorial board draw the opposite lesson: Israel's 2007 strike was the decisive act, the paperwork merely confirms it, and the episode is a live argument for preventive action against Iran's program today. National Review adds the nonproliferation angle — that North Korean export activity went undetected for years is the part that should worry people most.

Watch for

The Board of Governors meets in September; watch whether Syria's cooperation is enough to close the file or whether a formal noncompliance finding is pursued against the former government. The practical question is logistics — where 73 tons of uranium metal actually goes, and who pays to move it. Expect the report to be cited immediately in the Iran debate by both camps, to opposite ends.

Nepal–Tibet flood toll passes 1,000 with thousands still missing

Facts

  • Nepal's National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority put the death toll at 1,003, with more than 4,400 people still missing.
  • More than 11,000 people have been rescued across the affected region; 279 workers were pulled from hydropower project sites. Rescue teams from India and China are working alongside Nepali crews.
  • The disaster was triggered by a glacier collapse and landslide on the Nepal–China border. It is Nepal's deadliest natural disaster since the 2015 earthquake.
  • About 10% of Nepal's electricity generation capacity is offline, forcing power imports from India. Road access to Nuwakot, one of the worst-hit districts, has been restored.

Left view

The Guardian, NPR and Al Jazeera foreground glacial retreat in the Himalaya and the specific risk category — glacial lake outburst floods — that scientists have flagged for years, along with the argument that Nepal contributes almost nothing to global emissions while absorbing this kind of loss. Coverage also questions the siting of run-of-river hydropower in valleys with known outburst risk.

Right view

Right-leaning coverage of the disaster is thin; this is largely a wire and center-left story so far. Where it appears, the emphasis is on relief logistics, on China's and India's competing aid footprints in Kathmandu as a strategic contest, and on the standard skepticism about attributing any single event to climate change. Worth flagging: mainstream US coverage overall has been light relative to the toll.

Watch for

The missing-persons number is the one to track — a gap of 4,400 between confirmed dead and unaccounted for usually narrows downward slowly and painfully. UN agencies have already escalated their appeal. Watch Nepal's electricity import bill and whether the monsoon's tail brings further landslides onto newly destabilized slopes, which is the standard second wave in disasters of this shape.
