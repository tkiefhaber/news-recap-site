Facts

He left after reports of affairs, including one with an executive whose Singapore firm is under investigation over chips allegedly diverted to China. Things to watch: whether the Fed still hikes in December, how deep the SEC's Nvidia probe goes, and whether Asian markets, where the Nikkei and Kospi each fell more than 5%, keep falling on Monday. Left view

Al Jazeera's analysts call the blockade one of the most dangerous escalation scenarios, because it could link Hormuz and the Red Sea into one front. Coverage on the left focuses on the unproven chemical-weapons claim, which Iran compared to the 2003 Iraq WMD case, and on Vance's admission that the war cost Republicans the Senate.

Right view

Hawks and blockade supporters say it's working. Kpler data (via Reuters) shows Iran loaded no crude in October, the second straight month. TankerTrackers dismissed the convoy talk as "wishful thinking." The administration's position is that only a "real deal" ends the pressure, and Trump said the war raised fuel prices "far less than I thought."

Watch for

King's College London's Andreas Krieg expects Iran to try to make enforcement too costly, politically and economically, since it can't break the blockade militarily. The main risks are a convoy confrontation, Houthi attacks around Hodeidah (where Saudi Arabia warned civilians to leave), and higher Gulf shipping insurance, all of which could push oil prices back up.

The Senate passes a bipartisan permitting overhaul 62-33 as the risk of a Dec. 11 shutdown grows

Facts

  • Late Thursday, the Senate passed the bipartisan permitting bill 62-33. It would shorten environmental reviews and make it harder to sue over energy and infrastructure projects. It now goes to the House (CBS, Axios). It had cleared a procedural vote 73-25 on Nov. 9 (States Newsroom).
  • Trump claims it will put "at least $1,300 a year" back in Americans' pockets. Sen. Whitehouse (D-R.I.), a co-sponsor, calls it a climate and clean-energy bill (CBS, Just the News).
  • Government funding runs out Dec. 11, and there's no deal yet. Some Democrats, including Sen. Van Hollen, want to use the deadline to force an end to the Iran war, while others, including Sen. Booker, want to avoid a shutdown. GOP Sen. Mike Rounds warns that Trump's push for a $350 billion defense package in the lame-duck session would "absolutely" raise the risk of a shutdown (Latin Times, citing The Hill and WaPo).

Left view

Common Dreams and environmental groups call the bill a "massive giveaway" to fossil fuels and tech, citing its NEPA exemptions and limits on court challenges. They want it delayed until the new Congress. Sens. Merkley and Cortez Masto objected to it, Cortez Masto specifically over its tribal provisions.

Right view

A shutdown would be the fourth funding lapse since October 2025. The coverage on the funding fight comes mostly from aggregated reporting, and detailed mainstream coverage is still thin.

The world court rules 9-6 that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, the first time it has found a state responsible for genocide

Facts

  • In a case brought by The Gambia, the International Court of Justice found that Myanmar's security forces carried out genocidal acts with intent to destroy the Rohingya. It rejected Myanmar's counterinsurgency defense. The court put the death toll at about 10,000, and more than 700,000 Rohingya fled to Bangladesh (NPR, HRW, RFA).
  • Myanmar must investigate and prosecute those responsible, guarantee it won't happen again, and enable the Rohingya to return safely, or pay compensation. The amount will be set later. The ruling can't be appealed, but the court has no means to enforce it (HRW, RFA).
  • The U.K. says it will expel Myanmar's defense attaché. The U.S. State Department said it is "studying" the decision and warned against politicizing the court (RFA, NPR).

Left view

Human Rights Watch called it long-awaited recognition and a precedent for genocide victims elsewhere. NPR, citing Legal Action Worldwide, noted the ruling could shape how the court weighs intent in South Africa's case against Israel. UN Secretary-General Guterres called it a powerful recognition of the victims.

Right view

The WSJ editorial board argues the court has "defined genocide down" in a way that could later be used against the U.S. and Israel. U.N. Ambassador Mike Waltz had warned before the ruling that the ICJ must not lower the legal bar for proving genocide. JNS focused on what it means for the Israel case, where no merits ruling is expected for years.

Watch for

Richard Horsey of the International Crisis Group told RFA that governments dealing with Myanmar will find it harder to treat this as business as usual, but no enforcement mechanism exists. The Security Council has never acted on an ICJ non-compliance referral (HRW). The ruling's reasoning on intent will be closely read in the South Africa v. Israel case.
