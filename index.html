Supreme Court clears part of Trump's mail-voting order ahead of the midterms

Facts

  • In an unsigned order Monday, the Supreme Court divided 6–3 and lifted a June injunction from a Massachusetts federal judge that had blocked key parts of the president's elections executive order in California and 22 other states that sued.
  • The majority's stated basis was standing — the order was characterized as an internal directive to federal agencies that did not itself injure the states, meaning they sued too early rather than that the order is lawful.
  • What is now unblocked: DHS compiling a federal citizenship list to share with states, the Attorney General prioritizing prosecutions of officials who issue ballots to ineligible voters, and USPS rulemaking on mail-ballot design and tracking. The Postal Service's final rule says it will decline to deliver mail ballots in states that don't hand over lists of voters receiving them.
  • The Court did not reach the most contested piece — requiring USPS to verify each recipient's eligibility and withhold ballots from anyone missing from a government list.
  • The three liberal justices dissented; Justice Jackson wrote that the ruling injects avoidable chaos and uncertainty into the coming midterms.
  • A separate track of litigation continues before District Judge Indira Talwani, where voting-rights groups filed an emergency motion over the weekend arguing USPS defied an earlier injunction by publishing its final rule.

Left view

NPR and CBS emphasize that the win is procedural and narrow — the Court said the states sued too early, not that the order survives review — and that real hurdles remain in the parallel voting-rights cases. MSNBC's legal desk (ms.now) frames it as a "for now" ruling whose durability is untested. The Guardian-aligned and Democratic-state position, quoted across coverage, is that federal control over ballot distribution is an extraordinary intrusion on state-run elections; Sen. Jack Reed argues the practical effect falls hardest on servicemembers voting from abroad. Votebeat stresses the operational risk: a national citizenship list goes stale quickly, and election officials in both parties warned of confusion and disenfranchisement if rules shift months before ballots go out.

Right view

Fox News leads on it as a straightforward win for the administration's election-integrity agenda and reads the 6–3 line as the Court declining to let blue-state attorneys general pre-emptively block federal executive action. The broader argument on the right, echoed in Fox opinion, is that verifying eligibility before mailing a ballot is basic administrative hygiene, and that states resisting a shared citizenship list are objecting to the audit rather than the fraud. Worth noting for balance: the documented incidence of noncitizen voting found by states is real but far smaller than the scale the president has claimed.

Watch for

Judge Talwani's ruling on the emergency motion is the near-term hinge — if she finds USPS violated the earlier injunction, parts of the rule could be frozen again within weeks. Election administrators are the practical bottleneck: ballots for November start going out in some states in late September, so any rule that isn't settled by roughly mid-September becomes very hard to implement cleanly. Expect a second Supreme Court application this fall on the eligibility-verification piece the Court sidestepped. Separately, SCOTUSblog notes the Court is also weighing late-arriving mail-ballot deadlines in a case that could reshape counting rules in several states.

Iran answers "Operation Economic Outcast" — and oil falls anyway

Facts

  • Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent rolled out the sanctions package Monday under the name Operation Economic Outcast, designating roughly 60 individuals and entities based in Hong Kong, mainland China, Malaysia, the UAE, Singapore and other third countries for facilitating Iranian sanctions evasion.
  • The action widens the categories exposed to secondary sanctions — shipping, oil, gold, aviation, technology and digital assets — and sets country-by-country deadlines to sever ties. Bessent's line is that facilitators get cut off from the dollar system entirely.
  • Treasury says it has mapped Iran's oil-smuggling and evasion network and will present that mapping to governments still transacting with Tehran. Trump has been calling foreign leaders directly with specific requests.
  • Tehran's response Tuesday: it says it has a plan, calls the measures a violation of other states' sovereignty, and its president said Washington must change its approach and honor its own commitments under international law.
  • An oil tanker was attacked in the Strait of Hormuz off Oman on Tuesday. The June memorandum of understanding meant to end the war has not stopped periodic Iranian strikes on shipping.
  • Traffic is rerouting rather than stopping — more than 80% of liquids transiting in the past two weeks used the Omani corridor, and the US Navy has escorted over 15 million barrels of crude and products out of the strait in recent days.

Left view

NPR's coverage of the rollout foregrounds how much of this rests on third-country compliance the US cannot compel, and the humanitarian cost inside Iran of an economy already in crisis. Al Jazeera amplifies the sovereignty objection — that extraterritorial reach punishes Singaporean or Emirati firms for conduct legal where they operate. The Atlantic's Tom Nichols, appearing on C-SPAN Tuesday, situates the sanctions in a war now six months old with no settlement, and the persistent left critique is that maximum economic pressure has a long track record of hardening rather than toppling the regime.

Right view

The Washington Post editorial page — center-right on this file — argues in a Monday column that "zero leakage" is the only version of this that works, and that the campaign will look hollow unless the administration is willing to impose secondary sanctions on China specifically. Fox News highlights Iranian state media's human-rights framing as regime propaganda. The broader right-of-center case is that economic containment is the defensible alternative to further bombing after strikes and talks both stalled — Reason-style critics on the libertarian right dissent, calling dollar-system weaponization a tool that accelerates de-dollarization.

Watch for

Markets read the announcement as less severe than trailed: Brent fell 3.4% Tuesday to about $89.05 and WTI 3.6% to roughly $82. Morgan Stanley raised its Brent forecast and sees a peak near $100 in Q4; Commonwealth Bank of Australia expects a $70–$100 range through year-end and flags the tail risk that effective sanctions push Iran toward kinetic escalation in the strait. Fortune reports analysts see the war potentially running deep into 2027 precisely because Hormuz isn't fully closed — enough oil leaks out to make stalemate sustainable. The China question is the real test: if Beijing keeps buying and Washington doesn't designate a major Chinese bank, the deadlines lose force.

Canada suspends trade talks as 50% US tariffs bite; retaliation set for Sept. 8

Facts

  • US tariffs of 50% on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods took effect at midnight between Aug. 21 and 22, covering hockey sticks, building materials, liquors and certain apparel categories.
  • Talks collapsed late Friday, Aug. 21. Prime Minister Mark Carney issued a formal statement suspending negotiations and blamed what he called uneconomic and unfair US demands.
  • Carney has committed to matching the tariffs dollar for dollar beginning Sept. 8, and separately vowed to counter US auto tariffs.
  • At a press conference Saturday, Carney said Canada is at war in the sense that it was attacked — unusually sharp language from a sitting Canadian PM about the United States.
  • Earlier in the cycle Canada had removed retaliatory tariffs on CUSMA-compliant US goods; the new round reverses that de-escalation.

Left view

NPR frames Carney's "at war" remark as the moment a technocratic former central banker concluded that accommodation had failed, and centers Canadian workers and cross-border supply chains. The Guardian-side critique is that tariffs on building materials land directly on US housing costs at a moment when affordability is already the dominant domestic complaint, making this a self-inflicted consumer-price story.

Right view

The administration's case, carried on Fox News and in NY Post coverage, is that Canada extracted favorable terms for decades on dairy, lumber and digital services and that leverage is the only thing that has ever moved Ottawa. The Wall Street Journal editorial board has been consistently hostile to this approach, arguing that tariffs on a treaty ally are a tax on Americans and that CUSMA was supposed to have settled these questions — a live split between free-trade and economic-nationalist wings of the right.

Watch for

Sept. 8 is the date to circle. Auto and parts flows are the escalation risk with real GDP consequences on both sides given integrated assembly lines. Watch the Canadian dollar and lumber futures, and whether CUSMA's dispute-settlement machinery gets invoked — the treaty is up for joint review in 2026, so a failed round here poisons that process. NBC notes Carney called the new tariffs a miscalculation, language that leaves a door open if Washington moves first.

DRC's Ebola epidemic becomes the deadliest in the country's history

Facts

  • As of data through Aug. 22, the Democratic Republic of the Congo has recorded 5,514 confirmed cases and 2,642 deaths, surpassing the 2,299 deaths of the 2018–2020 outbreak and the 3,317 cases recorded then.
  • The pathogen is Bundibugyo virus, an Ebola species for which no approved vaccine or specific treatment exists — the licensed vaccines protect against Zaire ebolavirus, not this one.
  • UN Ebola coordinator Julien Harneis said Aug. 21 that transmission is accelerating faster than the response can contain it. Weekly deaths hit a record high this past week.
  • Cases have also been reported in neighboring Uganda, per ECDC monitoring.

Left view

The Guardian and NPR's global health desks tie the trajectory directly to the contraction of US and European global health funding over the past two years — fewer contact tracers and labs mean detection lags, and detection lag is the whole ballgame with a filovirus. The Washington Post's reporting emphasizes that a species-specific vaccine gap is a market failure that only public funding was ever going to close.

Right view

Right-leaning coverage of this outbreak has been thin, which is itself worth noting. Where it appears — National Review, the Washington Examiner — the emphasis is on WHO's institutional performance and whether the agency has earned renewed US funding, and on securing borders and travel screening rather than expanding multilateral commitments. The Free Press has covered the vaccine-development gap as a case for reforming, not defunding, biomedical R&D pipelines.

Watch for

Two thresholds matter: sustained transmission in a major urban center such as Kinshasa, and confirmed spread beyond Uganda. Either would move this from a regional emergency to a global one. Watch for a WHO emergency committee reconvening, whether any Bundibugyo-specific candidate vaccine gets an emergency-use pathway, and whether donor governments announce supplemental funding. Airline and travel-sector exposure is currently negligible but is the first market channel if the outbreak reaches a capital city.

The midterm money gap: Republicans open a roughly half-billion-dollar edge

Facts

  • New filings show the DNC trailing the RNC by more than $100 million in cash on hand, with the party committee carrying millions in debt.
  • The DNC entered July with about $16 million on hand against roughly $18 million in debt — more owed than banked. The RNC reported about $129 million on hand and no debt.
  • Much of the Democratic debt traces to a $15 million loan taken after the 2024 cycle.
  • Counting allied outside groups, Republicans hold something close to a half-billion-dollar advantage with under 100 days to the November election.

Left view

MSNBC (ms.now) reports the gap as real but argues committee cash is a weak predictor — candidate-level fundraising, small-dollar surges and volunteer energy have repeatedly outrun party balance sheets in recent midterms, and the out-party historically enjoys a turnout advantage regardless of committee reserves. Democratic officials quoted describe the lean committee posture as a deliberate reallocation toward state parties.

Right view

The Washington Examiner frames it as a donor-confidence problem rather than a strategy, pointing to spending on non-voting territories while servicing debt. Fox News highlights that the DNC has no transfers planned into the stretch run. RedState and similar outlets read it as base demoralization. The counter-consideration: money advantages have failed to translate into seats in several recent cycles, and both parties' committees are only one slice of total spending.

Watch for

Ad-reservation data in September is the tell — watch which House districts Democratic committees quietly stop reserving in, since that is where the cash constraint becomes a strategic retreat. Also watch whether major Democratic donors route around the DNC to super PACs, which would confirm the donor-confidence read while leaving total Democratic spending healthier than committee filings suggest.
