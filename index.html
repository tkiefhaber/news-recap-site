The Big Stories Today

Houthis seize Perim Island, completing their grip on the Bab al-Mandab

Facts

  • Houthi forces took Perim Island in the middle of the Bab al-Mandab Strait on Friday, a day after capturing the Red Sea port city of Mocha. Pro-government Yemeni forces withdrew rather than fight.
  • The group now holds effectively the entire Yemeni Red Sea coastline overlooking a strait through which roughly 12% of global trade passes.
  • Brent jumped more than 7% intraday on the news before settling near $104; WTI ended around $100 after touching $104. Both had topped $100 earlier in the week.
  • The IEA said Saudi crude output fell 2.3 million barrels a day in August to about 6 million — the lowest in more than three decades. Saudi flows through the strait have dropped from roughly 3 million bpd to about 400,000.
  • Strikes also shut a key Saudi pipeline. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has asked President Trump directly for US military action against the Houthis; the White House has so far offered intelligence and targeting help rather than strikes, keeping its focus on Iran and Hormuz.

Left view

NPR and NBC News frame this as the predictable cost of a war with no defined endpoint: seven months in, Iran's proxies have opened a second chokepoint rather than being deterred, and the administration is now fielding allied requests for a third front it has no plan to fight. The Guardian's line of criticism — a Middle East conflict entered without an exit strategy, at roughly a billion dollars a day — is being applied directly here.

Right view

Fox News and Al Majalla argue the opposite lesson: that a Houthi takeover of the strait "cannot be tolerated," and that hesitancy is what invited it. The complaint on the right is about tempo — that Riyadh reportedly did not get a green light for a large-scale air campaign in time to save Mocha, and that half-measures at Bab al-Mandab are what let Iran gain maritime leverage on both sides of the Arabian Peninsula.

Watch for

Analysts at the International Crisis Group and The Hill flag mines, short-range weapons and boarding operations from Perim as the near-term threat — the shift is from harassment to territorial control, which is much harder to reverse. Energy desks see the risk skewed upward: EIA's official second-half forecast of about $90 Brent now looks stale, and technical analysts are penciling ranges well above current levels if traffic stops entirely. Watch war-risk insurance rates, whether Saudi Arabia acts unilaterally, and the Oman meeting Iran says it will host with Gulf states on Hormuz shipping.

Inflation runs at 3.4% as a divided Fed heads into Wednesday's decision

Facts

  • August CPI rose 0.4% on the month, putting the annual rate at 3.4%. Core rose 0.3%, one-tenth above forecast, with the core annual rate at 2.4%.
  • The fed funds rate has sat at 3.50%–3.75% for all of 2026. The FOMC held in July on a 9–3 vote, with the three dissents favoring a hike.
  • Traders raised the odds of a quarter-point hike at the September 16 meeting after the report; CME FedWatch pricing has run in the mid-50s to mid-60s percent range. Nationwide now expects a hike.
  • Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said at Jackson Hole on August 28 that the central bank still has work to do on inflation and signaled readiness to act. Governor Christopher Waller had said he could accept a hold if CPI showed cooling.
  • Markets read the print as roughly in line and rallied: the S&P 500 snapped a four-session slide on Friday.

Left view

MSNBC's MaddowBlog and CNN tie the inflation problem back to the war rather than to Fed policy: energy is doing the work, households are absorbing it, and the president has said relief won't come until after the midterms. The argument is that a rate hike punishes workers for a supply shock the administration created, and that the Fed shouldn't be the only institution asked to clean it up.

Right view

The Wall Street Journal editorial page and Kiplinger's read of the data emphasize credibility: after leaving rates unchanged in July while inflation drifted above target, the Fed risks looking unwilling to finish the job, and investor doubt on that score is itself inflationary. Warsh's Jackson Hole framing — that there is still work to do — is treated on the right as overdue candor rather than hawkishness.

Watch for

Wednesday's vote and the dot plot are the whole story. A hike into a supply shock would be unusual and would sharpen the stagflation debate; a hold would likely be read as the Fed blinking. Either way, watch the dissent count — three dissents in July was already unusual, and a similarly split vote would tell you the committee has no consensus about what an energy-driven price spike requires. Mortgage rates and the 2-year yield will move first.

Trump's $5,000 "dividend" splits his own party as the GOP convention closes

Facts

  • Republicans wrapped their first-ever midterm convention in Dallas on Thursday night. JD Vance spoke, followed by Trump, who urged Americans to vote Republican.
  • Trump pledged a $5,000 "Trump dividend" to every adult citizen — conditional on Republicans holding both the House and Senate in November.
  • The cost would run past $1 trillion. Congress would have to appropriate it, and the GOP's House majority is razor-thin.
  • Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis said the payments would add to debt and inflation. Fox's Laura Ingraham pressed Trump on whether it amounts to a bribe. Rep. Robert Garcia (D-Calif.) said flatly it isn't happening.
  • Early convention nights drew rows of empty seats at the American Airlines Center, with last-minute efforts to fill them.

Left view

NPR and The New Republic cover the pledge as an admission that the affordability argument is landing: with a UMass Amherst poll showing Democrats ahead in both chambers and Republican unpopularity rooted in the cost of food, fuel and health care, the response is a one-time check rather than a policy. The conditional framing — money only if you vote a certain way — is the part Democrats are amplifying.

Right view

This is where the interesting fight is, because the sharpest criticism is conservative. National Review's Charles C.W. Cooke called the idea catastrophic morally, practically and politically. The Washington Examiner editorial board ran "Against Trump's $5,000 bribe," arguing it would widen deficits, raise inflation and worsen the affordability squeeze it's meant to fix. Former Sen. Pat Toomey was blunter still, and some on the right are calling it a first step toward universal basic income.

Watch for

Whether any appropriations vehicle materializes — nobody expects one before November. The more consequential question is the fiscal-hawk split the pledge exposed: the same week Treasury reported a $1.97 trillion deficit, the party's own editorial pages broke with its leader over a trillion-dollar giveaway. Watch whether House fiscal hawks put that objection on the record, and whether the dividend shows up in GOP candidates' own ads or quietly doesn't.

Federal deficit hits $1.97 trillion with one month left in the fiscal year

Facts

  • Treasury reported Friday that the deficit for the first 11 months of fiscal 2026 reached $1.97 trillion — already above the full-year fiscal 2025 gap of $1.775 trillion.
  • August's monthly shortfall was $166.8 billion, flattered by calendar timing: because August 1 fell on a Saturday, Medicare and Social Security payments went out in July instead.
  • Adjusting for those calendar shifts, the 11-month deficit is down about 5% from the same stretch of 2025.
  • Spending ran $6.81 trillion and revenue $4.85 trillion, each up about 3% on an adjusted basis. Interest costs hit a record — roughly $1.4 trillion.
  • September is often a surplus month because of corporate tax deadlines, so the final figure should land below the current running total's trajectory.

Left view

Center-left coverage at Bloomberg and Reuters focuses on composition rather than the headline: revenue growth is keeping pace with spending growth, so the gap is driven substantially by interest on debt accumulated under tax cuts, plus war costs. The implied argument is that the deficit is a revenue choice, not evidence that social spending is out of control.

Right view

The Epoch Times and the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget (nonpartisan but a favorite citation on the right) treat $1.4 trillion in annual interest as the alarm bell — debt service now rivals the largest line items in the budget, crowding out everything else and leaving the country exposed if bond markets demand higher yields. It's the same fiscal-hawk argument now being turned against the president's own dividend proposal.

Watch for

The final fiscal-year number in mid-October, and the 10-year Treasury yield in the meantime — rising yields make the interest line compound faster. Bloomberg notes the debt burden is heading within a few years toward levels unseen in US history. Also watch how the deficit figure gets used in the September appropriations fight, where both parties have an incentive to cite it selectively.

EU frees €6.1 billion for Ukraine, clearing the way for Patriot interceptors

Facts

  • The European Commission formally approved a €6.1 billion (about $7.1 billion) tranche on Friday for Ukrainian defense procurement.
  • The money covers air and missile defense, ammunition, drones and electronic warfare, and comes out of the EU's €90 billion support loan.
  • Crucially, the approval includes exemptions letting Kyiv buy US-made PAC-3 interceptors — among the few munitions capable of stopping Russian ballistic missiles.
  • Defence and Space Commissioner Andrius Kubilius announced the decision in Brussels; member states had signed off earlier in the week.
  • Ukraine has put its 2026 defense needs at roughly $120 billion.

Left view

The Guardian and European outlets present this as Europe doing what it said it would — converting a headline loan figure into actual interceptors on a timeline that matters, and specifically working around procurement rules to buy American systems Kyiv needs rather than waiting for European production to scale.

Right view

Coverage on the right splits. The Wall Street Journal editorial line has generally welcomed European money going to American defense manufacturers as burden-sharing that finally works. Restraint-minded outlets such as Reason remain skeptical of open-ended European and American commitments, especially with US attention and munitions stockpiles already stretched by the Iran war.

Watch for

Delivery timelines, not announcements. PAC-3 production is the binding constraint, and the same interceptor inventory is in demand for Gulf air defense right now — the Iran war and the Ukraine war are competing for the same missiles. Watch Lockheed Martin's production guidance and whether Washington prioritizes allocation.

America marks 25 years since 9/11

Facts

  • Trump observed the 25th anniversary at the Pentagon on Friday rather than at ground zero, listening as the names of the 184 people killed there were read.
  • Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Dan Caine also spoke; a large American flag was unfurled on the building's west face.
  • Trump called the attacks a moment of immense evil and linked the anniversary to the present, saying the country is being tested again.
  • Vice President JD Vance attended the New York ceremony on the plaza where the Twin Towers stood, alongside all four living former presidents.
  • Ceremonies also took place in Shanksville, Pennsylvania, and at memorials across the country.

Left view

The Washington Post and CNN coverage notes the choice of venue — the Pentagon over ground zero — and, more substantively, questions the rhetorical link between the anniversary and the current war with Iran, a country not involved in the 2001 attacks. The broader retrospective theme on the left is the cost of the post-9/11 wars and how little of it was ever reckoned with.

Right view

Fox News and ABC's coverage lead on the commemoration itself and on continuity of purpose — the unity of the days after the attacks and the argument that the threat never went away. The connection Trump drew to Iran is treated on the right as the natural throughline rather than as a category error.

Watch for

Nothing operational, but the framing matters politically: a quarter-century mark arriving in the middle of another Middle East war gives both parties a template. Expect the "forever wars" argument to resurface in midterm messaging on the anti-interventionist right as much as on the left.
