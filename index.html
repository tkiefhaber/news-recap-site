The Big Stories Today

Trump promises every adult a $5,000 check if Republicans hold Congress

Facts

  • Speaking Wednesday night at the RNC's midterm convention in Dallas, Trump pledged to issue a $5,000 “dividend” to every adult US citizen if Republicans keep both the House and the Senate in November.
  • He offered no funding mechanism, comparing it to a corporate shareholder dividend and to the $1,776 bonus payments his administration sent military members last year, and suggested tariff revenue could cover part of it.
  • NBC's Garrett Haake put the arithmetic at roughly 269 million adults, or about $1.35 trillion — on top of an annual deficit already near $1.8 trillion. Any such payment would require an act of Congress.
  • The convention is an unusual format: a Trump-centered party gathering in a non-presidential year, which the RNC says raised nearly $55 million. Embattled Senate candidates from Texas and Georgia also spoke.

Left view

CNN and CBS News framed the pledge as the headline takeaway of a convention built around Trump personally, and emphasized the missing price tag and the congressional approval it would need. Left-leaning commentators went further and called it vote-buying outright — Raw Story catalogued critics describing it as a “bribe,” and the broader critique is that conditioning a cash payment on an election outcome is a transactional use of the Treasury, arriving just as tariffs have raised consumer prices.

Right view

Fox News led with the promise as a “first-of-its-kind” play to energize a base facing a difficult map, treating it as a bold closing argument on the economy rather than a policy proposal. Notably, the skepticism was not confined to the left: Fox's own Jacqui Heinrich pointed out on air that Trump has floated voter payments before — including a $2,000 tariff dividend last year — without delivering. Deficit-focused conservatives have been the loudest critics of the cost.

Watch for

Budget analysts expect this to stay rhetorical; a payment this size has no obvious path through a Congress already struggling on appropriations. The more testable effects are political: whether GOP candidates in tight races adopt the pledge or dodge it, and whether Senate Republicans worried about the deficit are forced to say publicly what they think. Markets largely ignored it — bond desks have been focused on oil and the Fed — but a credible $1.3 trillion transfer would be an inflation story, which is exactly the wrong backdrop three weeks after a hawkish Jackson Hole.

Brent tops $100 after US destroys five Iranian tankers; Trump says the war ends after the midterms

Facts

  • Brent crude closed above $100 a barrel Wednesday for the first time since May, trading near $101 Thursday. WTI for October was around $96.
  • Central Command said US forces destroyed five Iranian crude tankers Tuesday, in retaliation for two failed Iranian attempts to strike a US Navy warship.
  • Iran-aligned Houthi forces launched missiles and drones at four southern Saudi cities, sparking fires at oil facilities. A separate report said several US aircraft were damaged in Iran's strike on a US airbase in Jordan.
  • Trump said at the Dallas convention that the war will “end immediately” after the November 3 midterms, arguing Tehran is holding out to influence the result. The conflict, which began February 28, is in its seventh month.
  • Reporting indicates White House advisers have privately raised with Trump the possibility the war runs through the rest of his term.

Left view

NBC News and NPR have centered the cost to households — pump prices up roughly a dollar in a month — and the absence of a stated war aim or exit condition after seven months. The left-leaning read of Trump's “ends after the midterms” line is that it concedes the timing is political: if the war can be stopped on November 4, the question is why it is still running on September 10.

Right view

The right is split, and unusually openly. The Washington Examiner reported the midterm claim straight but has run op-eds arguing the campaign is strategic overreach the US cannot afford, one explicitly comparing it to Bush-era hubris. National Review has published a run of pieces — “The Iran War Is Eating Trump's Presidency,” “Trump Doesn't Have the Stomach for a Long War” — arguing public opposition is constraining him. The Daily Caller and Washington Times covered the tanker strikes as a demonstration of resolve. Hawks maintain that degrading Iran's export capacity is the point; restrainers counter that $100 oil is Tehran's best weapon and the US just helped fire it.

Watch for

Goldman Sachs has said Brent could top $120 next quarter and average above $100 next year if Strait of Hormuz flows stay severely restricted, against a base case nearer $85. JPMorgan is the outlier, holding a roughly $60 baseline on the view that long disruptions are unlikely — a wide analyst spread that itself signals how much depends on shipping. Sustained $100+ crude feeds directly into the Fed's September 16 decision and into pump prices heading into voting season. Watch tanker insurance rates and Hormuz transit volumes, which move before prices do.

Trump asks the Supreme Court a third time to let the USPS mail-ballot rule take effect

Facts

  • The Justice Department filed an emergency application Sunday asking the Supreme Court to let the Postal Service implement its new ballot-mail rule before the midterms — the third time the administration has taken the matter to the justices.
  • The rule, published in the Federal Register on August 26 and stemming from a March 31 executive order, requires states to upload each mail voter's name and address to a federal portal, tied to a unique barcode. Carriers would deliver ballots only to voters on the state-supplied list.
  • USPS says it will not determine eligibility, maintain voter rolls, or count ballots, and that no birthdates or Social Security numbers go into the portal.
  • US District Judge Indira Talwani blocked the rule, finding that implementing it fewer than 70 days before the election risks disenfranchising millions and that the record contained no evidence of mail-voting fraud justifying the speed.
  • North Carolina and parts of Wisconsin began mailing absentee ballots last week; more states start this month.

Left view

NPR and the ACLU frame this as an administrative obstacle inserted mid-election with no fraud record behind it, and lean on Talwani's finding about the timing. The specific worry is mechanical rather than theoretical: if a state's upload is incomplete or a name is mismatched, a lawfully requested ballot simply is not delivered, and the voter may not learn why until it is too late to cure.

Right view

The executive order's own language, echoed by supporters, is that unique envelope identifiers confirm only citizens receive ballots. A 13-state coalition led by Alabama's attorney general argued in comments that the rule would help combat fraud, and supporters told USPS that better tracking would expose discrepancies between ballots sent and returned. The Washington Examiner has covered the litigation as the administration being repeatedly blocked on a modest chain-of-custody measure. National Review-adjacent election writers have been more cautious, noting that changing rules this close to voting invites the chaos both sides claim to want to avoid.

Watch for

The Court already let parts of the underlying executive order proceed in August, so the shadow-docket posture matters more than the merits right now. Election administrators expect that if the justices grant relief, states mid-stream in mailing ballots face the hardest operational problem — and litigation over individual undelivered ballots becomes likely in close races. A denial effectively shelves the rule until after November.

UK bans West Bank settlement imports and cites “ethnic cleansing”; Israel retaliates

Facts

  • Britain announced a ban on imports originating in Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank, with Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband saying settlers are carrying out ethnic cleansing and restating that the settlements are illegal under international law.
  • Canada and France said they will impose similar bans; Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Poland, Portugal and Sweden pledged support for further action — twelve states in all.
  • Israel responded by barring 12 British MPs and ordering the British consulate in Jerusalem closed within 30 days. The UK is defending the sanctions.
  • Foreign ministers of Türkiye and seven other countries welcomed the move Thursday.

Left view

The Guardian and Al Jazeera cast this as overdue rather than dramatic — a first concrete step after the UK recognized a Palestinian state last year, and one that follows existing UK legal position rather than breaking new ground. An ECFR poll cited by Al Jazeera found 72% of Britons back the trade ban when “don't knows” are excluded.

Right view

Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch, writing in The Sun, called the measures performative politics aimed at Labour MPs and activists regardless of the consequences for Britain. The wider right-of-center objection is about proportion and leverage: settlement goods are a small share of UK–Israel trade, so the ban costs Britain a consulate and diplomatic access while changing little on the ground. US coverage on the right has emphasized the split it opens between London and Washington.

Watch for

The consequential number is how many of the twelve states convert pledges into actual customs rules, and whether the EU takes it up as a bloc — that is the difference between a symbolic measure and a real trade instrument. Also watch whether Israel escalates beyond the consulate closure, and whether the Trump administration publicly breaks with London, which would be the first significant US–UK rift of this term.

Canada's retaliatory tariffs take effect as the trade fight widens

Facts

  • Canadian retaliatory tariffs of 15% to 50% took effect at 12:01 a.m. ET Tuesday on roughly C$27.6 billion (about US$20 billion) of American goods, including steel, household appliances, agricultural equipment and dairy. Ottawa paired them with support for affected domestic businesses.
  • The measures answer a US 50% levy on about $20 billion of Canadian goods imposed after talks broke down in late August.
  • The White House responded with presidential actions banning imports of many Canadian dairy products, motorcycles and alcoholic beverages beginning September 29.
  • Analysts cited the escalation as one of the drags on US equities Wednesday alongside oil and yields.

Left view

Al Jazeera and center-left economics coverage stress that the targets are consumer-facing — appliances, food, alcohol — so the incidence lands on households in both countries, and that the collapse of talks damaged a relationship that took decades to build. Prime Minister Carney's subsidy package is read as an admission that the tariffs hurt Canadians too.

Right view

Supporters of the administration's approach argue Canada's dairy supply-management system and digital-services measures were longstanding grievances the US finally priced, and that Ottawa blinked into retaliation rather than concede. Free-trade conservatives at outlets like Reason and the WSJ editorial page have been consistently hostile to the whole exercise, arguing tariffs are taxes on Americans and that a 50% levy on an allied economy is self-harm dressed as leverage.

Watch for

September 29 is the next hard date — the US import bans take effect then, and they hit categories with concentrated Canadian supply. Watch auto-parts and steel supply chains, where cross-border content rules make tariffs compound, and watch whether either side reopens talks before the US bans bite. Bank economists have flagged the dispute as a modest but real drag on Q4 North American growth, arriving alongside an oil shock.

Russian drone nearly hit Zelensky's plane leaving Moldova

Facts

  • A suspected Russian Shahed-type drone that violated Moldovan and Romanian airspace Tuesday came close to the aircraft carrying Volodymyr Zelensky as it departed Chisinau.
  • Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre disclosed the incident. Zelensky was traveling to Oslo for the funeral of King Harald V.
  • Moldova closed its airspace Tuesday afternoon, delaying three departures including Zelensky's. A drone crashed in a sunflower field in the Riscani district, starting four fires; no injuries were reported.
  • Moldova's ambassador to the US, Vladislav Kulminski, told CNN the incident was likely not an accident.

Left view

The Washington Post and European center-left coverage place this in a pattern of Russian airspace violations testing NATO and near-NATO responses, and argue it strengthens the case for extending air defense coverage over Moldova and Romania rather than treating each incursion as an isolated navigation error.

Right view

Right-leaning coverage of this one has been thin in the US — the Iran war and the convention are absorbing the attention — which is worth flagging. Where it has appeared, the framing is that Moscow escalates precisely when Washington is distracted, and hawks use it to argue against any settlement that leaves Russian strike capability intact. Restrainers on the right read the same facts as a reason to keep the US out of a second theater.

Watch for

Whether NATO members formally raise the incursion, and whether Romania invokes consultations. Moldova's airspace closures are becoming routine, which has practical consequences for civil aviation routing around the Black Sea. Also watch whether Kyiv uses the incident to press for longer-range air defense in ongoing talks over the US-brokered proposal shuttled between Moscow and Kyiv this month.
