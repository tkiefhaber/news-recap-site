The Big Stories Today

US and Iran trade fire at the Strait of Hormuz, ending a month-long lull

Facts

  • US forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz on Sunday, after the IRGC was observed preparing to fire rockets carrying sea mines into the waterway. It was the first American military action in roughly a month in a conflict now past the six-month mark.
  • Iran said it retaliated with missiles aimed at US sites in Jordan; Jordan's military said it intercepted eight missiles in its airspace early Monday.
  • Iranian state media reported a supertanker caught fire after striking two naval mines in the southern Strait.
  • Trump said the US is prepared to hit Iran "hard" if attacks on shipping continue. Secretary of State Rubio has argued Iran has no legal authority to close the Strait.
  • Crude jumped on the news: WTI rose about 1.8% Sunday and Brent about 1.9%, with Brent back above $90 by Tuesday.

Left view

NPR's six-month retrospective frames the war as strategically inconclusive — Iran's regime is battered but entrenched, and the campaign has cost at least 18 American lives and roughly $100 billion. The Guardian and Middle East Eye emphasize Democrats' "no end in sight" criticism, noting Trump promised a short war in March and now says he is not in a hurry. Just Security and Sens. Merkley and Gillibrand press the constitutional point: war powers resolutions passed both chambers, and continued strikes without fresh authorization are being called illegal.

Right view

Fox News frames Sunday's action as narrow and defensive — hitting launchers already loaded with mines, not opening a new front — and amplifies Rubio's argument that letting Tehran throttle Hormuz would set a precedent other states would copy. A Fox opinion piece is notably critical from the right, arguing Trump's campaign lacks a defined end state and pushing "hard coercive containment": keep the Strait open with coalition naval power, strike when attacked, tighten sanctions, arm Gulf partners, and avoid ground troops.

Watch for

Energy analysts see Brent trading on a Hormuz risk premium rather than fundamentals; EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook had modeled Q3 Brent near $85, and it is running above that. A confirmed mining campaign — the supertanker fire suggests mines are already in the water — would push insurance rates and freight costs up faster than crude itself. The Hill notes GOP strategists worry an unpopular, open-ended war is a midterm liability nine weeks out.

Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole turn sets up a September rate-hike fight

Facts

  • Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said at Jackson Hole that inflation progress has not been meaningful and that he would have little option but to raise rates this year if inflation stays well above the 2% target.
  • Fed funds futures now price roughly a 60% chance of a quarter-point hike at the September meeting, up from about 56% before the speech, per CME FedWatch.
  • Warsh also argued for a "quieter" central bank that speaks less often and less expansively.
  • Summer inflation prints came in better than expected, but Warsh said they do not show the underlying trend improving.

Left view

NPR's coverage stresses that a hike would land on households already squeezed by an energy shock they did not cause, and questions raising rates into oil-driven inflation that monetary policy cannot address. Bloomberg frames the speech as setting up a showdown with the Treasury, since higher rates raise the government's own borrowing costs.

Right view

The WSJ editorial board's long-running position — that the Fed lost credibility by tolerating above-target inflation — reads Warsh's stance as overdue discipline, and his "quieter Fed" line as a welcome retreat from the forward-guidance era. CNBC's analyst roundup notes hawks see the hike as insurance against expectations becoming unanchored while oil rises.

Watch for

Morningstar and several sell-side economists think the hike slips to October or December rather than September. Either way the Fed is now the swing variable for equities: Monday's decline came with the S&P still holding a positive August, so a surprise hike would test that. Watch the August CPI print and any Treasury pushback if Warsh moves.

Army Secretary Dan Driscoll resigns after repeated clashes with Hegseth

Facts

  • Driscoll submitted his resignation to Trump after 18 months in the job, effective September 3. The White House gave no reason.
  • He was the youngest Army secretary in US history and a Vance ally, nicknamed "drone guy" for his push on modernization.
  • The break followed Hegseth's move to force Army chief of staff Gen. Randy George into retirement, which Driscoll publicly opposed, plus the rollback of a drone program Driscoll championed.
  • Hegseth has removed more than a dozen senior military leaders. Reporting also indicates he ordered the removal of the Army's public affairs chief.
  • White House spokeswoman Anna Kelly's statement praised Driscoll for advancing Trump's agenda and did not mention the friction.

Left view

The Washington Post and CNN treat the resignation as evidence of severe dysfunction at the Pentagon during an active war, noting the timing — losing both the Army secretary and the Army chief of staff while US forces exchange fire with Iran. Responsible Statecraft describes a "wild west" Pentagon where loyalty tests outrank institutional continuity.

Right view

The Washington Examiner covers it primarily as a turf fight Hegseth won, framing the departures as the civilian leadership asserting control over a service bureaucracy. But the story splits the right: parts of the populist coalition backed Driscoll's drone and procurement agenda and see the rollback as a loss for reform, not a win for it.

Watch for

Who replaces Driscoll and whether the nominee's confirmation becomes a Senate proxy fight over Hegseth. Also watch the drone modernization line items in the next Army budget request — that program is the concrete thing at stake beyond personalities.

Commerce quietly drops the political-interference ban from its scientific integrity policy

Facts

  • An unannounced revision dated August 19 removed language barring attempts to shape or interfere with data collection and statistical analysis without scientific justification.
  • Also gone: the assurance that findings are not suppressed, delayed, or altered for political purposes, and the listing of "protection from inappropriate influence" as a hallmark of scientific integrity.
  • The revision came one day after the Census Bureau issued an unusual report making claims about non-US citizens.
  • The policy covers every Commerce agency, including the Census Bureau, the Bureau of Economic Analysis, and NOAA.
  • Nancy Potok, a former Census deputy director, called the removal a major change that eliminates guardrails against political interference in the department's data.

Left view

NPR broke the story and frames it as a direct threat to the credibility of federal statistics — GDP, trade, employment inputs, and the census itself all flow through Commerce agencies. The emphasis is on sequencing: the guardrail was cut the day after a contested report, not in a routine review cycle.

Right view

Coverage on the right is thin so far — this is largely an NPR-led story and mainstream pickup has been limited. The general conservative argument in this space, made by outlets like the Washington Examiner and National Review in earlier statistical-agency disputes, is that "scientific integrity" language has been used to insulate career staff from any political accountability, and that elected officials are entitled to direct agencies they oversee. Treat that as the likely framing rather than a reported response to this specific change.

Watch for

Markets care about this more than it looks: BEA and Census produce the data the Fed and bond market price off. Watch for congressional letters demanding the old language be restored, and for whether the American Statistical Association or former agency heads issue a formal objection. Any perceived break in data credibility tends to show up as a wider risk premium, not a headline.

Left view

CNN's new midterms newsletter and The Hill point to Trump's sagging approval, an unpopular Iran war, and economic anxiety as a compounding drag on GOP incumbents. Expect national Republicans to run against El-Sayed's primary positioning rather than on the war.

Watch for

Michigan is the cleanest test of whether the Iran war moves votes. Jury convicts Duane "Keffe D" Davis of first-degree murder in Tupac Shakur's 1996 killing

Facts

  • Davis, 63, was found guilty Monday of first-degree murder with use of a deadly weapon, thirty years after the Las Vegas drive-by shooting.
  • The verdict came after a roughly two-week trial and only hours of jury deliberation.
  • Prosecutors dismissed the gang enhancement charge before the case went to the jury.
  • The state's case leaned heavily on Davis' own accounts — police interviews and his 2019 memoir — portraying him as the shot-caller who ordered the shooting in retaliation for a casino-lobby assault on his nephew earlier that night.
  • Judge Carli Kierny will sentence him October 13; he faces life without parole.

Left view

NPR and CNN foreground the three-decade delay as an indictment of how thinly the case was investigated at the time, and note the defense argument that a conviction built largely on a defendant's own boasting — made for money and status — is thinner evidence than it appears.

Right view

Coverage on the right, including the NY Post, treats the verdict straightforwardly as accountability finally arriving, and as vindication for the cold-case detectives who reopened the file. There is little partisan disagreement on this story; the framing gap is tone rather than substance.

Watch for

An appeal is near-certain, focused on the admissibility of the memoir and interview statements and on whether an immunity agreement from an earlier federal proffer should have barred their use. The October 13 sentencing is the next date.
