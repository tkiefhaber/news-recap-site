Trump replaces Susie Wiles with Stephen Miller as chief of staff, and pressure on Hegseth keeps building

Facts

  • Trump said Friday that Deputy Chief of Staff Stephen Miller will become White House chief of staff on Monday, Nov. 9. Susie Wiles, who ran his 2024 campaign and has held the job since day one of the second term, resigned Thursday night and moves to the "Trump Political Operation." It's the biggest staff change since Tuesday's midterm losses.
  • Miller designed the administration's immigration-enforcement and deportation program and has a broad policy portfolio. CNN reports Trump has been furious about the midterm results.
  • Reuters, as cited by Fox News, reports that Miller, OMB Director Russ Vought and White House lawyers have spent months listing actions Trump can take without Congress, covering immigration, tariffs, housing and spending. Trump has called himself "Mr. Veto."
  • Defense Secretary Hegseth is under growing pressure. CBS reports Trump has asked advisers whether Hegseth should stay, though Trump says publicly he's "doing a very good job." Rep. Thomas Massie plans to force a House impeachment vote as soon as next week unless Hegseth resigns. Sen. Thom Tillis has urged Trump to fire him, and Oversight Chair James Comer says there are "a lot of questions." The Pentagon says Hegseth "will not back down."

Left view

House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries called Miller a "dangerous xenophobe" and told Trump to fire him (The Hill). CBS News and CNN framed the promotion as Trump doubling down on the agenda voters just rejected rather than moving toward the center. Incoming Oversight Chair Robert Garcia is already planning investigations into Trump family business dealings.

Right view

Fox News presents the move as a tactical shift toward governing by executive action. One administration official called Miller "a great pick" and said reversing mass migration is the defining issue, and Kayleigh McEnany praised Miller on Fox as a "fighter." Fox also found unease among Hill Republicans. One House Republican said they'd been worried about this for two years, and another expects the White House to keep governing "without Congress."

Watch for

Expect more executive orders and vetoes, and Democratic subpoenas once the new Congress is seated in January. Whether Hegseth survives the Massie vote is the next test of how far GOP lawmakers will break with Trump. The Dec. 11 funding deadline is the first big lame-duck fight. Tariffs are on the reported list of executive actions, so don't expect trade policy to soften.

Blowout jobs report: 165K added, unemployment falls to 4.1%, and Fed hike odds jump

Facts

  • The economy added 165,000 jobs in October. The Wall Street Journal's survey of economists expected about 65,000. Revisions added 84,000 to August and September, so hiring has averaged 137K a month over the last three months, up from 28K over May–July.
  • Unemployment fell to 4.1% from 4.2%. Professional services, health care, transportation and manufacturing accounted for about 94% of the gains, while leisure and hospitality lost jobs.
  • Wages rose 3.1% from a year earlier, the slowest pace since 2021 and below 3.6% PCE inflation, so real pay is still falling.
  • Fed-funds futures now put the odds of a December 8–9 rate hike at about 79%, up from about 67% Thursday (Hubbard-O'Brien Economics). The 10-year yield rose to about 4.86%.

Left view

NPR and NBC News called the report a welcome bright spot, but NBC stressed that the economy still risks stagnation. Center-left economists point out that paychecks are trailing prices, which fits voters' "affordability" anger on Tuesday. Bloomberg Intelligence's Ira Jersey noted underemployment ticked up.

Right view

The White House called it a "blowout" that "smashes expectations" and credited tariffs, tax cuts and reshoring. Fox Business and conservative economists such as Steve Moore and E.J. Antoni echoed that message. The administration is using the report to argue the midterm losses weren't about the economy.

Watch for

A December hike is now the base case for markets. That would mean higher mortgage and credit-card rates and probably a stronger dollar. The October CPI report is the next big data point. Allianz Trade's Dan North called the labor market "stable but perhaps unexciting," and the household survey still shows job losses for the year, so some economists aren't convinced.

Iran: U.S. hits Qeshm air defenses again as Trump claims "a lot of progress" in talks

Facts

  • U.S. forces carried out limited strikes on Iranian air-defense systems on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz Friday night. Trump called it a "minor" action and said "we are not fighting." Iran's Revolutionary Guard says it shot down a U.S. drone, which the U.S. hasn't confirmed (Euronews).
  • Trump also said the U.S. has made "a lot of progress" and that Iran wants a deal. Qatar, the main mediator, denied reports that talks had collapsed.
  • Tehran says it won't discuss its nuclear program until seven conditions are met. They include ending the port blockade, lifting sanctions, unfreezing assets, paying war reparations and stopping Israeli operations in Lebanon. Security official Mohsen Rezaei, who met Putin in Moscow, says Hormuz stays shut until then (The National, Reuters). Separately, Iran's navy reported another ship fire in Hormuz.
  • Trump met his security team twice this week. Fox News reports CENTCOM is finishing preparations for a possible larger operation, but no order has been given.

Progressive outlets and Democrats call the new strikes proof that the war is still going despite Tuesday's verdict. They want Congress to enforce the War Powers Resolution, which is also the basis of Massie's Hegseth impeachment articles. International coverage (Euronews, The National) treats Iran's conditions as a sign Tehran thinks the midterms gave it leverage.

Right view

Fox News coverage highlights military readiness and the blockade as leverage, and treats limited strikes as pressure that keeps Iran negotiating. Hawks want no sanctions relief before nuclear concessions. The Vance-aligned restraint wing sees Trump's "progress" talk as a step toward the deal it has been pushing.

Watch for

The new moon on Nov. 9 favors night operations, and the USS Roosevelt arrives mid-November. Brent sits just under $100. Analysts cited this week say a big escalation would push it back above $110, while a framework deal could send it much lower and keep gas prices falling.

Israel: Herzog holds off on a mandate until Sunday despite Eisenkot's 67–46 edge

Facts

  • After consultations, 67 Knesset members recommended Yashar leader Gadi Eisenkot and 46 backed Netanyahu. United Torah Judaism recommended no one.
  • President Herzog said the numbers look clear "on the surface," but he wants to confirm a government can actually be formed, given vetoes between and within the blocs. He's holding direct talks with party leaders and will announce Sunday, Nov. 8 (Ynet).
  • Eisenkot's support includes the Arab parties Ra'am and the Joint List alongside Yisrael Beytenu and the center-left. Yashar wants a "national emergency government" formed quickly.

Left view

Center-left Israeli and international outlets (Ynet, Al-Monitor) see Herzog's caution as procedure, not a reversal, and treat Netanyahu's exit as likely. They also question whether a coalition this broad can hold together.

Right view

Likud's Yoav Kisch warned Herzog against a government that relies on Arab parties, and pointed to Iran and Palestinian statehood as the stakes. Right-leaning Brussels Signal framed the delay as Herzog "holding back" a mandate despite a clear majority. U.S. right-leaning coverage is thin, with attention on the midterms.

Watch for

Whoever gets the mandate has 28 days, plus a possible 14-day extension. An Eisenkot government would come in as Washington decides between a deal and more strikes on Iran, and Vance is already calling for a change in U.S.–Israel ties.
