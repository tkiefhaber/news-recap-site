The Big Stories Today

Witkoff and Kushner leave Moscow for Kyiv with a “concrete” Ukraine proposal

Facts

  • Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Vladimir Putin at the Kremlin on Saturday for roughly three hours, followed by dinner. It was the first substantive US–Russia session on Ukraine in about eight months.
  • The envoys fly to Kyiv today to brief Volodymyr Zelensky on Putin’s response and to present what the White House calls a concrete settlement proposal. Details are promised “in the coming weeks.”
  • No breakthrough was announced. Reporting from the Kremlin readout describes ideas exchanged rather than commitments made.
  • The core gap is unchanged: Russia wants territorial recognition in Donbas and a neutral Ukraine; Kyiv wants sovereignty and enforceable security guarantees.
  • Putin said Thursday there is “a chance” of a deal, while also saying Ukrainian aviation activity was making one harder.

Left view

The Guardian and NPR frame the trip as pressure applied in one direction. Zelensky has said since Munich in February that Washington keeps returning to what Ukraine must give up rather than what Russia must. Critics note Kushner is a private citizen with extensive Gulf business ties negotiating on behalf of the US government, and that a January Kyiv International Institute of Sociology poll found 54% of Ukrainians still oppose ceding territory — so a deal signed over that opposition may not hold.

Right view

Fox News and the broader pro-negotiation right cast this as momentum after two years of stalemate, arguing only Trump’s envoys have gotten inside the Kremlin at all. The Free Press and Reason split from that: several conservative writers have pushed back on Trump’s framing that Russia not conquering all of Ukraine counts as a concession, pointing out Ukrainian forces — not Russian restraint — are the reason. National Review’s line remains that a deal ratifying Russian gains rewards the invasion.

Watch for

Analysts at Carnegie argue any durable deal needs European enforcement Washington has not yet secured, so watch whether the Kyiv readout mentions guarantors. Markets would read a credible framework as bearish for European defense names and modestly bearish for oil, though the Iran war is currently the dominant crude input. If Zelensky rejects the proposal, expect the next fight to be over US intelligence sharing and weapons flow as leverage.

Iran strikes Kuwait as the war Trump said would end soon widens again

Facts

  • Kuwait’s army said it confronted incoming Iranian missiles and drones on September 3, retaliation for renewed US bombing. Iran has also fired on targets in Bahrain, Jordan and Iraq during this round.
  • Trump said the renewed hostilities would not run “too long,” and separately warned Iran would be “totally wiped out” if it kept retaliating.
  • The war began February 28 with joint US–Israeli strikes that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and much of Iran’s senior leadership. Thirteen US service members and more than a thousand civilians have been killed since.
  • Vice President JD Vance said he is “extremely skeptical” of Iranian claims that a US strike hit a wedding party, and declined to call the conflict a war.
  • Congress passed a concurrent War Powers resolution in June directing removal of US forces from hostilities with Iran. Fighting has continued.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian emphasize the civilian toll and the constitutional question: both chambers voted to end the hostilities and the strikes continued anyway. Hakeem Jeffries’ framing — a war with no stated objective, no exit strategy and no congressional authorization — is the standard Democratic line, and Vox has argued the decapitation strike removed the only Iranian actor capable of ordering a stand-down.

Right view

Fox News covers the Kuwait attacks as proof Iran remains the aggressor and that pressure must be sustained. But the right is genuinely split: Reason and parts of the America First coalition treat this as exactly the open-ended Middle East war Trump campaigned against, and The Dispatch has criticized the administration for prosecuting a war it will not name or define. National Review is closer to the hawkish position that a nuclear-capable Iran was the greater risk.

Watch for

Reuters reports Trump aides are pushing to keep escalation contained before the November 3 midterms, which argues for a tactical pause rather than a settlement. Energy desks are watching Kuwaiti and Emirati export infrastructure — Brent rose nearly 9% last week on this. Any confirmed hit on a loading terminal would put Brent through $100 and add roughly 15–25 cents to the pump within a month.

A hot jobs report sets up a rate-hike showdown between the Fed and the White House

Facts

  • August nonfarm payrolls rose 162,000 against a consensus near 53,000. Unemployment held at 4.1%.
  • Gains were concentrated in food services and drinking places (+59,000) and local government education (+42,000). The information sector shed jobs.
  • Average hourly earnings rose 0.3% on the month to $37.75, up 3.1% year over year.
  • Futures now put roughly 60–66% odds on a quarter-point hike at the September 15–16 FOMC meeting, up sharply after Fed Chair Kevin Warsh’s Jackson Hole speech warning that summer inflation readings showed no meaningful improvement.
  • Three FOMC members already dissented in favor of a hike at the July meeting. In the past week Trump, Vance, Treasury Secretary Bessent and a senior White House economic adviser have all publicly urged the Fed not to raise rates.

Left view

NPR and The Atlantic focus on the pressure campaign itself: a coordinated push by four senior administration officials against an independent central bank ten days before a decision is the story, regardless of what the right call on rates is. The Guardian adds that the job gains are concentrated in low-wage services and public education, which is a weaker composition than the headline implies.

Right view

The Wall Street Journal editorial board and National Review have generally backed Warsh’s inflation vigilance, treating the willingness to hike into an election year as the credibility test the Fed failed in 2021. The more Trump-aligned right, and Bessent specifically, argue the war-driven energy spike is a supply shock the Fed should look through rather than answer with tighter money that would hit housing and small business.

Watch for

Next week’s CPI print is the swing factor; a hot core reading likely locks in the hike. A hike would extend the dollar’s run and pressure the long end further — the 30-year already touched a 19-year high last month. Watch mortgage rates and regional bank earnings guidance, and watch whether Warsh addresses the White House pressure directly at the post-meeting presser.

Judge extends block on Trump’s mail-ballot rules as the case heads to the Supreme Court

Facts

  • US District Judge Indira Talwani issued a preliminary injunction finding the new USPS mail-ballot rule likely unlawful and unconstitutional, extending an earlier temporary block indefinitely.
  • The rule would require states to give USPS a voter’s name and address tied to a unique barcode on each mail ballot; carriers would deliver only to voters on the state-supplied list.
  • Talwani found the compressed timeline made voter disenfranchisement “practically inevitable.” Roughly one-third of US voters cast mail ballots.
  • Participation is nominally voluntary for states. No state has announced it will opt in.
  • The administration has asked the Supreme Court to let it implement the rule. Ballots are already going out in some states for the November 3 midterms.
  • A USPS whistleblower told reporters on September 1 the agency was rushing an online system that could “derail” the election if courts allow it.

Left view

NPR, MSNBC and the Washington Post treat the injunction as a narrow save. Their argument: the executive branch has no authority to set ballot-handling rules, election officials say they cannot rebuild systems mid-cycle, and a federal voter roll attached to individual ballots is a surveillance mechanism dressed as fraud prevention. Mother Jones goes further and reads the timing — ballots already mailing — as the point.

Right view

Fox News and the New York Post frame this as another district judge blocking a presidential directive nationwide, part of a broader complaint about single-judge injunctions. The Washington Examiner argues verifying that ballots reach real registered voters at real addresses is unobjectionable in principle and that the objection is really about the calendar. The Dispatch and some conservative election lawyers break here, calling the rushed rollout unworkable regardless of the merits.

Watch for

The Supreme Court partly cleared an earlier piece of this order in late August, so the emergency application is live and consequential. Election administrators say a ruling after roughly early October is operationally moot for November. If SCOTUS lets any of it take effect, expect immediate state-level litigation and slower mail-ballot processing in the states with the tightest Senate races.

The anti–data center backlash is the rare bipartisan issue of the midterms

Facts

  • Campaigns and outside groups have spent more than $45 million this year on ads mentioning data centers across gubernatorial, House and Senate races.
  • More than 99% of that spending is against AI infrastructure projects. Last month, data center and AI-related spots took more than 8% of all broadcast TV campaign spending.
  • Both parties are running essentially the same argument: too many tax breaks, rising residential electricity bills, and too little local control.
  • In Ohio, Democrat Sherrod Brown has spent millions branding Republican Sen. Jon Husted “the face of data centers.” In Texas, Gov. Greg Abbott is pushing to restrict rural siting and strip incentives. In Pennsylvania, Gov. Josh Shapiro imposed new guardrails after criticism he had courted developers.

Left view

Vox and Mother Jones read this as utility bills finally becoming an electoral force: ratepayers subsidize transmission for facilities that create few permanent jobs, and the water and land use falls on communities with the least leverage. Axios notes leading 2028 Democrats have already retreated from earlier pro-AI positioning, which the left reads as the base pulling the party.

Right view

National Review and The Dispatch see a property-rights and localism story — state governments handing out targeted corporate subsidies over the objections of the towns absorbing the cost. Fox News emphasizes the electricity-bill angle. But there is a real split: the pro-growth right, including parts of the WSJ editorial page, warns that blocking capacity cedes the AI buildout to China and that Abbott’s restrictions cut against Texas’s own economic model.

Watch for

RealClearPolitics is calling this a September surprise. Analysts expect it to show up first in state legislative and gubernatorial races rather than federal ones, and to force hyperscalers toward behind-the-meter generation and self-funded transmission. Watch utility regulator filings in Ohio, Virginia and Georgia, and watch whether any major operator publicly restructures a tax package before November.

Himalayan flood toll passes 1,100 as a survivor is pulled from a tunnel after ten days

Facts

  • Rescuers pulled a Chinese national alive from a flooded hydropower tunnel on Saturday, ten days after the disaster.
  • Combined deaths in Nepal and China exceed 1,100, with roughly 4,500 people unaccounted for. Nepal reported 781 dead and 2,502 missing, including 592 foreign nationals; China reported 16 dead and 546 missing.
  • Part of a glacier at about 5,200 meters collapsed, fell roughly 1,200 meters gathering rock and debris, and hit the Lende River.
  • About 90,000 people in Nepal are affected. Around 900 workers are missing across 12 hydropower projects, with an estimated 500 believed trapped in tunnels.
  • Teams from India, China and South Korea have joined the search. Operations have been repeatedly suspended for weather and fresh flood risk.
  • A CNN-reported assessment found warning signs were visible before the collapse.

Left view

The Guardian and PBS NewsHour center glacial retreat: warming has destabilized high-altitude ice across the Hindu Kush–Himalaya, and building hydropower tunnels below unstable glaciers without adequate monitoring is a policy failure with a known cause. The reporting that warning signs were visible beforehand is the core of this framing.

Right view

Coverage on the right is thin — this is a story mainstream and international outlets are leading and conservative US outlets have largely not engaged with. Where the right has weighed in, the emphasis is on engineering standards, siting decisions and construction oversight in the specific projects rather than on climate attribution, and on the practical limits of aid delivery in that terrain.

Watch for

Glaciologists quoted by PBS expect pressure for a regional early-warning system covering glacial lake outburst risk. Near-term effects are local — Nepal’s hydropower pipeline and its power-export contracts with India are both exposed, and the missing-foreign-national count means diplomatic friction with China and South Korea. Reinsurers are likely to reprice Himalayan infrastructure risk.

Philippine VP Sara Duterte posts bail on charges she threatened to have Marcos killed

Facts

  • Duterte posted 360,000 pesos (about $5,740) bail on Saturday for three counts of grave threats, a day after a court ordered her arrest.
  • The charges stem from remarks in a 2024 online news conference in which she said she would have President Ferdinand Marcos Jr., his wife and then-House Speaker Martin Romualdez killed by an assassin if she were killed.
  • She says she was expressing fear for her own safety, not making a threat, and denies the allegations.
  • She presented a court order lifting the arrest warrant and skipped the standard booking photographs, per the Manila Times.
  • The same remarks are central to her pending Senate impeachment trial. Conviction would remove her from office and permanently bar her from politics.

Left view

NPR and Al Jazeera present this straightforwardly as an accountability test for Philippine institutions, and note the Duterte family’s own record — her father’s drug war and his ICC case — as the backdrop against which claims of political persecution should be weighed.

Right view

US conservative outlets have given this little attention. The sympathetic reading, which comes mostly from Duterte-aligned Philippine media rather than the American right, is that criminal charges layered on top of an impeachment trial against the leading 2028 opposition candidate look like elimination of a rival by legal process. The Manila Times’ detail about skipped mugshots feeds the counter-argument that she is receiving unusual deference.

Watch for

The Senate impeachment trial is the consequential track; the criminal case mainly shapes its politics. Watch the Philippine peso and defense-cooperation signaling — the Duterte wing favors accommodation with Beijing while Marcos has deepened US basing access, so her political survival has direct implications for US posture in the South China Sea.
