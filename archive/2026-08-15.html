The Big Stories Today

Trump says he will declare the Strait of Hormuz a U.S. territory; Iran says it stays closed

Facts

  • Speaking Friday at the David S. Mack Center in Garden City, N.Y., Trump said that once Iran is defeated he will declare the Strait of Hormuz a territory of the United States. He offered no legal mechanism, timetable, or formal territorial process.
  • Iran's deputy foreign minister rejected the claim, saying the strait will remain Iranian and cannot be taken by declaration, aircraft carrier, or campaign speech. Tehran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority repeated that the waterway stays blocked until Iran's conditions are met.
  • The strait has been effectively closed since fighting began in late February. Transit traffic sank to near three-month lows this week, per CNBC, and the IEA warns inventory buffers are draining.
  • Trump said he would "never apologize" for the military campaign and asked Americans to tolerate higher pump prices while it continues. Press secretary Karoline Leavitt said low gas prices and denying Iran a nuclear weapon are equally important goals.
  • A carrier strike group is en route to the region to replace the USS Lincoln, a U.S. official told CNN.

Left view

Al Jazeera and Common Dreams frame the declaration as legally empty and rhetorically reckless — a president claiming an international waterway with no authority to do so. NPR-adjacent and Guardian-style coverage stresses that UNCLOS guarantees transit passage through international straits and that no state can acquire one unilaterally. Rep. Ted Lieu mocked the announcement on X. The National Iranian American Council's Ryan Costello argued the bravado is undercut by U.S. bases near the strait being largely abandoned under Iranian missile and drone threat.

Right view

Fox News is not uniformly on board: Bret Baier pressed Energy Secretary Chris Wright on air over the administration's claim that Washington controls the strait, and Wright struggled to substantiate it. Fox's live coverage has run Iran's "miscalculated" taunts prominently. The broader pro-administration case is that maximum pressure — reinstated port blockade, naval interdiction — is the only lever that forces Tehran to reopen the waterway, and that a nuclear-armed Iran would be far costlier than $4 gas. Notably, parts of the right (Tucker Carlson, Megyn Kelly, Marjorie Taylor Greene, Joe Rogan) have broken with Trump on the war entirely.

Watch for

Maritime law scholars say any transit toll or territorial claim would be unenforceable and would invite challenge; expect no formal executive action to actually follow. Energy analysts are watching whether draining OECD inventories force a strategic reserve release or coordinated IEA action. Brent above $88 with the strait shut keeps the U.S. pump average pinned near $4; a genuine reopening deal is the single biggest downside catalyst for oil. Politically, the gas-price line is the one Democrats intend to run on in November.

Consumer sentiment falls to 51.0, ending a two-month recovery

Facts

  • The preliminary University of Michigan consumer sentiment index for August fell about 8% to 51.0 from 55.2 in July, well under the 55.0 consensus.
  • Expectations dropped to 50.6 from 55.4; current conditions fell to 51.8 from 54.8. Expected business conditions sank 11% short-run and 17% long-run.
  • One-year inflation expectations ticked up to 4.3% from 4.2%, still far above the 3.4% pre-war reading in February. Five-to-ten-year expectations held at 3.3% for a third month.
  • Declines were broad across demographic and political lines, with unusually sharp drops among Republicans, older consumers, lower-income households, and non-college respondents. Only 8% expect income growth to outpace inflation, down from 18% in December 2024.
  • Stocks slipped on the print after the S&P 500 set a record earlier in the week.

Left view

MS NOW (formerly MSNBC) and The Washington Post's politics desk tie the sentiment collapse directly to the Iran war: a president who campaigned on cheaper energy has instead delivered a 29% year-over-year jump at the pump, and the Republican-heavy drop in confidence is the tell that his own coalition is feeling it. Time's coverage emphasizes Democrats calling the "peanuts" framing on gas prices out of touch.

Right view

Fox News has documented the GOP's own tonal shift — from attacking Biden over gas prices to calling today's prices a small price to pay — and is reporting Democratic attacks on the "broken promise" straight rather than dismissing them. The steadier conservative economic case, made in outlets like the WSJ editorial page and The Dispatch, is that soft-survey sentiment has diverged from hard data for years, that the labor market and equity markets remain intact, and that a war-driven energy shock is temporary and reversible in a way structural inflation is not.

Watch for

Economists generally treat sentiment as a weak predictor of actual spending, but a sustained sub-52 reading paired with 4%+ one-year inflation expectations complicates the Fed's path. Futures pricing has leaned toward another cut around September; a fresh energy-driven inflation impulse argues the other way. Watch the final August UMich reading, the next CPI print, and back-to-school retail data for whether pessimism is converting into weaker consumption.

Second federal court blocks Trump's mail-in voting order ahead of the midterms

Facts

  • U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani granted a preliminary injunction Tuesday barring the U.S. Postal Service from implementing the mail-voting provisions of Trump's March executive order, nullifying that directive nationwide.
  • The order had directed USPS to build a system regulating who may vote by mail and pushed states to create special absentee-request lists. Plaintiffs include the League of Women Voters.
  • This is the second judicial hold on the order. The first is pending before the Supreme Court, where the administration has urged the justices to rule and challenging states have asked them to leave the limits in place.
  • The practical effect is that the order is now very unlikely to be operative for the November 3 election.
  • Separately, the Supreme Court ruled 5–4 in June that federal law does not bar states from counting mail ballots that arrive after Election Day, upholding a Mississippi grace period. Justice Barrett joined the majority; Justice Alito dissented.

Left view

NPR, Votebeat, and the ACLU frame this as courts holding the line against an executive attempt to rewrite election administration by decree — a power the Constitution assigns to states and Congress. The Brennan Center's argument is that the order's real function was deterrence and confusion in the weeks before a midterm, and that blocking it nationwide is the only remedy that works.

Right view

Fox News covered the June Supreme Court ruling largely through the lens of conservative revolt, quoting Sen. Eric Schmitt calling the opinion shockingly wrong and Cleta Mitchell warning it hands the left a weapon in every close race. PJ Media declared election integrity effectively dead as a legal project. The substantive right-of-center case is Alito's: post-Election Day counting invites litigation and erodes public confidence, and the answer is legislation like the SAVE Act rather than judicial improvisation.

Watch for

Election lawyers expect the Supreme Court to act on the pending application in the next several weeks; an emergency-docket order either way sets the ground rules for November. If the injunctions hold, expect the fight to shift to state legislatures and to post-election certification disputes in close races. With Democrats leading the generic ballot by roughly 5 to 11 points depending on pollster, the margin questions may matter most in a handful of House seats.

Luigi Mangione pleads guilty to federal stalking charges in UnitedHealthcare CEO's killing

Facts

  • Mangione, 28, entered an open plea Friday in Manhattan federal court to two counts of stalking resulting in death — guilty to all pending federal counts with no agreement or concessions from prosecutors.
  • He admitted trailing Brian Thompson to a December 2024 investor conference, having contacted the company posing as an investor at a large firm to obtain details, and shooting Thompson on a Manhattan street on the morning of December 4, 2024.
  • Federal guidelines suggest roughly 24 to 30 years; prosecutors remain free to seek life. Judge Garnett set sentencing for December 18.
  • Thompson's mother, widow, and other family members were in the front row. The family called the plea an important step toward justice; UnitedHealthcare thanked law enforcement.
  • Defense counsel immediately moved to dismiss the pending New York state trial on double jeopardy grounds.

Left view

PBS NewsHour and CNN largely keep the framing on the victim's family and the legal mechanics, while noting the case became a cultural flashpoint over health insurance denials — including audience cheers at a Saturday Night Live mention. Left-leaning commentary tends to separate the two threads explicitly: the killing was indefensible, and the wave of public sympathy is a symptom of real grievance about coverage denials that the industry has not answered.

Right view

Conservative commentary, including The Conservative Treehouse, has focused on what it calls the unnerving volume of online support for a confessed murderer, treating the folk-hero response as evidence of moral rot rather than as a policy signal. NY Post-style coverage emphasizes the premeditation admitted in open court and argues the guidelines range is too lenient for a planned assassination.

Watch for

Two legal questions now drive the case: whether Judge Garnett departs upward toward life at the December 18 sentencing, and whether New York's double jeopardy statute — broader than the federal constitutional rule — actually knocks out the state prosecution. A dismissal there would be a meaningful precedent for federal-first charging strategies. The federal death penalty is off the table given the counts pled.

Colombia's new president invites the U.S. antidrug military campaign into his country

Facts

  • Abelardo de la Espriella, sworn in Friday in Cali rather than Bogotá, has asked the Trump administration to extend its lethal counternarcotics military campaign to Colombia, the world's largest cocaine producer.
  • Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said the new president authorized joint U.S.–Colombian military operations targeting cartels; Colombia is joining Trump's Shield of the Americas program.
  • De la Espriella delivered his first remarks from a military base and pledged to eradicate illicit crops using aerial herbicides he says are safe for people and the environment.
  • Trump endorsed de la Espriella during the campaign. His predecessor, leftist Gustavo Petro, called the U.S. boat strikes off South America murder and disputed the administration's unevidenced claims that the vessels carried drugs.
  • The Trump administration had already begun fast-tracking military cooperation with Bogotá earlier this month.

Left view

The Washington Post's reporting foregrounds that the U.S. campaign has killed people at sea without publicly demonstrated evidence, and that a new government is now inviting that model onshore. CEPR's running tracker of U.S. escalation in the Caribbean and Latin America frames it as an expansion of extrajudicial force with minimal congressional authorization, and notes aerial fumigation was suspended in Colombia for years over health and environmental findings.

Right view

The Washington Examiner presents the shift as a democratically elected partner choosing cooperation after years of Petro's obstruction — a validation of the pressure strategy. The broader argument on the right is that supply-side interdiction plus a willing host government is the fastest route to cutting cocaine flows, and that Colombian sovereignty is respected precisely because Bogotá requested the operations.

Watch for

Regional analysts expect friction over rules of engagement, evidentiary standards for strikes, and whether U.S. forces operate on Colombian soil or only offshore. Watch for congressional demands for a War Powers accounting, for coca cultivation and eradication data over the next two quarters, and for how Venezuela and Brazil respond to a permanent U.S. military footprint on the continent's Pacific and Caribbean approaches.

UN warns Yemen is closer to full-scale war than at any point since the 2022 truce

Facts

  • UN Special Envoy Hans Grundberg told the Security Council that Yemen faces a more serious risk of returning to large-scale conflict than at any time since the UN-mediated 2022 truce.
  • He cited the most significant intensification of military activity since that truce, including Houthi attacks in Marib, Hadramout, and Mocha, with military and civilian casualties.
  • The UN says more than half of Yemen's population is experiencing acute hardship.
  • Grundberg called for maximum restraint and protection of civilians, and warned that renewed fighting would hit a fragmented state and an economy with little capacity to absorb another shock while the wider region is at war.
  • Several Security Council members condemned the escalating Houthi attacks.

Left view

Al Jazeera and Guardian-style coverage put the humanitarian arithmetic first — a decade of war, collapsed institutions, and aid budgets already cut — and argue the Yemen front cannot be separated from the U.S.–Iran war, since escalation there raises the odds here. Coverage in this camp is skeptical that more military pressure produces anything but more displacement.

Right view

Right-of-center defense outlets and FDD's daily brief treat Houthi shipping and territorial attacks as an Iranian proxy problem that diplomacy has failed to contain, arguing the 2022 truce mainly bought Ansar Allah time to rearm. The prescription is sustained interdiction and pressure on Tehran rather than a renewed negotiating track. Mainstream U.S. coverage of Yemen is thin right now; the war with Iran is absorbing the bandwidth.

Watch for

The direct market channel is Red Sea and Bab al-Mandab shipping. With Hormuz already shut, a second chokepoint under threat would compound tanker and container rerouting around the Cape and push freight rates higher — a live inflation input. Watch insurance war-risk premiums for Red Sea transits, any Security Council product beyond statements, and whether Marib frontline movement resumes in force.
