The Big Stories Today

US shifts from strikes to sanctions on Iran as Oman floats a Hormuz workaround

Facts

  • Secretary of State Marco Rubio told several foreign counterparts that the US does not expect to launch new strikes on Iran “for the time being,” with pressure shifting to economic tools, according to reporting compiled by Just Security's daily brief.
  • The Treasury package — branded “Operation Economic Outcast” — targets Iranian aviation, shipping, technology, gold and digital assets, and threatens secondary sanctions on countries that keep economic ties to Tehran. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has called it an “economic D-Day.”
  • Iran and Oman say they have discussed a framework for a temporary joint shipping corridor through the Strait of Hormuz as a precursor to a permanent arrangement. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the strait will not reopen until Washington eases sanctions and pays war reparations.
  • Oil fell for a third straight session Wednesday on those talks: Brent down about 2.5% to roughly $86.38, WTI down about 2.2% to roughly $80.52.

Left view

Al Jazeera quoted analysts describing the sanctions rollout as a sign of desperation after six months of strikes failed to force terms. CNN's political desk made the structural point more bluntly: the strategy only works if it changes China's behavior, and Beijing — Iran's largest trading partner — says the threats will only intensify tensions. NPR and Democracy Now! have emphasized the humanitarian cost inside Iran of a blockade-plus-sanctions squeeze.

Right view

Fox News and allied outlets frame the pivot as leverage working as designed — cutting every economic lifeline to force Tehran to the table without additional American casualties. The Wall Street Journal editorial side has argued the risk is not that sanctions are too harsh but that enforcement against Chinese buyers stays symbolic. The Free Press and The Dispatch have been more split, with some writers warning that an open-ended economic war with no defined endpoint is how the US drifted into past quagmires.

Watch for

Whether the Iran–Oman corridor is actually signed, and on what terms. Energy desks at Reuters and Bloomberg note that traders are already pricing in partial reopening — a confirmed deal could take Brent below $80, while a collapse would put $95 back in play within days. The swing variable is Chinese compliance with secondary sanctions; watch shipments to Shandong teapot refineries as the tell.

Nvidia's blowout quarter lands the night before Warsh's first Jackson Hole speech

Facts

  • Nvidia reported fiscal Q2 results after Wednesday's close: earnings of $2.22 per share on revenue of $96.2 billion, up roughly 106% year over year and above the $93–95 billion analysts had modeled. The company guided Q3 above expectations.
  • Shares jumped in after-hours trading. The major indexes had closed lower on the day — the S&P 500 essentially flat around 7,676, the Dow off about 0.2%, the Nasdaq slipping.
  • The Kansas City Fed's Jackson Hole symposium runs today through Saturday, themed “Financial Innovation: Implications for Payments and Policy.” Kevin Warsh, who succeeded Jerome Powell as Fed chair in May, delivers his first keynote as chair Friday at 10:00 a.m. ET.
  • Futures markets put roughly 89% odds on a quarter-point cut at the September 17 FOMC meeting. Core PCE is running near 3.3% year over year, unemployment is 4.1%, payroll growth has slowed to a 12-month average near 34,000 a month, and Q2 GDP came in at 1.5% annualized.

Left view

Vox and The Atlantic have pressed the concentration argument: a labor market adding 34,000 jobs a month is close to stall speed, and the headline economy is being propped up by a handful of firms building data centers for each other. NPR's business coverage adds that AI capex is not translating into hiring, which is why consumer sentiment keeps diverging from index levels.

Right view

The Wall Street Journal editorial board and Reason argue the Fed should not be cutting with core inflation above 3%, and see Warsh — a longtime critic of Fed balance-sheet expansion — as the right person to say so. National Review's finance writers have used Nvidia's numbers as evidence that the AI buildout is a genuine productivity story, not a bubble, and that the risk is regulatory rather than financial.

Watch for

Warsh's Friday text is the week's biggest market event. Bloomberg reports investors still do not have a clear read on his reaction function; if he declines to validate September, rate-sensitive trades — small caps, homebuilders, the long end of the curve — reprice quickly. On the corporate side, watch whether Nvidia's beat lifts the broader semiconductor complex or just Nvidia, which has been the pattern for three quarters.

CIA director's secret Moscow trip, and Kyiv is asked to hold its fire

Facts

  • CIA Director John Ratcliffe flew from Riga to Moscow on Tuesday aboard a US Air Force C-17. The Kremlin confirmed he held talks with Russian intelligence counterparts — a rare visit given the state of relations.
  • Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Ratcliffe did not meet Vladimir Putin, that Putin was briefed on contacts held “on the intelligence agencies' level,” and declined to describe the agenda.
  • A senior Ukrainian official told the Associated Press that Washington notified Kyiv in advance and asked it to suspend strikes on Moscow, St. Petersburg and some northern regions while the delegation was in country. The stated US purpose was to assess whether peace talks can resume.
  • President Trump played the trip down, calling it “sort of semi-routine.”
  • Separately, former defense chief Mykhailo Fedorov's call for wartime elections continues to pressure Zelenskyy, who said a vote now would be a “tsunami” that could split the country. Polling cited by Foreign Policy found only about 15% of Ukrainians want elections before hostilities end.

Left view

The Guardian and MSNBC commentators focus on the sequencing: the US asked Ukraine to stop hitting Russian territory before any Russian reciprocity was on the table, which they read as a unilateral concession made through a back channel with limited allied coordination. The Atlantic has argued that intelligence-level diplomacy without a State Department track tends to produce deals that cannot survive contact with Congress or NATO capitals.

Right view

The Dispatch and National Review make the practical case that intelligence channels are historically how wars actually get wound down, and that a quiet trip is preferable to a summit that locks in positions. On Ukraine's internal politics, The Hill's opinion page and HotAir both argued Fedorov's election push mainly serves Moscow, and that he has overplayed a weak hand given how unpopular a wartime vote is with Ukrainian voters.

Watch for

Two tells in the next week: whether a formal negotiating round gets scheduled, and whether Ukrainian long-range strikes resume at their prior tempo now that the delegation has left. Analysts quoted by Reuters see the Ratcliffe channel as a probe rather than a breakthrough — the substantive gap on territory and security guarantees has not moved. A Fedorov campaign launch would complicate any US-brokered timeline.

States and the Postal Service scramble to implement Trump's mail-voting order

Facts

  • On Monday the Supreme Court voted 6–3 to lift a lower-court injunction blocking parts of Trump's March executive order on mail voting, holding that California and 22 other states lacked standing. The order expressly does not rule on whether the executive order is lawful.
  • What is now permitted to proceed: DHS compiling a federal citizenship list and sharing it with states; the Attorney General prioritizing prosecution of state officials who issue ballots to ineligible voters; and USPS rulemaking governing state mail-ballot designs.
  • A second injunction, from a separate case, remains in place, and the ACLU's challenge is proceeding with respect to the midterms. Election officials in several states spent this week trying to determine what changes, if any, apply before November 3.
  • Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson's dissent said the ruling would create a “Kafkaesque nightmare” that injects chaos into the midterms.

Left view

Mother Jones voting correspondent Ari Berman, speaking on Democracy Now!, argued the order would radically restrict vote-by-mail and that the practical burden falls hardest on voters with disabilities and rural voters who have no realistic in-person alternative. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer called the order blatantly unconstitutional and said the Court declined to stop it. NPR's coverage stressed that state election directors still do not know which rules govern their ballot printing.

Right view

Fox News covered the decision as a straightforward win for the administration and for election-integrity advocates who have pushed for citizenship verification. Notably, the right is not unified: Reason's write-up was skeptical, framing federal direction of state ballot design as exactly the centralization conservatives have historically opposed, and several state-level Republican election officials have raised the same objection.

Watch for

The operational clock. Legal analysts writing at SCOTUSblog and The Conversation note the ruling settled standing, not constitutionality — so the underlying merits fight continues while USPS rulemaking races ballot-printing deadlines that begin in September in most states. If the rules land after printing starts, expect a wave of emergency litigation in October and real variation in what a mail ballot looks like state to state.

Record August gas prices become the midterms' loudest number

Facts

  • AAA's national average for regular was about $4.10 a gallon as of August 20. The month-to-date average of roughly $4.06 makes this the most expensive August on record, surpassing 2022's $3.97.
  • Gasoline is running roughly 30% above August 2025. Diesel is near $5.47, up about 48% year over year — a freight-cost pass-through that shows up in goods prices with a lag.
  • House Speaker Mike Johnson acknowledged the Iran conflict has been the cause of rising pump prices, while predicting improvement in what he called a new phase of the conflict.
  • Trump has shifted his framing over the month, earlier telling Americans to expect sustained high fuel prices and more recently describing the war's economic toll as a small detour while attributing inflation to Democrats.

Left view

MSNBC and Spotlight PA have made pump prices the center of their midterm coverage, arguing this is the rare economic statistic every voter checks weekly and that Republicans in swing districts have no message for it. Democratic senators including Richard Blumenthal have pushed the administration for an accounting of the war's household cost, tying $4 gas directly to a discretionary conflict.

Right view

Fox News has covered the Democratic offensive as opportunism, noting the same members opposed domestic production expansions that would cushion a supply shock. The New York Post and WSJ energy writers argue the binding constraint is refining capacity and permitting, not the war, and that prices fall on their own once Hormuz traffic normalizes. Several conservative commentators have privately conceded, per reporting in The Dispatch, that the number is a genuine political problem regardless of whose fault it is.

Watch for

Crude's three-day slide should reach the pump in roughly two to four weeks if it holds — energy analysts generally cite that lag. Labor Day travel demand is the near-term test. The number to watch politically is whether the national average drops back under $4 before early voting begins in October; if it does not, expect it to headline every closing ad in a swing district.

Haiti's largest mass kidnapping in years follows an attack that killed 47

Facts

  • The United Nations says at least 47 people were killed and more than 50 kidnapped when armed men attacked Kenscoff, a community in the hills above Port-au-Prince, on Sunday.
  • It is believed to be the largest mass kidnapping in Haiti in recent years. Kenscoff had until recently been considered one of the safer areas near the capital.
  • In a video posted to social media, a gang leader threatened to kill the hostages if any of his fighters are harmed, addressing Haitian National Police director Vladimir Paraison directly.
  • Romain Le Cour of the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime described the situation as a major political and security test, noting the hostage threat is designed as a trap: inaction means failing to protect hostages, while a raid that goes wrong lets gangs blame the state.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian have framed this as the predictable result of an underfunded and understaffed international support mission, and have pressed on the gap between pledged and delivered support. Coverage has emphasized displacement figures and the collapse of basic services rather than a security-first framing.

Right view

The Washington Times and New York Post have led with the security and spillover angle — migration pressure on the region, and the argument that successive internationally backed missions have failed to produce a functioning Haitian state. National Review writers have used Haiti as a case against open-ended nation-building commitments.

Watch for

Whether Haitian police attempt a rescue, and how the UN Security Council responds on mission funding, which has repeatedly stalled. Regional analysts expect renewed migration pressure toward the Dominican Republic and the US within weeks if Kenscoff residents flee en masse — a dynamic with obvious US political resonance in an election year.
