The Big Stories Today

US and Iran trade heaviest blows since July as Hormuz mining resumes

Facts

  • US Central Command struck targets across southern Iran Monday and Tuesday, ending roughly a month in which the administration had leaned on sanctions rather than force. Al Jazeera reported at least 11 killed; local officials said one strike hit a house where a wedding was being held, killing five.
  • Trump described the strikes as retaliation for an Iranian attempt to lay sea mines in the Strait of Hormuz and for a missile attack on a US base in Jordan, and warned that a larger response was "waiting in the wings."
  • Iran fired missiles at US bases in Bahrain, Jordan and Iraq; the Jordan volley was intercepted. Drones launched toward Kuwait were shot down, and the UAE downed an Iranian drone over its waters Monday.
  • The war began with Operation Epic Fury on February 28 and is now in its seventh month. Two tankers were disabled by mines this week, though Energy Secretary Chris Wright said 17 million barrels transited Hormuz Monday — the highest daily volume since the war started.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian frame this as an undeclared war now far past the War Powers Resolution's 60-day clock, with the Senate having killed a joint resolution 47–49 in July after the House passed its own 214–208. Democracy Now! and Vox emphasize civilian casualties and the absence of a stated endgame; Just Security argues the renewed hostilities should restart the statutory clock rather than be treated as a continuation of an already-expired authorization.

Right view

Fox News Digital's live coverage casts the strikes as a proportionate answer to mining a waterway carrying a fifth of global oil, and quotes Trump saying the US now has "almost total control" of Hormuz. PJ Media treats the strikes as evidence of leverage rather than escalation. But the right is not unified — Reason has criticized the administration's shifting justifications, and Washington Times reporting notes fatigue with the war inside parts of Trump's own base.

Watch for

Energy analysts cited by CNBC and OilPrice see Brent holding a $5–10 war premium as long as mining continues; a confirmed closure of the strait, versus this week's harassment, is the difference between $95 and triple-digit crude. Watch tanker insurance rates and Kpler transit counts as the real-time gauge — only four crossings were logged Tuesday against a 10-day average well above that. A renewed war-powers push in Congress is possible but faces a near-certain veto.

Army Secretary Driscoll resigns after turf war with Hegseth — then Trump asks him to stay

Facts

  • Army Secretary Dan Driscoll submitted his resignation to Trump on Monday after months of conflict with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over Army modernization and the firing of senior generals.
  • The Washington Post reported Wednesday that Trump encouraged Driscoll to stay at least through the November midterms — an unusual public show of support for a subordinate in a dispute with the Pentagon chief.
  • Reporting from NPR and Axios points to a specific flashpoint: Hegseth allegedly removing four officers — two Black men and two women — from a promotion list after Driscoll declined to do so.
  • Driscoll is a close friend of Vice President JD Vance. His exit would be the latest in a long run of senior departures under Hegseth, now about 20 months into the job.

Left view

MSNBC/MS Now and PBS NewsHour present this as Hegseth consolidating control by purging officers who resist him, with the promotion-list episode treated as evidence that personnel decisions are being driven by ideology rather than merit. The Hill frames the outcome as Hegseth "imposing his will" on the Army.

Right view

Supportive Republicans quoted by Roll Call and MS Now, including Rep. Brian Mast, argue Hegseth is rightly refocusing the ranks on warfighting capability. But defense hawks on the right are visibly uneasy: Roll Call's reporting is headlined around hawks lamenting Pentagon "churn," and one House Republican described unresolved dissension at the top. Washington Times sourced the resignation to a toxic internal environment rather than a policy disagreement.

Watch for

Whether Driscoll actually stays. If he goes, Senate Armed Services Republicans have signaled appetite for oversight hearings on Pentagon personnel churn — an awkward proposition eight weeks before the midterms while the US is at war. The Vance connection also makes this a proxy fight over post-Trump control of the party's national-security wing.

Germany formally blames Russia for Leipzig airport drone attack, shuts a consulate

Facts

  • German authorities announced Tuesday that intelligence services attribute the August 4–5 drone incidents at Leipzig/Halle Airport to Russia. Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt said the explosives-laden drone found near a Ukrainian cargo aircraft matched Russian technology.
  • Germany ordered the Russian consulate in Bonn and the Russian House in Berlin closed, tightened entry controls for Russian nationals, and raised its national threat level from "general" to "high."
  • A second drone was struck by a Boeing 757 going around after the airport closed. No fatalities were reported in either incident.
  • Russia denies involvement; Ambassador Sergey Nechayev called the accusations absurd. The European Commission said further sanctions are being prepared; NATO, France, Poland and Italy backed Berlin.

Left view

The Guardian and NPR treat this as confirmation that Russian hybrid operations have crossed from sabotage into attacks that could have killed civilians, and press for the EU to move faster on the shadow-fleet crackdown. EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas's characterization of the incident as apparent state-sponsored terrorism is being widely quoted on the European center-left as justification for a harder sanctions package.

Right view

Washington Times argues the point of the campaign is to test NATO's thresholds cheaply and see whether Europe will define a red line, and is skeptical that consulate closures and sanctions lists constitute deterrence. Right-leaning European commentary questions why Berlin took four weeks to attribute an attack it now calls state-directed. There is also a strand on the American right that reads this as Europe demanding US backing while under-investing in its own defense.

Watch for

Whether Germany or any ally invokes NATO Article 4 consultations — nobody has yet, and the alliance's stated position that hybrid attacks could in principle trigger Article 5 has never been tested. Von der Leyen has promised a response package at the next foreign ministers' meeting; watch whether it names GRU-linked individuals directly. European defense and counter-drone equities have been bid up on the expectation of expanded airport-security spending.

The long bond is having its worst stretch since 2006, and Treasury's fix isn't working

Facts

  • Bloomberg reports the 30-year Treasury entered September on its worst run since 2006. The yield hit 5.34% in mid-August, the highest since 2007, and has closed above 5% on 55 days this year — more than any year since 2006. It eased to about 5.27% on September 2.
  • Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced on August 19 that the department would at least double scheduled long-dated buybacks from $2 billion to $4 billion, beginning September 9. Bloomberg's read is that the gains from that announcement have already been erased.
  • Drivers cited across coverage: large deficits, borrowing needs against roughly $40 trillion in federal debt, core inflation still above the 2% target, higher oil feeding into inflation expectations, and heavy corporate issuance tied to the AI capex boom.
  • The Fed held at 3.50%–3.75% on July 29 in a 9–3 vote, with three dissenters preferring a hike. The next FOMC decision is September 16. After Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech on August 28, markets briefly leaned toward a hike before drifting back — odds of a hold rose to about 56% in late August as labor-market data softened.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian tie the yield surge to the administration's own fiscal choices — deficit-financed tax cuts and tariff-driven price pressure — and note that Michigan Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed has begun campaigning explicitly on "Trump's mismanagement" of the bond market. The framing is that ordinary borrowers pay for it through mortgage and auto rates.

Right view

The Wall Street Journal's editorial page and Forbes commentary land in a similar place from the opposite direction: the problem is spending, not the Treasury's issuance calendar, and buybacks are a cosmetic intervention that cannot substitute for deficit reduction. Reason and fiscal-conservative outlets argue the bond market is doing the disciplining Congress won't. Notably, this is one of the rare stories where left and right agree on the diagnosis and differ only on the cure.

Watch for

The August jobs report Friday is the swing factor for September 16 — a weak print likely kills the hike case outright. Watch the September 9 buyback operation for whether it moves the long end at all; strategists quoted by Bloomberg and Fortune are openly skeptical. A 30-year sustained above 5.35% would put pressure on mortgage rates and on the equity rally that resumed Wednesday.

Xi in Cairo pitches a Middle East security order without Washington

Facts

  • Xi Jinping met Egyptian President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi in Cairo on Tuesday, his first visit to Egypt in a decade. He called on regional states to resist external interference and to consider reshaping the region's security architecture.
  • China has invested more than $10 billion in Egypt, including a business district east of Cairo and an electric rail line in the Nile Delta. Annual bilateral trade runs around $20 billion.
  • In August, China and Egypt held a multi-day joint air exercise across several Egyptian bases — the first combat exercise involving China's J-16 fighters alongside Egypt's French-built Rafales.
  • The trip lands while the US-Iran war continues to disrupt Gulf shipping and while Washington's regional partners are openly reassessing their strategic bets.

Left view

NPR and Al Jazeera present the visit as a direct consequence of the Iran war: American influence has been spent on a conflict with no resolution, and Beijing is offering economic depth and non-interference to a US treaty partner. The Guardian-adjacent framing stresses that Egypt's hedging is rational rather than disloyal.

Right view

The Washington Examiner and National Review-style commentary read this as a warning about the cost of distraction — China building military interoperability with an American security partner is treated as the real story, more than the trade numbers. The Free Press and hawkish outlets argue the answer is a faster, more decisive conclusion in Iran, not a smaller US footprint.

Watch for

Analysts cited by Bloomberg are watching for concrete deliverables: Suez Canal-linked infrastructure, yuan settlement in bilateral trade, and any follow-on defense procurement. Egypt taking Chinese hardware into a Rafale-and-F-16 fleet would be a significant interoperability signal. Also watch whether Gulf states schedule similar visits this autumn.

Two months out, the midterm map is moving toward Democrats — on the economy

Facts

  • The November 3 midterms will contest all 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats.
  • Trump's approval sits near a second-term low. Trackers put it around 38–39%; a July Pew survey had it at 34% approve / 64% disapprove, and a recent CNN poll found Democratic enthusiasm rising.
  • The Silver Bulletin generic-ballot average has Democrats at roughly D+6 to D+6.6, effectively unchanged since early August. Under neutral turnout assumptions that range implies roughly 20–30 House seat gains.
  • Pew's July survey found the economy is the dominant issue for voters heading into the cycle — ahead of foreign policy despite an ongoing war.

Left view

The Guardian and MSNBC read the numbers as the electorate pricing in $4 gas, a 5%+ long bond and an open-ended war simultaneously, and argue that Democratic candidates are for once running on cost-of-living from a position of advantage. The Conversation notes Trump's net approval is near a record low for the term.

Right view

The Dispatch and National Review caution that a D+6 national number distorts a House map where relatively few seats are genuinely competitive, and that generic-ballot leads of this size in September have historically compressed by November. Right-leaning analysts also point to Senate math that favors Republicans regardless of the national environment. Fox News coverage emphasizes that a Hormuz resolution and falling gas prices would reset the race quickly.

Watch for

Gas prices are the cleanest leading indicator here — the pump average has moved with the war all year, and every 25-cent move has tracked measurable shifts in economic sentiment. Also watch the September 16 Fed decision: a rate hike into a soft labor market would be an unusual pre-election headwind, and a hold would signal the Fed sees the jobs picture deteriorating.
