The Big Stories Today

Long bond hits a 19-year high, then Treasury steps in — and the Fed minutes float a hike

Facts

  • The 30-year Treasury yield topped 5.33% on Tuesday, its highest level since 2007 — a 19-year high — after clearing 5.31% on Monday, per CNBC and Bloomberg.
  • On Wednesday the Treasury Department said it would more than double the size of its buybacks of long-dated debt. The 30-year fell 9 basis points to 5.19% and the 10-year fell 5 basis points to 4.65%.
  • Minutes of the Fed's July meeting, released Wednesday, showed officials discussed raising rates if inflation fails to cool further. The July decision was a hold, with three FOMC dissents.
  • Stocks snapped a three-day losing streak on the yield relief: S&P 500 +0.43%, Dow +0.25%, Nasdaq +0.40%.
  • Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, in office since May, has suggested high long-term yields are doing some of the Fed's inflation-fighting work, arguably reducing the need for hikes.

Left view

Axios reads the yield move as the bond market's verdict on fiscal policy — persistent deficits plus tariff-driven price pressure mean investors are demanding more to hold long paper, and debt service is becoming the binding constraint on everything else. NPR and other outlets on this side emphasize that the pain shows up in mortgages and consumer credit, not in Washington.

Right view

The Wall Street Journal editorial page and outlets like The Dispatch put the blame squarely on issuance and spending rather than tariffs: the government is flooding the market with supply, and buybacks are a liquidity patch, not a fix. Some on this side add that Warsh's arrival has usefully broken the assumption that the Fed will always cut into weakness.

Watch for

Whether September's FOMC meeting becomes live for a hike — economists note it is the last meeting before late October, uncomfortably close to the November midterms for a rate move. Also watch how much buyback capacity Treasury actually has, the read-through to 30-year mortgage rates, and whether the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow (which slipped to 4.3% for Q2 after soft retail sales) keeps deteriorating.

Three more Trump-endorsed candidates lose, making it his roughest primary cycle since 2022

Facts

  • Rep. Cory Mills (R-FL-7), who is under a Justice Department investigation and multiple House ethics inquiries, lost his primary to challenger Ryan Elijah by roughly 13 points on Tuesday.
  • Catalina Lauf, a former Commerce Department adviser who picked up a late Trump endorsement, lost the crowded FL-19 primary. Megan Degenfelder, Trump's pick for Wyoming governor, lost by nearly 16 points.
  • That brings Trump-endorsed primary losses to 10 this cycle — six of them in August alone, including Mike Lindell in Minnesota, Rep. Andy Ogles in Tennessee and Amir Hassan in Michigan.
  • Ballotpedia still puts Trump's 2026 primary endorsement record at about 96%, since he has endorsed in hundreds of races.
  • Trump said after the result that he had told Mills to drop out of the race.

Left view

CNN and MSNBC frame this as erosion of the endorsement's coercive power: Trump has now lost more primaries this year than in 2018, 2020 and 2024 combined, and unlike 2022 — when half the losses came from a single anti-Trump backlash in Georgia — the defeats are scattered across states. The New Republic focuses on Trump distancing himself from Mills after the fact.

Right view

The New York Post and Washington Examiner treat this as a candidate-quality story rather than a Trump story: Mills was carrying a DOJ investigation into his primary, and voters priced that in. They point to the 96% overall record and argue a handful of losses in a cycle with hundreds of endorsements is statistical noise, not a referendum.

Watch for

Whether Trump slows his endorsement pace through the remaining primaries — one more loss ties his 2022 record. Analysts are also watching the Alaska Senate race, where candidates are still competing for the final two spots, and whether wobbly Trump leverage over House Republicans complicates the SAVE America Act whip count in the Senate.

US sanctions the ICC's sitting president, escalating a campaign to defang the court

Facts

  • On Aug. 18 the Treasury Department added ICC President Tomoko Akane, a Japanese national who has led the court since 2024, and senior trial lawyer Abdoulaye Seye of Senegal to its Specially Designated Nationals list.
  • The designations were made under Executive Order 14203, signed in February 2025, which has now been used against 10 ICC officials including former Chief Prosecutor Karim Khan and eight judges.
  • The listings freeze any US assets and bar Americans from transacting with the two officials.
  • Secretary of State Marco Rubio described the court as corrupt and politicized and accused it of illegitimate judicial overreach over non-member states. The ICC said it strongly rejects the sanctions and called them an attack on judicial independence.
  • UN Secretary-General António Guterres voiced serious concern; Germany, the Netherlands and Japan condemned the move.

Left view

The Guardian, NBC News and Time cast this as the United States sanctioning individual judges for the content of their rulings — a tool built for terrorists and traffickers now aimed at a court's presiding officer. They note the practical effect is to make it harder for the ICC to function at all, which is what critics say is the point, and that Japan's objection is notable given Akane's nationality.

Right view

Fox News and allied outlets amplify Rubio's framing: the ICC has no jurisdiction over nationals of states that never ratified the Rome Statute, and its warrants against Israeli officials amount to lawfare. Supporters also point to the ethics scandal that engulfed former prosecutor Khan as evidence the institution's credibility problems are self-inflicted. European conservatives, including Hungary's Viktor Orbán and Czech PM Petr Fiala, have made similar arguments.

Watch for

Whether the EU invokes its blocking statute to shield European banks and firms dealing with the court, whether Tokyo escalates beyond a statement, and whether more judges get designated. Legal observers also expect knock-on effects for ICC cases unrelated to Israel — including Ukraine and Sudan — because staff and contractors become skittish about exposure to US sanctions.

Iran deadline lapses: Trump demands surrender and threatens to bomb Oman over a Hormuz deal

Facts

  • The 60-day memorandum of understanding between Washington and Tehran expired Monday without a comprehensive peace agreement. Iran ruled out an extension.
  • Trump publicly called on Iran to raise a white flag of surrender and said the US would bomb Oman if Muscat got in the way of reopening the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Iranian officials say they have reached an arrangement with Oman on a transit corridor for commercial shipping through the strait. The MOU Trump signed had itself tasked Oman with brokering that dialogue.
  • The war is in its sixth month. Shipping through Hormuz remains restricted. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth was questioned by Senate appropriators in July over the conflict's $37.5 billion cost.
  • An AP-NORC poll conducted July 23–27 found 64% of Americans say the war has not been worth fighting. Senate war-powers resolutions have failed twice in two weeks, most recently 49-50.

Left view

Al Jazeera, MSNBC and CNN's Middle East desk argue that threatening the one mediating ally still talking to both sides destroys the only functioning diplomatic channel, and that Trump is threatening Oman for doing exactly what his own signed memorandum asked it to do. Commentary on this side notes Omani public anger and that European allies see the threat as evidence US security guarantees are transactional.

Right view

Fox News reports that the threat has rattled Republicans, with several breaking ranks. Hawks on the right defend the pressure campaign as the only thing that has moved Tehran, and argue a corridor negotiated by Iran and Oman without Washington would ratify Iranian control over the strait. Libertarian-leaning outlets like Reason and Senator Rand Paul take the opposite view, calling six months of undeclared war a constitutional failure.

Watch for

Crude is the transmission channel: WTI has been running near six-month highs and every escalation adds a few dollars. Analysts expect pump prices to stay elevated into Labor Day, though seasonal demand normally eases after. Also watch whether Oman formalizes the corridor over US objections, and whether a third war-powers vote picks up defectors as midterm season starts.

Trump wants the Senate to skip its break over the SAVE America Act. Thune wants a vote count.

Facts

  • Trump is pressing Majority Leader John Thune to cancel the August recess until the Senate passes the SAVE America Act, a bill requiring documentary proof of citizenship to register to vote.
  • Thune's public response was to ask supporters to show him a path to passage. Republicans hold 53 seats and the bill needs 60 to break a filibuster.
  • The Senate has already voted on the measure five times and spent weeks of floor time on it. Thune says he lacks the votes both to pass it and to change the rules around it.
  • Trump has said the bill would guarantee the midterms and has claimed election cheating is rampant, a claim election officials and courts have repeatedly rejected.
  • Separately, the administration in July removed the Election Assistance Commission's two Democratic commissioners and pushed its Republican commissioner to resign.

Left view

NPR and the Center for American Progress argue documentary proof-of-citizenship rules disenfranchise eligible voters who lack easy access to a passport or birth certificate — disproportionately married women whose names have changed, rural voters and the elderly — and that the EAC purge is part of a pattern of executive interference ahead of November.

Right view

A Washington Examiner op-ed makes the populist case: proof of citizenship polls at roughly 80% support, so senators should stay in Washington rather than leave a popular bill on the floor. Fox News opinion contributors, including House members threatening to block Senate business, frame the filibuster as the only thing standing between the bill and law.

Watch for

Whether Thune schedules a sixth vote as a show of effort, whether filibuster carve-out talk resurfaces (Thune has consistently ruled it out), and whether Trump's slipping primary leverage changes any Republican calculations. Litigation over the EAC firings is the other track to watch, since it determines who certifies voting-system standards before November.

Ukraine hits Russian oil infrastructure again as Moscow's missile tempo climbs

Facts

  • Ukrainian drones struck an oil refinery in Russia's Republic of Tatarstan and an oil terminal in Krasnodar Krai overnight into Aug. 20, extending a months-long campaign against Russian energy infrastructure.
  • A Russian drone strike on police headquarters in Zhytomyr killed two people and injured at least 40.
  • July was the deadliest month of the war since May 2022 by Ukrainian counts, with Russia firing 376 missiles — up from 288 in March and 192 in July 2025.
  • The White House redirected some air-defense supplies to Middle East allies following US and Israeli strikes on Iran, and moved to rebuild its own strategic stockpile.

Left view

The Guardian and Al Jazeera focus on the civilian toll and the gap between Washington's stated support and actual deliveries, arguing the Iran war has quietly become Ukraine's biggest supply problem. They characterize Russia's escalation as an attempt to manufacture the appearance of momentum rather than a real battlefield breakthrough.

Right view

National Review and The Dispatch argue the refinery strikes are the most cost-effective leverage Kyiv has and should be encouraged, not restrained — hitting Russian export revenue does more than another aid tranche. They also treat the diverted interceptors as evidence the US industrial base cannot sustain two theaters, and press for expanded production rather than choosing between allies.

Watch for

Russian refining outages tighten global diesel and jet fuel, which compounds the crude squeeze already coming from Hormuz — that combination is what analysts say could push US pump prices higher into the fall. Also watch European gas storage levels ahead of winter and whether interceptor deliveries to Kyiv resume once Middle East demand plateaus.
