The Big Stories Today

Trump says a “big decision” is coming on resuming major strikes on Iran

Facts

  • In an interview published Thursday, Trump said he is weighing whether to return to large-scale attacks on Iran, framing it as a choice between annihilating the regime and holding off.
  • The comment follows a US strike on Iranian targets Tuesday and Iranian missile and drone retaliation across the region — the sharpest exchange in weeks of intermittent fighting that began in late February.
  • He is scheduled to meet the leaders of Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait and Oman on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly in New York next week, with the war expected to dominate.
  • Trump has not said whether he will decide before the Nov. 3 midterms or after.
  • Traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains largely restricted; the IEA has called it the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market.

Left view

NPR and The Hill's opinion pages frame this as an open-ended war with no defined objective and no congressional authorization, now in its seventh month, with escalation decisions being weighed against an election calendar rather than a strategy. The Middle East Council's analysis, widely cited on the left, argues the war has crowded out domestic priorities and that each round of strikes has produced retaliation rather than deterrence.

Right view

Breitbart and other pro-escalation outlets present the decision as long-overdue resolve — that half-measures have let Tehran keep Hormuz closed and that finishing the job restores deterrence. But the right is split: National Review has argued since July that public opposition is real and that Trump's ambitions are being checked by voters, and restraint-minded conservatives at The Dispatch and Reason warn that an open-ended regional war is exactly what the 2016 and 2024 coalitions voted against.

Watch for

Energy analysts expect crude to stay in a $95–$115 Brent band while Hormuz stays constricted, with any strike announcement worth $5–10/bbl immediately. The EIA has already raised its price outlook on the Hormuz disruption. Domestically, the pass-through is gasoline: every $10/bbl is roughly 25 cents at the pump within three weeks, which lands on voters right before the midterms — the reason several analysts think the decision slips past November.

Trump calls the EU's “associate member” offer to Canada a hostile act, threatens heavy tariffs

Facts

  • European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen told the European Parliament in Strasbourg on Wednesday that she wants Canada to become the EU's first “associate member” — an entirely new status whose details have not been defined.
  • She proposed cooperation spanning manufacturing, AI, critical raw materials, energy and defense, describing it as moving beyond a trading relationship.
  • Prime Minister Mark Carney had said earlier in the week that Canada wants a “unique alliance” with the EU but not membership.
  • Trump responded Thursday by calling the idea laughable, warning it could be treated as a hostile act, and threatening very heavy tariffs or a halt to trade with the bloc.
  • Context: US tariffs on Canadian steel, aluminum, lumber and autos, matching 50% Canadian retaliation in August, and collapsed bilateral talks.

Left view

The Guardian and NPR treat this as the predictable cost of coercive trade policy: tariffs plus “51st state” taunting pushed a treaty ally toward Brussels, and the threat to punish the EU for Canada's own diplomacy confirms the pattern. Al Jazeera and European outlets emphasize von der Leyen's framing of a fracturing rules-based order.

Right view

Fox News and the NY Post carry the administration's line that Ottawa has been a poor trade partner and that Brussels is using Canada as leverage in its own tariff fight. WSJ's editorial page and The Dispatch are less sympathetic to the tariff threat — their long-running position is that punishing allies over trade drives them into other blocs, which is what just happened.

Watch for

Whether the associate-member idea gets substance at the EU–Canada summit track or stays rhetorical — analysts note the EU treaties have no such category, so it would require new legal machinery and unanimity. Near term, watch for a formal Section 232 or IEEPA threat against EU goods; European equities and the euro have so far treated the threat as negotiating posture rather than policy.

State Department clears a $24.3 billion F-35 sale to Saudi Arabia

Facts

  • A congressional notification issued Thursday approves a potential sale of 48 F-35A fighters and 49 Pratt & Whitney F135 engines, plus training, equipment and logistics support, valued at up to $24.3 billion.
  • The State Department says the sale would strengthen Saudi deterrence and homeland defense and improve interoperability with US and allied forces.
  • Congress has 30 days to object. The package sits alongside Riyadh's designation as a major non-NATO ally and a separate civil nuclear agreement.
  • Israel is currently the only Middle East operator of the F-35; the Israeli Air Force submitted a formal position paper warning its air superiority could erode.
  • US law since 2008 requires the government to weigh the effect of major regional arms sales on Israel's qualitative military edge.

Left view

Al Jazeera and progressive foreign-policy writers link the sale to Riyadh's human rights record and to the absence of any Saudi commitment on Palestinian statehood in return. Rep. Raja Krishnamoorthi (D-Ill.), on the House Intelligence Committee, went at it from the technology angle, saying it should not proceed while the intelligence community warns the jet's most sensitive technology could end up within reach of Beijing.

Right view

Fox News and Washington Examiner coverage casts it as locking Saudi Arabia into the American defense ecosystem instead of the Chinese one, and as the reward for a partner that has flown alongside US aircraft against Iran-aligned militias. Pro-Israel conservatives are the dissenters: Israel Hayom's framing is that the deal could end Israel's regional air edge, and some Republicans want the downgraded-capability assurances in writing before the 30 days run.

Watch for

A classified DIA briefing to Congress on technology-transfer risk is the near-term tell; analysts at Breaking Defense note that a resolution of disapproval has never successfully blocked a major sale but can force conditions. Lockheed Martin is the obvious beneficiary — the order would extend F-35 production well into the 2030s. Delivery realistically begins no earlier than the early 2030s, so the immediate effect is diplomatic, not military.

Stocks rally the day after the Fed's first rate hike since 2023

Facts

  • The FOMC voted unanimously Wednesday to raise the federal funds target range a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00%, the first increase since 2023, citing inflation driven largely by energy.
  • Sixteen of 18 participants penciled in another hike this year; futures put October at roughly a coin flip and December at about 88%.
  • Markets fell modestly on the decision itself, then reversed hard Thursday: S&P 500 +1.14% to 7,637, Nasdaq +1.69% to 26,418, Dow +0.62% to 51,779, helped by a pullback in crude.
  • Core PCE has run from 3.0% in December 2025 to 3.3% in July 2026. August CPI, released Sept. 11, showed headline at 3.35% year over year with core at 2.45%.
  • Trump, who appointed Chair Kevin Warsh in January, demanded on Truth Social that rates be cut immediately — but has conspicuously not attacked Warsh by name.

Left view

Common Dreams and progressive economists argue working families are paying twice — once for tariff- and war-driven price increases, again for the borrowing costs imposed to contain them. NPR's coverage stresses the “low-hire, low-fire” labor market, where a hike risks stalling job growth without touching the supply-side source of the inflation.

Right view

The Wall Street Journal editorial board and Washington Times treat the unanimous vote as proof the Fed is doing its job and a rebuke to political pressure. The Washington Examiner notes the awkwardness for the White House: Trump picked Warsh precisely to get cuts. National Review's monetary writers have long argued credibility is worth more than a quarter point of stimulus before an election.

Watch for

The two-year yield at 4.74% and the ten-year at 5.02% leave the curve nearly flat — a signal bond desks read as the market pricing hikes now and a slowdown later. Rate-sensitive sectors (housing, regional banks, small caps) are the stress points; the Russell 2000 fell on the decision. The September CPI release on Oct. 14 lands two weeks before the October meeting and will effectively settle that coin flip. Also watch whether Trump escalates from criticizing the committee to challenging Fed independence directly — CNBC flagged that as the live risk.

Supreme Court leaves Trump's mail-ballot rule blocked for the midterms

Facts

  • On Monday the Court declined, in an unsigned order, to let the Postal Service implement parts of a new mail-in voting rule ahead of the Nov. 3 elections.
  • The rule would have required states to submit lists of eligible voters to USPS for verification before ballots were mailed, and imposed envelope design requirements.
  • The Court said the administration is unlikely to succeed on the merits of its challenge to a Boston federal judge's nationwide preliminary injunction.
  • Justice Kavanaugh concurred, writing that the rule may well fall within USPS authority but that applying it in 2026 would be arbitrary and capricious because officials lack time to implement it.
  • Separately, the Missouri Supreme Court ruled a new Republican-drawn congressional map cannot be used this cycle, and the House voted down a constitutional amendment to fix the Supreme Court at nine seats.

Left view

NPR and Votebeat frame it as a straightforward win for ballot access: mail voting in November will work the way it has, and the executive branch does not get to rewrite election mechanics weeks before an election. The Guardian and MSNBC tie it to the broader pattern of litigation over 2026 voting rules.

Right view

Fox News and the NY Post emphasize Kavanaugh's concurrence — that the Court blocked the rule on timing, not on authority, leaving the underlying claim that USPS can police ballot handling alive for 2028. The Washington Examiner notes the administration lost on the calendar rather than the principle.

Watch for

The merits case continues in the First Circuit, so a 2027 ruling on USPS authority is the real stake. Election lawyers on both sides expect a surge of post-election litigation regardless. Forecasters currently have Democrats at about 59% to take the Senate, their high for the cycle, with the generic ballot running D+8 (NYT/Siena) to D+12 (YouGov/Economist); Republicans hold 220–215 in the House and Democrats need three seats.

Saudi Arabia floats a two-week truce to the Houthis through Oman

Facts

  • Riyadh has asked Oman to carry a proposal to Sanaa for a two-week ceasefire, intended to open talks on humanitarian demands and then a comprehensive agreement.
  • The proposal follows Saudi consultations with the US, Egypt, Pakistan and Oman.
  • US representatives met senior Houthi officials at the US embassy in Muscat over the weekend, with Omani facilitation.
  • Houthi forces have continued attacks on Saudi territory, and a planned Gulf–Iran meeting on a Strait of Hormuz proposal was postponed on Sept. 13.
  • Reporting on whether the Houthis will accept is contradictory; no acceptance has been confirmed.

Left view

The Guardian and Al Jazeera treat any move from the military channel to the diplomatic one as overdue, stressing Yemen's humanitarian toll and arguing the blockade dynamics cannot be resolved by force. Some on the left note the awkward sequencing — Washington brokering Houthi talks the same week it clears a $24 billion arms package for the other side of that war.

Right view

The Jerusalem Post and Washington Examiner read the outreach as a sign the Houthis are being squeezed, and warn that past truces let them rearm. National Review's regional writers argue a truce that leaves Bab al-Mandab under Houthi control just ratifies the gains they made in early September.

Watch for

Shipping insurers are the practical barometer: war-risk premiums for Red Sea transits are what would actually fall if a truce holds. A reopening of Bab al-Mandab would matter to container rates and European supply chains more than to crude, which is bottlenecked at Hormuz. Analysts caution that the Strait of Hormuz track and the Yemen track are now being negotiated separately, and progress on one does not imply the other.
