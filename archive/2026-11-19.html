Anthropic prices the biggest IPO ever at $83 and debuts on Nasdaq today; tech futures jump 2%

Facts

  • Anthropic priced Wednesday night at $83 a share, above its $70–78 marketed range. It upsized the deal to 980 million shares, about 956 million of them sold by the company (AP).
  • The deal raises about $81B, the largest IPO on record and ahead of SpaceX's roughly $75B sale in June. It values Anthropic at about $1.5T (AP, Fortune). Seven tech companies, including Google and Nvidia, have separately agreed to buy $26B of non-voting shares at the IPO price (filings via StockTitan).
  • Shares trade on Nasdaq as "ANT" starting today. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley lead the deal. On Nasdaq the first trade waits for the lead bank's go-ahead, and SpaceX's opened more than two hours after the bell (StockTitan).
  • As of 5:48 a.m. ET, Nasdaq-100 futures were up 2.15%, S&P futures 1.25% and Dow futures 0.67%. Investors Amazon and Alphabet rose 2.2% and 3.9% premarket, and chip stocks bounced. Nvidia was up 1.8% after five straight losses, including a 2.9% drop Wednesday to $150.96 that erased its post-earnings pop (Reuters via Yahoo Finance).

Left view

Left-leaning opinion coverage was thin overnight. Wire coverage (AP) puts the risks up front: a possible regulatory crackdown when Democrats take Congress in January, growing public hostility to AI, bubble worries, and the leftover uncertainty from the Nvidia/Huang scandal.

Right view

The Free Press presents the IPO as exposing a "great AI divide." Bankers are debating whether AI is a bubble, while tech insiders believe superintelligence is close. The outlet notes the company is selling a technology its own CEO warns could be catastrophic. Its columnist Joe Nocera, a veteran of financial-crisis coverage, calls the Nvidia scandal more bad news for an industry carrying heavy debt.

Watch for

StoneX's Fiona Cincotta calls a strong debut the "tonic" a volatile market needs after weeks of selling in chip and cloud stocks. A weak or choppy open would revive bubble talk just as the Fed signals more hikes. After today, watch the banks' option to buy extra shares, the closing of the $26B side sale, and the Big Tech investors whose stakes now carry a public market price.

Trump, Xi and Putin hold the first three-way summit and agree to talk about nuclear risk, but announce no breakthroughs

Facts

  • The three leaders met for more than an hour at Kylin Villa in Shenzhen on the sidelines of APEC. The BBC put it at about 90 minutes. Their joint statement commits them to a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing proliferation (AP, BBC via NewsCord).
  • Trump said the three powers are "uniquely positioned" to cut arsenals and that "we are really making history." Xi called for nuclear weapons to be banned and destroyed (CCTV). Putin pointed to shared responsibility as WWII victors and "many common interests," including Ukraine (AP via Korea Herald).
  • New START expired in February. China has about 600 warheads, compared with more than 5,000 each for the U.S. and Russia, and Beijing says arms control should stay a U.S.–Russia matter (AP, NBC).
  • Trump told the other two leaders the U.S. "will not let Iran have a nuclear weapon," pressed for oil to keep flowing through Hormuz, and ruled out new sanctions on Russia. He also said the three would discuss shielding their sovereignty from the International Criminal Court (Iran International, AP). Ukraine and its European allies were not at the table.
  • Next steps: Trump and Xi meet again at the G20 in Florida in December, and the U.S.–China trade truce expires Jan. 10. U.S. officials say Beijing hasn't fully met its farm-purchase and rare-earth commitments (AP).

Left view

In the Philadelphia Inquirer, Trudy Rubin argues Trump is trading U.S. security for flattery and a photo op. She warns that Moscow wants a "new Yalta" that puts Ukraine under its influence and ties the summit to Trump's softness on the Nvidia–China chip allegations. CNN's coverage questions why Trump treats Russia and China as friends. NPR says Putin came to push for an end to the Ukraine war on Moscow's terms.

Right view

Fox News centers Trump's nonproliferation push and his own framing that "they both like Trump," along with his claim that Obama-era energy policy pushed Russia and China together. In National Review, Jianli Yang cautions that great-power bargaining can reward coercion without producing stability. He argues U.S. leadership still depends on credible deterrence and allies who can trust American commitments.

Watch for

Daryl Kimball of the Arms Control Association says the meeting can at most start a process, and analysts quoted by DW expect each leader to restate positions rather than offer solutions. Market-relevant items are whether the Jan. 10 trade truce gets extended, rare-earth and soybean compliance, and any chip-control language before the Florida G20. Kyiv and European capitals will react to the Ukraine discussion and the ICC language.

Iran war: Trump calls the Islamabad framework a "waste of time" as CENTCOM tightens the blockade after strikes on Kuwait and Bahrain

Facts

  • On Wednesday, Iran fired on Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait and at targets in Bahrain. It followed a U.S. strike Tuesday on an IRGC missile site on Qeshm Island. Kuwait and Bahrain say they intercepted the drones (AP/Washington Post, Al Jazeera).
  • In a Fox News interview, Trump called the preliminary Islamabad memorandum with Iran a "waste of time." He said he wants a lasting deal, will end the war "soon," and doesn't need Chinese or Russian help (Iran International).
  • CENTCOM warned that ships taking cargo at sea from vessels loaded in Iran could be seized or face military force. A third U.S. carrier is now in the region. Israel Hayom, citing a senior diplomat, reports Trump is weighing more strikes on nuclear and missile sites (Al Jazeera, Iran International).
  • Iran's inflation hit 88.7% year over year in October. Treasury Secretary Bessent predicts the blockade will shrink Iran's economy 25% by March 2027. Iran's central bank chief says it has $30B in reserves for essential imports and rejects the forecast (Al Jazeera).

Left view

Democrats in Congress call it an "illegal and unauthorized war" and are tying it to the funding deadline (see shutdown story; Punchbowl). Al Jazeera's reporting from Tehran shows real economic strain but no sign of imminent collapse. A Tehran economist it quotes says Bessent's comparison to the fall of the Soviet Union may not hold.

Right view

On Fox News, Trump calls the naval blockade highly effective and says Iranian officials want a deal. Earlier this week the Washington Examiner editorial board argued that Iran's lashing out shows the pressure is working and gives Washington leverage.

Watch for

The tit-for-tat cycle (Qeshm strike, then Gulf attacks) raises the risk of miscalculation, and a U.S. casualty would be the trigger for a broader campaign. Oil isn't pricing escalation: WTI fell 3.3% Wednesday to about $81.85. The Dec. 11 funding fight is now Congress's main lever on the war.

A Dec. 11 shutdown is looking more likely as Democrats split over cutting off Iran war funding

Facts

  • Government funding runs out Dec. 11. Congress leaves for Thanksgiving and returns Nov. 30. Punchbowl reports the four congressional leaders have no visible path to a deal (Punchbowl).
  • Speaker Johnson and Majority Leader Thune reject any bill that limits Trump's war powers. Thune calls Iran restrictions "not a realistic ask." Jeffries and Schumer say they won't back funding without limits. Thune needs 60 votes and probably can't find 8–9 Democrats for a clean stopgap (Punchbowl).
  • Democrats are divided. Sen. Chris Van Hollen, who is challenging Schumer for the leadership, wants funds cut off now. Sen. Jack Reed wants to avoid a shutdown. Rep. Rosa DeLauro prefers a short stopgap into February, when Democrats will control both chambers. Schumer has floated a temporary extension with war-spending limits (Punchbowl; NYT via PJ Media).

Left view

MS NOW (MSNBC) and The Hill frame the midterm wave as a mandate to end the war and the deadline as Democrats' best leverage before January. They also report a real split over whether a shutdown is worth it. Van Hollen argues Trump would own any shutdown.

Right view

At PJ Media, Rick Moran argues a shutdown would strengthen Tehran and furlough federal workers right before Christmas, all for limits Trump would veto, and Democrats can't reach 67 votes to override. Thune says nobody wins in a shutdown. Punchbowl notes some Republicans think a fight could help a demoralized GOP regroup.

Watch for

The likeliest paths are a short stopgap or a brief shutdown. The Fed meets Dec. 8–9, and a lapse would delay federal data just as policymakers weigh a hike. The Van Hollen–Schumer leadership race makes compromise harder for Senate Democrats.

  • A "vast majority" worry inflation could persist even if gas prices fall, as price pressure spreads beyond energy and tariffs. The Fed raised rates in September for the first time in three years (AP).

Watch for

The Fed meets Dec. 8–9, and the debate is now 25 vs. 50 basis points rather than whether to hike. A stronger dollar weighs on multinationals' overseas earnings, one reason the Dow fell 561 points Wednesday. A possible shutdown could hold back the data the Fed relies on.
