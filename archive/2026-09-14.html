The Big Stories Today

Hormuz reopening talks collapse before they start

Facts

  • Iranian and Gulf diplomats were due to meet in Salalah, Oman today to discuss an Omani plan for restoring regular tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. Oman's foreign ministry said Sunday the meeting is postponed indefinitely, giving no new date.
  • Hormuz has been largely closed to commercial shipping since late February, when Iran blocked the waterway following US and Israeli strikes. Transits are running at their lowest level since May.
  • The Omani framework reportedly included voluntary payments to Tehran framed as navigation-safety and environmental fees — the sticking point for several Gulf capitals.
  • The US was not a party to the talks. CNN reports administration officials have told Gulf counterparts privately they want any future US–Iran channel focused on the nuclear file, not on the strait.
  • Brent settled near $104 Friday; WTI was around $102 early Monday, up roughly 21% in a month and about 50% since the war began.

Left view

NPR and Al Jazeera coverage frames the postponement as the predictable cost of a strategy with no diplomatic exit: seven months of strikes and tanker interdictions have not reopened the strait, and Washington's refusal to join the one negotiation actually aimed at reopening it undercuts the regional partners it says it is defending. The Guardian's line through this conflict has been that the economic damage lands on consumers worldwide while the stated objective — Iran's nuclear program — remains unresolved.

Right view

Fox News and Fox Business emphasize that the pressure campaign is working on Tehran's balance sheet — CENTCOM strikes near Kharg Island and Jask have crippled Iranian export revenue — and that paying Iran transit fees would reward the blockade. Trump has argued new overland pipeline routes across the region, including through Syria, will make the strait less decisive over time. The Dispatch and other conservative outlets have been less sanguine, questioning whether a squeeze with no defined endpoint is a strategy or a stalemate.

Watch for

Energy analysts quoted by Reuters and CNBC see Brent holding a $95–$115 range while the strait stays shut, with spikes on any new attack. Axios has flagged that Trump's claim that Hormuz no longer matters much will be tested in the next few weeks by tanker-tracking data. Watch Gulf sovereign wealth flows, marine insurance rates (already the practical constraint on transits), and whether Oman can reconvene the meeting before the Fed's decision Wednesday hardens the inflation picture.

The Fed meets tomorrow with a rate hike on the table

Facts

  • The FOMC convenes September 15–16. Futures put the odds of a 25-basis-point hike between roughly 66% and 83% depending on the day and the data print; the odds were near 49% a week ago.
  • The current target range is 3.50%–3.75%. The Fed has neither cut nor hiked in 2026. A hike would be the first since 2023.
  • Chair Kevin Warsh said at Jackson Hole that inflation remains above the 2% target and that prices are the committee's predominant focus. He has not ruled out a hike.
  • Economists are split with the market. In Bloomberg's September survey, only 13 of 48 respondents expected an increase this month; the majority saw a hold.
  • CNBC reported that the president, vice president, Treasury secretary and senior economic advisers have all publicly urged the Fed not to raise rates. Warsh says the pressure has had no effect on his decisions.

Left view

NPR and Vox-style commentary makes two points at once: the White House campaign against Warsh is a direct assault on central-bank independence and would be treated as a scandal under a Democratic administration — and separately, that the hawks may be wrong on the merits, because rate increases are a poor tool against a supply shock caused by a war the administration started. Raising borrowing costs, on this reading, taxes households twice.

Right view

The Wall Street Journal editorial board's long-running position is that credibility is the Fed's only real asset and that flinching under political pressure would cost more than a quarter point ever could. National Review and Reason writers have made the same argument from the other direction — that the honest fix is ending the war premium and deregulating supply, not asking the Fed to accommodate inflation the executive branch manufactured.

Watch for

Barclays economists told Bloomberg the AI capex boom is acting as a shock absorber, blunting the usual channel by which higher rates slow housing and construction — which means a hike may buy less disinflation than the model says. Futures already price roughly 4.1% by December and about 4.6% by September 2027. A surprise hold would likely steepen the curve and weaken the dollar; a hike lands seven weeks before the midterms and will be read politically no matter how it is explained.

Midterm season opens with gas prices as the central argument

Facts

  • Labor Day marked the unofficial start of the general election. All 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats are up on November 3.
  • Trump told reporters last week that oil and gas prices will fall "right after" the midterms and predicted the Iran war would end soon afterward. CNBC reported the remarks the same day crude topped $100.
  • US News and CNN both noted the tension with internal planning: the White House has separately discussed scenarios in which the conflict runs past January 2029.
  • Generic-ballot averages have Democrats up roughly 5 to 7.6 points depending on the model and likely-voter screen. Trump's approval sits in the high 30s.
  • Democrats need a net gain of three seats for the House. Forecasters currently favor them there; the Senate map remains unsettled.
  • At the GOP midterm convention in Dallas, Trump said handing Democrats Congress would set the country back a decade.

Left view

The Washington Post and NPR treat the "prices fall after the election" line as the story: it concedes that the pain is policy-driven and timing-dependent, which is the opposite of the usual argument that presidents don't control gas prices. MSNBC and The Atlantic have argued that a $4.31 national average and a $6.20 diesel record are the kind of kitchen-table numbers that override every other issue in a midterm.

Right view

The Washington Times and Fox News frame the fall campaign around a choice rather than a price: confronting Iran was a decision Trump made and Democrats would not have, and voters who want the nuclear program stopped should accept the interim cost. National Review and The Free Press have been more willing to say the quiet part — that a high-30s approval and $4-plus gas is a difficult combination, and that generic-ballot leads of this size have historically translated into real House losses.

Watch for

Historically a D+5.4 generic ballot maps to a net gain of 15–30 House seats. The variables to track: whether pump prices peak before early voting begins in October, whether the Fed's decision this week reads as a recession signal, and whether any Hormuz breakthrough materializes in time to move the numbers. Political scientists caution that gas prices affect vote choice with a lag of several weeks, which cuts against the president's timeline.

Indonesian ferry capsizes in the Java Sea; 129 still missing

Facts

  • The Indonesian-flagged ferry Virgo Transport 8 sank early Sunday in the Java Sea while sailing from Surabaya, East Java, to Banjarmasin, South Kalimantan.
  • The vessel carried 243 people and 89 vehicles. The captain's last radio contact, around 2 a.m. local time, reported the ship listing.
  • As of Monday: six confirmed dead, 107 rescued, 129 to 130 unaccounted for.
  • Indonesia's search agency has deployed 17 ships and aircraft and roughly 622 personnel from the military, police and other agencies. Rough seas and high waves preceded the sinking.

Left view

The Guardian and NPR situate this in a pattern rather than an accident: Indonesia's inter-island ferry network is chronically overloaded and under-inspected, and repeated disasters — Bali in 2025, others before it — have produced inquiries without enforcement. The framing is regulatory failure and the cost of thin maritime safety budgets in an archipelago of 17,000 islands.

Right view

Coverage in right-leaning US outlets is thin on this story, which is itself worth noting. Where it appears, the emphasis is on the rescue operation and on questions of operator liability and manifest accuracy rather than on systemic regulation. Wire services — AP, Reuters, ABC — are carrying most of the reporting.

Watch for

Survival odds fall sharply after 48 hours in open water; the search will likely shift from rescue to recovery within days. Expect scrutiny of whether the actual passenger count exceeded the manifest — a recurring finding in Indonesian ferry inquiries — and of why the vessel sailed into forecast heavy weather. Jakarta has previously responded to disasters of this scale with temporary route suspensions.

OpenAI rules out a 2026 IPO as AI leaders call for a slowdown

Facts

  • Sam Altman told Fortune in an interview published Saturday that OpenAI will not go public this year, calling an IPO now an ill-advised move given the safety work outstanding. A listing had been discussed for 2026 at a potential trillion-dollar valuation; the timeline now slips toward 2027.
  • Altman said OpenAI has internally discussed pausing development as models reach new capability thresholds, to let safety work catch up.
  • CNBC reported that Anthropic's Dario Amodei has proposed a plan to slow the pace of advancing AI capabilities, a position Altman and Elon Musk have both since echoed.
  • Separately, Layoffs.fyi counted 128,536 tech workers cut across 299 companies through September 10 — already past all of 2025. Uber's roughly 3,300 corporate cuts, about 10% of that workforce, lead the September total of 6,300-plus in ten days, alongside PayPal, Apple, Oracle and Zomato.
  • Stanford's 2026 AI Index found employment among software developers aged 22–25 down nearly 20% since 2024.

Left view

The Atlantic and Vox have argued that voluntary slowdowns announced by the companies that set the pace are governance theater — the same firms are simultaneously cutting entry-level staff and reinvesting in capacity. Mother Jones and labor-focused coverage put the emphasis on the 128,000 figure: whatever the stated cause, the displacement is real and the policy response is absent.

Right view

Reason and the Wall Street Journal editorial page have been skeptical of slowdown talk from a different angle, warning that safety framing becomes a moat: incumbents who call for a pause after building a lead are asking regulators to freeze the field. The Free Press and National Review have noted that Uber's restructuring is not, by the company's own account, an AI story — it is a reorganization to fund autonomous-driving competition, and conflating the two inflates the case for intervention.

Watch for

An OpenAI listing pushed to 2027 removes a major expected supply of equity from next year's IPO calendar and complicates valuation marks for private AI holdings — watch secondary-market pricing and SoftBank-style vehicles. On the labor side, the signal to track is whether entry-level technical hiring recovers in Q4 or whether the Gartner finding — that 22% of chief HR officers report a business leader halting junior hiring over automation — hardens into a structural change.

Zelensky offers to meet Putin at the Miami G20; the Kremlin says no

Facts

  • In a Deutsche Welle interview released September 12, Zelensky said he would meet Putin at the G20 leaders' summit in Miami on December 14–15.
  • Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov rejected the idea, telling AFP that any face-to-face could only happen in Moscow. Presidential aide Yuri Ushakov said Russia has not decided whether Putin will attend at all.
  • The two have not met in person since before the February 2022 full-scale invasion.
  • Russian strikes killed nine people across Ukraine over the weekend, per RFE/RL.
  • Zelensky has said he plans to meet Trump in late September, as Washington pushes for renewed direct Moscow–Kyiv contact.

Left view

The Guardian and NPR read the offer as Zelensky maneuvering to keep Ukraine on an agenda that Iran and energy prices have crowded out, and the Kremlin's Moscow-only counter as a demand for symbolic capitulation rather than a serious condition. The concern in this framing is that a distracted Washington accepts a settlement Kyiv cannot live with.

Right view

The Wall Street Journal editorial board and National Review have argued that summits without leverage produce nothing, and that the answer is sustained materiel and sanctions rather than venue diplomacy. A separate strand on the right — the Trump-aligned position — treats any meeting as progress and pressure on both capitals as the point, with the Miami venue itself a deliverable.

Watch for

The Zelensky–Trump meeting expected in late September is the more consequential date; the G20 offer mostly sets up that conversation. Analysts also note the overlap with the Iran file: Russian crude is a direct beneficiary of the Hormuz closure, which reduces Moscow's incentive to settle while prices stay above $100. Watch European commitments at the next EU summit and whether Putin confirms G20 attendance at all.
