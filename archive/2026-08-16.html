The Big Stories Today

Iran rejects Trump's claim of "total control" over Hormuz as another ship is struck

Facts

  • Trump said this week the U.S. has total control of the Strait of Hormuz and described the blockade as a "wall of steel," saying he expects to keep it. A senior Iranian lawmaker dismissed the claim as bluffing, and Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said the strait opens and closes only on Iran's command.
  • U.S. Central Command said it has redirected 55 commercial vessels away from Iranian ports since the blockade began — up from 35 as of Aug. 2, per CNBC. Another vessel was reported struck this week.
  • Transits through the strait have fallen to roughly three-month lows. The blockade has been in place since April 13 after the Islamabad talks collapsed; a June interim deal has not been revived.
  • Treasury has layered on sanctions targeting Chinese, Indian and third-country oil brokers, tanker operators and Iran's "shadow fleet." Iran's rial has weakened sharply against the dollar over the course of the campaign.
  • Iran and Oman have said they are close to an arrangement on managing the strait; Trump has characterized the U.S. as only "semi-negotiating," and Tehran denies direct talks.

Left view

NPR and CBS News have focused on the domestic bill: Moody's Mark Zandi told NBC News the war is costing the average household north of $1,200, with roughly $60 billion in cumulative added energy costs nationally. Guardian- and Vox-aligned commentary frames the blockade as an open-ended act of war Congress never authorized — the through-line of Sen. Adam Schiff's War Powers Resolution, co-sponsored by Kaine, Kim, Merkley and Van Hollen, which argues no legal basis exists for the administration to claim another 60-day clock.

Right view

Fox News opinion pieces argue maximum pressure is exposing the IRGC's structural weakness and that currency collapse and lost oil revenue are the metric that matters, not shipping counts. Notably, Fox News' own straight news desk has been more skeptical, reporting that two months of strikes and blockade have not produced the nuclear concessions Trump is seeking and that Iran has proven better at absorbing and rerouting pressure than Washington expected. That gap between the opinion and news sides is worth watching.

Watch for

Energy analysts tie near-term crude almost entirely to Hormuz headlines — prices slid toward the $70s on reopening optimism and back into the $80s on renewed uncertainty within the same month. EIA's outlook has Brent averaging around $85 in Q3. A confirmed Oman-brokered arrangement would likely take several dollars off crude and, with a lag of two to three weeks, off pump prices. Escalation instead would test the $4.50-plus gasoline highs seen in July. Watch also whether any Senate war powers vote gets floor time before the fall recess.

U.S. to tell about 35 partners: pick a side in the AI race with China

Facts

  • Reuters reported the State Department is preparing to tell roughly 35 partner countries that joining China's World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organisation (WAICO) means exclusion from the U.S.-led Pax Silica bloc.
  • Pax Silica, launched last year, coordinates supply chains for AI models, advanced semiconductors and critical minerals, and pushes members toward joint projects and aligned export controls. About two dozen countries have joined, including Japan, Australia and South Korea.
  • Xi Jinping announced WAICO on July 16 in Shanghai with 29 founding members, including Brazil, Russia and South Africa.
  • Kazakhstan — a key critical-minerals source — has joined both blocs, which is the overlap the U.S. statement appears aimed at.
  • Reporting notes the draft statement lacks specific enforcement mechanisms or sanctions.

Left view

Left-of-center outlets and NPR-adjacent analysts read this as coercive diplomacy that risks pushing fence-sitting middle powers toward Beijing, and question whether an ultimatum without enforcement teeth is credible. The Kazakhstan case gets cited as evidence that resource-rich states will simply hedge.

Right view

The Free Press, National Review and WSJ editorial-page arguments treat chips and critical minerals as national-security infrastructure where neutrality is not really available, and see forcing a choice as overdue. Reason and libertarian-leaning writers dissent from the right, warning that bloc-building fragments supply chains and raises costs for U.S. firms.

Watch for

Analysts expect the first real test to be Gulf states and Southeast Asian partners with large Chinese data-center investments. Semiconductor equipment names and critical-minerals miners are the obvious market read-through. Watch whether the final statement adds enforcement language — without it, most trade analysts expect quiet non-compliance rather than defections.

  • Trump's approval hit new lows in late July, per aggregate polling, driven by the Iran conflict and prices.

Left view

MSNBC and Guardian coverage casts the PAC spending as a candidate-quality bailout and reads Lindell's loss as evidence that Trump's primary endorsements no longer travel. The Flanagan result is framed as the party's base rewarding economic populism.

Right view

Washington Examiner and NY Post framing emphasizes that the Senate map still favors Republicans and that Flanagan's primary win hands the GOP a more beatable general-election opponent. The Dispatch is more downbeat, arguing prices and the war are structural drags that money cannot paper over.

Watch for

Election forecasters treat gas prices and the Iran conflict as the two variables most likely to move the generic ballot between now and November. Watch September fundraising disclosures for whether Trump's PAC money actually lands in competitive House seats or in loyalty-driven races, and watch for the first post-Labor Day battleground polling.

Ukraine's deep-strike campaign escalates as talks stay frozen

Facts

  • Ukraine launched hundreds of drones into Russia over the weekend, with at least six reported killed in the latest wave, per wire reporting.
  • Earlier this month a Ukrainian drone strike on Nizhnekamsk in Tatarstan — roughly 800 km east of Moscow — killed at least 13, including a child, and wounded about 39, per Al Jazeera. Russia said it intercepted 456 drones across some 15 regions in that night's attacks.
  • Kyiv accuses Moscow of refusing to end the war and says it is consulting mediators this week.
  • A Ukrainian peace negotiator told NPR the theory of the case is that sustained long-range strikes eventually leave Putin no option but to negotiate a stop.

Left view

The Guardian and NPR give weight to the civilian toll on both sides and question whether deep strikes shorten the war or entrench it, noting Russia has absorbed comparable campaigns before without changing its negotiating position.

Right view

National Review and Washington Examiner writers argue the strikes are the only leverage Kyiv has and that pressure on Russian refining and logistics is what brings Moscow to the table. The Dispatch has pressed the harder question of whether U.S. attention and munitions are being consumed by Iran at Ukraine's expense.

Watch for

Energy analysts watch Russian refinery outages closely — repeated hits on refining and export infrastructure tighten global diesel, which is already at record seasonal U.S. prices. A Turkey-hosted round of talks remains the most-floated venue. Any strike causing mass casualties in a major Russian city raises escalation risk around Western-supplied capabilities.

The Fed's September call: cooler inflation, but a weak labor market and an energy shock

Facts

  • July CPI rose 0.1% on the month and 3.4% on the year; core rose 0.2% and 2.5% annually. Both headline and core ticked down 0.1 point from June, per CNBC.
  • The University of Michigan's preliminary August sentiment index came in at 51, well below forecasts near 55.
  • Three FOMC members dissented at the last meeting in favor of a 25 basis-point increase — an unusual configuration driven by the energy-price shock.
  • CME FedWatch odds of a September hike have fallen to roughly 42%. July showed a net job loss, which has cooled the case for tightening.
  • Energy is doing most of the work in the headline number; the Fed has publicly attributed part of the overshoot to supply shocks.

Left view

Vox and NPR-adjacent economists argue raising rates into an oil shock punishes households twice, since the Fed cannot manufacture crude, and point to the sentiment print and the July job loss as evidence the real risk is demand, not overheating.

Right view

WSJ editorial writers and Forbes contributors on the hawkish side argue credibility is the asset at stake with inflation at 3.4% for this long, and that tolerating supply-driven inflation is how expectations came unanchored in the 1970s. Reason-style critics add that tariffs are a self-inflicted part of the price level the Fed is now being asked to offset.

Watch for

The August CPI and jobs reports land before the September FOMC and will effectively decide it. Rate-sensitive corners — small caps, housing, regional banks — have been trading on each print. A hold plus hawkish language is the modal expectation on the street; an actual hike would be a genuine surprise given current futures pricing.

National Guard deployments hit the one-year mark in D.C. — and the courts

Facts

  • The D.C. National Guard deployment has been extended, roughly a year after the crime emergency declaration that started it.
  • A federal judge ruled the original D.C. deployment unlawful and ordered troops out; appellate courts have stayed that order, so it has had no practical effect.
  • As of early August, more than 4,600 military personnel from D.C. and 24 states have been involved across deployments, which have also touched Memphis, Los Angeles, the Chicago area and New Orleans.
  • The Supreme Court declined in December to let the administration proceed immediately with a Chicago deployment; Trump subsequently said he was dropping the Chicago, Portland and Los Angeles push "for now."
  • The Center for American Progress puts the cost of the deployments at about $1.7 billion and argues they have not reduced urban crime.

Left view

Center for American Progress, the Guardian and MSNBC frame the deployments as expensive political theater with no measurable crime effect, and as a federalism problem — troops in cities over the objection of state and local officials.

Right view

Fox News and NY Post coverage highlights local crime declines in deployment cities and argues federal presence deters. The R Street Institute — right-leaning but libertarian — has publicly criticized the practice, and National Review writers have split, some backing the policy and others warning about normalizing domestic military deployment.

Watch for

Legal analysts expect the D.C. case to be the vehicle that eventually settles the standing question for domestic deployments. Whether St. Louis or New York see deployments is likely to turn on that litigation rather than on crime statistics. Watch for appropriations fights over the roughly $1.7 billion price tag if Democrats take the House.
