Strait of Hormuz standoff drags on as Trump insists U.S. is in "total control"

Facts

  • Trump reiterated on Truth Social that the U.S. has "total control" of the Strait of Hormuz and warned Iran would "get blown away" if it acted; Iran's foreign ministry publicly declared the strait blocked, and FM Abbas Araghchi denied Tehran is in direct talks with Washington.
  • Iran-Oman negotiations over inbound/outbound shipping lanes have run three-plus weeks with broad agreement on routes but no final deal; tankers have been largely unable to transit since Iran closed the waterway when fighting resumed in July.
  • U.S. Central Command said it disabled a Panama-flagged cargo vessel that tried to break the U.S. blockade of Iranian ports; Houthi forces killed six aboard a cargo ship in the Bab el-Mandeb strait — the first Red Sea shipping fatalities in over a year — and claimed an earlier strike on Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery.
  • The strait carried roughly one-fifth of global oil supply before the war; the IEA-tracked forecast now sees global supply falling about 4.3 million barrels/day.

Left view

Left-leaning and mainstream outlets stress the gap between Trump's rhetoric and facts on the ground: NPR frames the war and resulting price spike as having driven his economy approval to around 31%, and notes voters who backed him to lower costs and avoid foreign wars are getting the opposite. A Washington Post opinion piece argued Trump is wrong to scapegoat oil companies for prices the war itself is driving. Sen. Chuck Schumer questioned how durable any reopening deal would even be.

Right view

Fox News amplified administration voices: Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Iran now faces "someone that actually takes action," and VP JD Vance described talks as being in "the middle of the game" with the goal of maximizing oil flow. Notably, some hawks broke with the White House — former Defense Secretary Mark Esper (via Fox) warned that letting Iran and Oman help administer strait traffic could be a "very bad outcome" for the U.S.

Watch for

Analysts tie near-term oil to the deal odds: Brent hovered near $89-90 and WTI in the low $80s, with any breakdown in Oman talks or fresh Houthi strikes seen pushing crude back toward triple digits. A reopening would ease pump prices before the midterms; continued closure keeps gasoline elevated and pressures Republicans on affordability.

Gas hits highest-ever level for mid-August as pump pain becomes political

Facts

  • AAA put the national average for regular at about $4.03/gal as of Aug. 12, roughly $0.16 higher than a month earlier and up sharply from around $2.98 before the Iran war.
  • GasBuddy said the average is at its highest-ever level this late in the calendar year — no prior year saw the average above $4 after Aug. 12.
  • State spread is wide: California ($5.66), Hawaii ($5.45) and Washington ($5.13) highest; Texas ($3.60), Indiana ($3.61) and Mississippi ($3.65) lowest.
  • Trump has directed the DOJ to investigate Big Oil for possible price gouging, publicly pressured Exxon and Chevron over profits, and is weighing extending a Jones Act waiver.

Left view

The Washington Post editorial/opinion side calls it misdirection to blame refiners when the war-driven crude spike is the cause, arguing the administration owns the geopolitical decisions behind higher prices. Coverage in outlets like NPR and Yahoo highlights voters who feel Trump broke a core promise to bring costs down.

Right view

Fox News frames the squeeze as a byproduct of ending years of "radical climate" energy policy and points to price gouging by oil majors, echoing the White House. The Washington Examiner reported Trump warning prices "may have to" rise further as leverage against Iran, casting the pain as a temporary cost of a hard line.

Watch for

Energy analysts say a Jones Act waiver alone is unlikely to move pump prices much (OilPrice.com); the real lever is Hormuz reopening. With midterms ahead, sustained $4+ gas is the affordability metric strategists in both parties are watching most closely.
