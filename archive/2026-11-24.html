Nvidia reinstates Jensen Huang after Trump intervenes; the stock jumps 31% and Congress vows hearings

Facts

NATO sends AWACS to Poland after confirming all three objects were Russian cruise missiles; Moscow warns Estonia

Facts

  • Poland now says all three objects that came down in its southeast on Nov. 20 were Russian Kh-101 cruise missiles. Earlier reports said two missiles and a drone (Notes from Poland, Kyiv Post).
  • Monday's emergency North Atlantic Council meeting said Russia "bears full responsibility." NATO's military commander authorized the forward deployment of an AWACS surveillance plane and is weighing more assets. Poland's other requests go to next week's NATO foreign ministers' meeting (Notes from Poland, UNITED24).
  • Late Monday another Russian drone flew about 20 km into Poland. It turned back before jets fired. Moscow hasn't commented on any of the incidents (Notes from Poland).
  • Separately, Russia's Foreign Ministry warned that Estonia risks "open war" by closing its land border crossings from Dec. 1. Tallinn blames Russian services for an attempted arson attack on a defense firm, which Moscow denies (10 Things, Al Jazeera).

Left view

European and Ukrainian outlets are leading. Kyiv Post quoted Poland's deputy defense minister, who called it hard to imagine an accident and said all allies expressed solidarity. That framing contrasts with Trump's weekend remark that the missiles landed "by mistake."

Right view

Right-leaning US coverage is mostly wire copy. The Washington Times carried the NAC meeting straight. Hawkish voices welcome NATO's quick AWACS move as deterrence, but there's no clear right-wing pushback on Trump's "mistake" framing. Mainstream US coverage is also thin.

Watch for

Next week's NATO foreign ministers' meeting, where Poland will press for more warplanes and air defense. Also watch Estonia's Dec. 1 border closure and whether Russia escalates in the Baltics.

Western intelligence: Russia could launch a nuclear-armed satellite as soon as December

Facts

  • A joint investigation by Germany's WDR, NDR and Süddeutsche Zeitung reports that Western intelligence believes Russia could put a nuclear-armed satellite, "Sputnik-S," into orbit as early as December. It was reportedly built years ago, is stored near Moscow, and could be launched within 60 days (Moscow Times, Hromadske).
  • Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said he hadn't seen the report and dismissed "possible" claims as not worth taking seriously. Russia's Berlin embassy called it propaganda (Moscow Times).
  • The US DIA said in October that Russia is developing a nuclear anti-satellite capability. A nuclear detonation in orbit would violate the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, which Russia signed, and could disable thousands of satellites, including GPS (10 Things, IBTimes).

Left view

European and Ukrainian outlets are leading. Coverage frames it as part of a broader Russian escalation pattern alongside Poland and Estonia, and as a test of whether Washington takes it seriously.

Right view

US right-leaning coverage is thin so far. Vision Times ran the intelligence warning. Defense hawks have long cited this threat in calls for more spending on space defense. US mainstream coverage is also light; this is mostly a European story for now.

Watch for

Any US or NATO confirmation, and activity at Russian launch sites. A launch would put satellite operators and defense-space stocks in focus.
