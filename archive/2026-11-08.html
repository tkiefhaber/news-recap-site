Iran: U.S. adds a land "Task Force Iron Gate" to its sea blockade as mines hit two tankers and Tehran weighs Qatar's offer

Facts

  • CENTCOM on Saturday launched Task Force Iron Gate, run from Tampa with other agencies. Its job is to cut Iran's land and Caspian Sea routes for weapons, dual-use technology, sanctioned goods and illicit revenue. CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper said it backs up the "ironclad" naval blockade (Al Jazeera).
  • Iranian state media reported explosions Saturday evening on Qeshm Island and at Jask, Sirik and Bandar Abbas, with no cause given. On Sunday the IRGC Navy said two tankers hit mines after taking an "unauthorised route" through Hormuz with their navigation systems off (ABC News Australia, Al Jazeera).
  • The U.S. disabled the cargo ship M/V Huda in the Gulf of Oman after it ignored warnings. CENTCOM says it has redirected 147 commercial vessels and disabled five since July.
  • On talks, the AP reports Iran is reviewing Qatari proposals on the nuclear dispute. Tehran still refuses to negotiate its atomic program until the U.S. accepts its conditions. Separately, the IRGC lifted its self-imposed 2,000 km cap on ballistic-missile range.

Left view

Democrats and antiwar groups argue a new task force expands the war rather than winding it down, days after voters punished the GOP over it. Expect them to tie it to War Powers fights in the lame duck and next year. Al Jazeera quoted former Pentagon official Michael Mulroy as saying any new escalation would likely target Iran's economy and infrastructure, not just its military, which critics say raises civilian and oil-market risks.

Right view

Administration allies and Fox News coverage present Iron Gate and the blockade as leverage that keeps Tehran at the table without a full air campaign. GOP hawks, as Gulf News and Fox have reported in recent weeks, worry that Trump will accept a Qatar-brokered deal that unfreezes assets before Iran gives up enough on its nuclear program. Lifting the missile-range cap feeds that skepticism.

Watch for

The Nov. 9 new moon and the mid-month arrival of the USS Roosevelt are the near-term military markers. Mines in Hormuz are the biggest risk for oil: Brent ended the week near $98 after falling about 16% on deal hopes, and analysts say a real escalation would quickly erase that drop. Watch whether Iran formally answers Qatar's draft, and whether Iron Gate brings secondary pressure on Caspian neighbors and on Turkey and Iraq border trade.

Miller takes over as chief of staff Monday, the same week Massie tries to force a Hegseth impeachment vote

Facts

  • Stephen Miller becomes White House chief of staff on Monday, Nov. 9, replacing Susie Wiles. At a Mar-a-Lago event this weekend he called the promotion the "honor of my life" (The Hill). A senior official told reporters Miller "has Hegseth's back" (Yahoo News).
  • Rep. Thomas Massie (R-Ky.) plans to force a House vote Monday or Tuesday, when lawmakers return, on his eight-article impeachment resolution against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. He first filed it in September and says he'll drop it if Hegseth resigns (Newsweek).
  • Trump says publicly he has "every intention" of keeping Hegseth, though CBS reports he has privately asked advisers about Hegseth's future. Sen. Ted Cruz predicts an impeachment vote in the lame duck. Impeachment needs a simple House majority, and removal needs two-thirds of the Senate.
  • The Wall Street Journal described Miller as an advocate of the no-holds-barred governing style that much of the country rejected on Election Day. The Washington Post reports that industries dependent on immigrant labor are objecting to his promotion.

Left view

The Washington Post's analysis presents the promotion as the start of a new era of confrontation with an incoming Democratic Congress. Democrats, led by Hakeem Jeffries, say Trump is doubling down on the hard-line immigration agenda voters just rejected. They see the Hegseth vote as a test of whether House Republicans will hold the administration accountable for the Iran war before Democrats take the gavels in January.

Right view

The right is split. The Washington Examiner's Beltway Confidential called the Miller pick a defiant answer to defeat. Fox News' Reporter's Notebook looked at how the midterm losses could shift the House impeachment math on Hegseth in the lame duck. Trump loyalists argue Miller is the right person to govern by executive action and veto against a hostile Congress.

Watch for

The first vote on Massie's resolution is likely a motion to table, which is a cleaner test of GOP loyalty than an up-or-down vote. Watch Miller's first executive orders on immigration and tariffs. Business groups in agriculture, construction and hospitality are already warning about labor shortages. The Dec. 11 government-funding deadline is the first big negotiation of the Miller era.

Israel: Eisenkot gets the mandate to form a government today, but a 61-seat coalition is still hard to assemble

Facts

  • President Isaac Herzog formally hands Yashar leader Gadi Eisenkot the mandate at a 5 p.m. ceremony Sunday. Eisenkot was recommended by 67 of 120 Knesset members to Netanyahu's 46. United Torah Judaism (7 seats) recommended no one (Times of Israel).
  • Eisenkot has 28 days, plus a possible 14-day extension. If he fails, Netanyahu could get a turn.
  • The math is tight. The 48-seat "change bloc" plus UTJ reaches 55. Getting to 61 would also take Shas's tacit support, and Shas calls reports that it might give it "total fake news." Hawks in Eisenkot's bloc reject relying on the Arab parties Ra'am and the Joint List.
  • Netanyahu released a video calling for a "national unity government" that includes all his former partners, and he warned against any government that relies on Arab parties. Yashar replied that Likud could join only under Eisenkot and without the "extremists."

Left view

NPR and Al Jazeera treat Herzog's choice as a major setback for Netanyahu after nearly two decades of dominance. CNN stresses that the path is still complicated. Center-left Israeli commentary focuses on a reported months-long Liberman–Haredi back channel (Channel 13, per Times of Israel) and whether a compromise on Haredi draft service could unlock a coalition.

Right view

JNS gives prominence to Likud's claims of voter fraud and intimidation in Arab towns, and to a Shin Bet probe into suspected foreign interference. It also argues that the larger Arab bloc complicates coalition math. Netanyahu's camp says it remains the largest party and that Trump backs him. Breitbart also called the decision a setback for Netanyahu.

Watch for

The key question is UTJ and Shas: whether Eisenkot can agree on a Haredi enlistment deal that his secular partners accept. A government that doesn't depend on Arab parties would be more stable but would need Haredi support. A shift away from Netanyahu could change Israel's posture in Lebanon and toward Iran, both of which are among Tehran's conditions for a deal, so U.S.–Iran diplomacy is also watching.

Senate stragglers: Colom holds ~2,500-vote lead in Mississippi; the administration sues over Alaska's two Dan Sullivans

Facts

  • Mississippi: As of late Saturday, Democrat Scott Colom led Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by about 2,500 votes with 97% counted. Thousands of absentee and affidavit ballots are still under review, including more than 2,000 in DeSoto County, a Hyde-Smith stronghold. Counties must certify by Friday, Nov. 13 (Action News 5).
  • Hyde-Smith said, "We are not backing down. This election is not over." Colom said his side has "an army of lawyers" and that local officials won't be intimidated.
  • Alaska: Democrat Mary Peltola leads Sen. Dan Sullivan by fewer than 700 votes with 74% counted. The margin barely moved Friday, and the ranked-choice tabulation won't happen before Nov. 18 (WABI/AP).
  • The Trump administration has sued to block certification if Peltola wins. It alleges Democrats helped put a second "Dan Sullivan" on the ballot as a third-party candidate to siphon votes, and the DOJ and FBI are investigating. Peltola's campaign has separately sued the state for records on rejected absentee ballots (The Hill).

Left view

Democracy Docket argues new data shows Alaska voters weren't confused by the two Sullivans and that Peltola is likely to win. It calls the federal suit an attempt to overturn a result through the courts. In Mississippi, it and Mississippi Free Press say Colom is winning the remaining ballots by wide margins, even in Republican-leaning counties.

Right view

Republicans say the duplicate-name candidacy was a dirty trick and that it should be investigated before Alaska certifies. In Mississippi, Hyde-Smith's campaign and the state GOP are watching ballots county by county and promise to challenge improper ones. Hyde-Smith's camp is counting on the large batch of DeSoto County ballots still under review.

Watch for

Mississippi has no automatic recount, so a loser who stays close would go to a "ballot box examination" and then court, likely after Nov. 13. Wins in both races would give Democrats 56 Senate seats, which matters for confirmations next year, including any Hegseth successor. The Alaska lawsuit's first hearing will show whether federal courts take the confusion theory seriously.

Judge freezes Trump's 250-foot arch near Arlington Cemetery hours after planners approved it

Facts

  • The National Capital Planning Commission approved the arch at Memorial Circle 8–4 Thursday after a meeting of almost 10 hours. U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan then issued a 14-day temporary restraining order blocking construction.
  • The attorneys general of D.C. and Virginia sued Oct. 31. They argue Congress never authorized the arch and that it violates height, historic-preservation and environmental rules. Rep. Don Beyer's bill to bar and defund it has about 80 cosponsors (KPBS/NPR).
  • The administration cites a 1925 law that authorized two 166-foot columns at the circle that were never built, and it describes the arch as a "dual-purpose" security structure. Chutkan wrote that the security claims don't appear anywhere in the planning documents.
  • White House spokesman Davis Ingle blamed an "activist district court" and said the administration expects to be "fully vindicated." Trump wants the arch finished before his term ends.

Left view

The Daily Beast called the ruling a humiliating reversal for a vanity project. Preservation groups such as the National Trust for Historic Preservation called the review a "sham," and critics say a monument next to Arlington National Cemetery is self-memorialization. Democrats plan to use their new majorities to defund it.

Right view

Conservative outlets are less united than usual. RedState's writer was skeptical that a bridge law can authorize a giant monument and suggested the White House may have to ask Congress, even as the site's editor's note criticized unelected judges. National Review's Corner covered the order as a significant setback. The White House says the arch serves both a commemorative and a security purpose and that an "activist" court is holding it up.

Watch for

Expect a preliminary-injunction hearing before the TRO expires around Nov. 19, and an appeal to the D.C. Circuit is likely. Bloomberg Law reported Chutkan saying she feared waking up to bulldozers. If the injunction holds into January, a Democratic Congress can cut off funding.
