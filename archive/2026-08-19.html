The Big Stories Today

UAE reports Iranian missiles as Trump declares Hormuz under US control and rules out talks

Facts

  • The UAE said its air defenses detected two ballistic missiles launched toward the country from Iran, then issued an all-clear. Iran's foreign ministry spokesman denied Tehran was responsible.
  • The US–Iran memorandum of understanding on reopening the Strait of Hormuz — signed June 17 and never fully implemented — has now lapsed, and Trump rejected an extension.
  • Trump said there are no talks or conversations underway or scheduled with Iran, and characterized the strait as effectively under US control. Secretary of State Rubio emphasized freedom of navigation.
  • Hormuz traffic remains near-collapsed: roughly 8–15 transits a day in early August versus about 130 before the war began in late February.
  • Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi has said the waterway stays shut until Washington eases sanctions and addresses war reparations.

Left view

Al Jazeera and NPR frame the collapsed MOU as evidence that maximum-pressure escalation has not produced a durable agreement, and that declaring the strait "US-controlled" is a rhetorical claim the shipping data does not support. The Hill's opinion section argues the administration's approach isn't moving the needle and that an implied regime-change goal has no defined endpoint.

Right view

Fox News and Newsmax coverage stresses that Iran, not Washington, walked away from an implementable deal, and that Iranian demands for sanctions relief and reparations were never serious. Treasury Secretary Bessent has pitched economic isolation as the lever that ends the war without further strikes; The Free Press and National Review have generally argued that backing off now would validate the blockade as a working tactic for any adversary.

Watch for

Energy analysts see Brent staying in the high-$80s to low-$90s while transits stay suppressed; the IEA has flagged that global stockpiles are drawing down fast, which limits how long prices can stay merely elevated rather than spiking. A confirmed missile strike on a Gulf state — as opposed to a detection — would be the escalation marker to watch, since it would pull the UAE and Saudi Arabia further in. Domestically, sustained $4 gasoline is a live midterm variable.

Meta goes on trial in Oakland over claims its apps were built to hook children

Facts

  • Opening arguments began August 18 in the US District Court for the Northern District of California before Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers. The trial is expected to run about six weeks.
  • A bipartisan group of 29 states is suing. Four — California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey — are the trial plaintiffs seeking penalties that could theoretically reach $1.4 trillion under state consumer-protection law.
  • The states allege Meta designed features including the like button, infinite scroll and recommendation algorithms to prolong use by minors, misrepresented the risks publicly, and collected data on under-13 users without parental consent.
  • Meta calls the claims unsubstantiated and the financial demands disproportionate. It lost a last-minute bid to pause the trial.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian place this alongside tobacco and opioid litigation — the argument being that internal documents, not just outcomes research, are what move public opinion and force product changes. Coverage emphasizes the youth mental-health framing and the states' claim that Meta knew and shipped anyway.

Right view

Fox Business leads with the states' allegation that Meta viewed young users as its most valuable cohort, and conservative coverage has been notably supportive of the plaintiffs — this is one of the rarer cases where Republican and Democratic AGs are aligned. The Dispatch and Reason-style skeptics push back on a different axis: that a trillion-dollar consumer-protection theory stretches those statutes well past their design and invites courts to regulate product design.

Watch for

Legal analysts expect the discovery record — internal research and growth-team communications — to matter more than the damages number, which almost certainly won't survive intact. A plaintiff win would likely produce injunctive design changes (default settings for minors, algorithmic limits) that ripple to TikTok, YouTube and Snap. Meta's stock has largely shrugged so far; a bad first week of testimony is the thing that would change that.

The 30-year Treasury yield hits a 19-year high as the long end of the bond market sells off

Facts

  • The 30-year yield topped 5.33% on August 18, its highest since 2007, after climbing to about 5.31% the prior day. It eased slightly to roughly 5.285% intraday.
  • Drivers cited: heavy long-dated issuance, a CBO deficit projection revised up to $2.1 trillion (about $200 billion above the February estimate), and inflation running above the Fed's target for five years.
  • Foreign Treasury holdings fell in June, with the UK, China and Japan all trimming positions.
  • July CPI came in at 3.4% annual, down a tenth from June; core rose 0.2% on the month. Fed funds has sat at 3.50%–3.75% for five straight meetings, with three FOMC dissents favoring a hike.

Left view

NPR and Vox-adjacent commentary tie the term premium to tariff-driven price pressure and to tax cuts that widened the deficit, arguing bond investors are pricing in fiscal choices rather than spending on programs. Axios frames rising yields as the market's verdict on both the deficit path and questions about Fed independence.

Right view

The WSJ editorial board and National Review read the same yield as a straightforward spending indictment — that Washington is issuing debt faster than the world wants to absorb it, and that entitlement and discretionary growth, not revenue, is the binding constraint. Fox Business notes inflation cooled in July and argues the Fed should not be pushed into cuts that would steepen the curve further.

Watch for

Strategists flag that a sustained 30-year above 5% raises mortgage rates, squeezes housing affordability, and hits companies facing refinancing walls — CNBC has laid out three paths that could push it higher still. Futures pricing puts September rate-hike odds around 42%, so the September FOMC and the next auction results are the near-term tells. A weak long-bond auction is the single event most likely to accelerate the move.

Alaska's all-party primary sets up a Sullivan–Peltola Senate race that could decide the chamber

Facts

  • In preliminary results, Democrat Mary Peltola led with about 47.3% and Republican incumbent Sen. Dan Sullivan drew about 44.0%, with roughly 70% of precincts reporting.
  • Alaska uses a top-four all-party primary; the general election in November is decided by ranked-choice voting, so the primary margin is a weak predictor of the final result.
  • Alaska's US House primary produced no surprises. The Senate outcome is one of a handful that could determine control of the chamber.
  • Separately, Minnesota Gov. Peggy Flanagan won that state's Democratic Senate primary, extending a run of progressive wins over centrists.

Left view

MSNBC and the Alaska Beacon read Peltola's first-place finish as evidence a Democrat can lead statewide in Alaska and as part of a broader map that has Democrats competitive for the Senate. NPR's elections coverage pairs it with Flanagan's win as a sign the party's progressive wing is consolidating primary energy.

Right view

The Washington Examiner and National Review caution that a top-four primary understates Republican strength, since GOP votes split across multiple candidates and consolidate under ranked choice in November — Sullivan's 2022 analogue is the reference point. Fox News coverage focuses instead on the money picture, noting Trump has cleared millions from his PAC for vulnerable Republicans. CNN reports some Trump campaign advisers privately concede that holding both chambers is slipping.

Watch for

Election analysts expect Alaska to be a persistent tossup and a ranked-choice modeling headache; second-choice transfers from minor candidates decide it, not the top-line spread. Watch for renewed Republican efforts to repeal ranked-choice voting in the state, and for outside spending to surge into Alaska now that the field is set.

Indiana seeks a major disaster declaration as historic flooding displaces more than a thousand

Facts

  • Storms beginning August 11 brought straight-line winds, tornadoes and record rainfall across central and southern Indiana. At least seven people have died.
  • Gov. Mike Braun declared a statewide disaster emergency, activated the National Guard, and announced $5,000 relief grants for affected individuals.
  • President Trump approved a federal emergency declaration on August 15 covering more than 50 counties from Lake Michigan to the Ohio River, directing FEMA to supplement state and local response.
  • Braun is now requesting a major disaster declaration, which would unlock individual assistance paid directly to homeowners rather than reimbursement to the state.
  • More than 1,000 people remain out of their homes.

Left view

NPR and the Indiana Capital Chronicle connect the event to a documented rise in extreme-precipitation days across the Midwest and question whether FEMA's reduced staffing and shifted mission can handle simultaneous disasters. The Guardian-style framing emphasizes that flood-plain mapping and insurance coverage lag current rainfall patterns.

Right view

Fox News and local conservative outlets center the speed of the federal approval and Braun's National Guard activation as the operational story, and push back on climate attribution for a single storm system. The broader right-leaning argument, made in outlets like the Washington Examiner, is that states should hold larger reserve funds so federal declarations are a backstop rather than the default first move.

Watch for

A major disaster declaration typically takes days to weeks after a state request; the Individual Assistance component is the one homeowners care about. Watch crop-loss estimates out of southern Indiana, which feed into corn and soybean futures, and expect renewed pressure on the National Flood Insurance Program given how many damaged homes sit outside mapped flood zones.

Russia threatens the UK over British-made drones used in strikes inside Russia

Facts

  • Moscow warned on August 17 that the UK would face consequences after reports that Ukraine used drones built by two British companies for strikes deep inside Russian territory.
  • The Russian embassy in London said Britain is deliberately choosing escalation and that greater support for Kyiv means a higher price.
  • Reported targets over the past six months include oil refineries at Volgograd and Yaroslavl.
  • The threat follows one of Ukraine's largest aerial operations of the war: Moscow's mayor said at least 180 drones were downed over Moscow Oblast, with Ukrainian claims of more than 600 launched at the region.

Left view

The Guardian and BBC treat the statement as familiar deterrent messaging aimed at fracturing European support, and note Russia has issued similar warnings to the UK, France and Germany without following through on direct retaliation. Coverage stresses that Ukraine's refinery campaign is a legitimate military-economic target set under the laws of armed conflict.

Right view

The Telegraph and Washington Examiner argue the threats confirm the refinery strikes are working, and that London should accelerate rather than moderate deliveries. A dissenting restraint-minded strand — visible in The American Conservative and parts of Reason — warns that Western-supplied deep-strike capability makes NATO a co-belligerent in Moscow's framing and raises the odds of sabotage or hybrid retaliation on British soil.

Watch for

Analysts expect any Russian response to be asymmetric — undersea cable interference, GPS jamming over the Baltic, or sabotage of defense-linked firms — rather than overt military action. Refinery damage has already tightened Russian domestic fuel supply; further strikes would pressure Russian diesel exports at the same moment Hormuz is constraining Gulf crude, a compounding effect on global product prices.

US missile interceptor stockpiles are badly drawn down, and rebuilding them will take years

Facts

  • CNN reported commanders warning that nearly 80% of interceptors for a key missile-defense system have been expended since the Iran war began.
  • CSIS estimates fewer than 827 Patriot and fewer than 278 THAAD interceptors remain in US stockpiles; THAAD inventory was roughly 452 before the war.
  • Analysts estimate US forces fired hundreds of Tomahawks and more than 1,000 Patriot and THAAD interceptors in the war's early phase.
  • The Pentagon moved on August 3 to roughly triple Patriot and quadruple THAAD interceptor production. Lockheed Martin delivered a record 620 missiles in 2025 and is targeting up to 2,000 a year.
  • CSIS estimates three or more years to restore THAAD inventories. US officials have publicly disputed characterizations that supplies are dangerously low. A Patriot shortfall has also raised air-defense concerns in South Korea.

Left view

NPR and The Atlantic use the depletion as the concrete cost of an open-ended war authorized without a congressional vote — the House passed a war-powers resolution 214–208 in July while the Senate version failed 47–49, and no such resolution has ever survived a veto. The argument is that magazine depth, not rhetoric, defines how long this can continue.

Right view

National Review and the Washington Examiner largely agree on the facts and draw the opposite lesson: the shortfall is an indictment of a decade of underinvestment in munitions industrial base, and the answer is multiyear procurement contracts, not withdrawal. Fox News has amplified administration pushback that the reporting overstates the problem and reveals readiness details adversaries can use.

Watch for

The Christian Science Monitor notes China and Russia are reading the same numbers, which is the strategic risk beyond the Gulf. Near term, watch defense appropriations for multiyear interceptor buys, Lockheed and RTX guidance, and whether Patriot batteries get repositioned away from the Korean Peninsula or Europe — each move is a signal about where the Pentagon thinks the next demand comes from.
