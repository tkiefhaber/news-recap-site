The Big Stories Today

Trump weighs a major Iran escalation as UN General Assembly week opens

Facts

  • The war that began with joint U.S.–Israeli strikes on Feb. 28 is in its seventh month and has been intermittent since an April 7 ceasefire collapsed.
  • Trump told Axios on Sept. 17 he is at a crossroads over whether to restart large-scale attacks; on Sept. 20 he said "very big things" are coming and that he would probably be open to meeting Iran's president.
  • Iran's military says it has been told the U.S. is preparing to strike again, and Tehran has vowed "painful" retaliation, per CNBC.
  • CENTCOM figures given to congressional committees put the war's cost at $43.6 billion as of early September, with 18 U.S. service members killed and more than 800 wounded.
  • The UNGA general debate opens Tuesday with roughly 130 leaders speaking. Trump is expected to argue Iran cannot be allowed a nuclear weapon.
  • The House passed a third war powers resolution on Sept. 15 with seven Republicans joining Democrats, up from four on the previous two. It is non-binding.
  • Iran's rial has fallen past two million to the dollar and domestic inflation is spiking.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian keep the legal question in front: the war was never authorized by Congress and critics say it violates the UN Charter's prohibition on force. Democracy Now! and Guardian coverage cite international law scholars warning that Trump's threats to destroy Iranian civilian infrastructure could amount to Rome Statute violations. The framing is of an open-ended war with no stated endpoint and a rising body count.

Right view

Fox News coverage frames the pressure campaign as leverage that is working: Vice President Vance warns a U.S. exit would trigger a worldwide energy crisis, and UN Ambassador Mike Waltz points to Iran's collapsing currency. But the right is split — a Fox News poll taken Sept. 11–14 found 70% of voters say Trump lacks a clear strategy and 60% call the military action the wrong decision, and Tucker Carlson has called the campaign "Israel's war." Media Matters counts more than 250 calls for escalation across Fox's main programs since April.

Watch for

Brent closed Friday near $104 and WTI near $100. Citi puts roughly 30% odds on Brent spiking to $150; Wood Mackenzie says $200 is not impossible if Gulf supply losses hold near 15 million barrels a day. Any renewed strike package would move crude, and gas prices, inside six weeks of the midterms — which is the political variable analysts keep flagging. Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan says Ankara and others are circulating new proposals to get talks concluded.

AfD wins a second German state in two weeks; Merz calls it a disaster

Facts

  • In Sunday's Mecklenburg-Vorpommern state election the AfD took about 38%, ahead of the SPD at 35.5% and the Left at 6.5%.
  • Chancellor Friedrich Merz's CDU finished at roughly 4.8%, below the threshold for proportional seats and its worst result in any German state election.
  • It is the AfD's second state win in two weeks, after taking 43.8% in Saxony-Anhalt on Sept. 6.
  • Merz called the result a disaster, said he will not resign, and again ruled out any cooperation with the AfD.
  • The other major parties' "firewall" of non-cooperation is expected to keep the AfD out of government in both states despite its first-place finishes.

Left view

CNN and The Guardian read this as the center collapsing rather than the far right simply rising — Merz's own line was that the CDU got crushed in the polarization between the SPD and the AfD. The concern flagged is normalization: a party under domestic surveillance scrutiny now routinely finishing first in the east.

Right view

Breitbart and the Hungarian Conservative treat the result as a verdict on migration policy and on Merz personally, noting he refuses to resign after the CDU's worst showing since 1949. A quieter strand inside the CDU, covered by the Atlantic Council and Fortune, argues the firewall itself now feeds AfD grievance and needs adjusting — a position Merz's leadership rejects outright.

Watch for

Pressure on Merz's federal coalition and his reform agenda, which he pledged Saturday to press ahead with. Analysts are watching whether CDU state parties start breaking ranks on the firewall ahead of the next round of eastern elections, and what a weakened German chancellor means for EU positions on Ukraine funding and energy.

ICE officer shoots a DoorDash driver in Austin

Facts

  • Just before 1 p.m. Sunday, near U.S. 183 and Anderson Square in north Austin, a federal immigration officer fired at least two shots after what Austin Police Chief Lisa Davis described as a short foot pursuit.
  • The man was hit once in the upper torso. He was treated, released, and taken into federal custody Sunday evening.
  • An immigration attorney representing the family identified him as Wilber Rafael Garces Perez, 28, of Venezuela, and says he entered the U.S. legally and held a valid work permit. DoorDash confirmed to CNN he was making a delivery at the time.
  • A witness described an ICE Ford Expedition striking the side of a blue sedan before the officer got out and fired.
  • Homeland Security Investigations is leading the inquiry with FBI assistance. DHS says Garces Perez is stable and in custody pending removal.
  • It is at least the third shooting of a person by an ICE officer in Texas since March 2025, and comes two months after agents fatally shot a Mexican man during a Houston traffic stop.

Left view

The Texas Tribune and NPR place the shooting in a pattern tied to the administration's enforcement surge and to a running tally of agent-involved shootings. Rep. Greg Casar of Austin is demanding bodycam footage and an independent investigation, arguing ICE has not been straight with the city about what happened.

Right view

Fox News covered the incident but led on the political reaction rather than the enforcement question, framing it around the Democratic congressman's demands. The Texas Tribune notes that Gov. Greg Abbott and Texas GOP leaders have stayed publicly silent here, as they did after the July Houston shooting — the conservative position on these cases is largely being made through DHS statements defending officers' discretion rather than through elected officials.

Watch for

Whether the bodycam video is released and on what timeline; whether DOJ opens a civil rights review on top of the HSI inquiry; and whether Garces Perez's removal proceeds before the investigation concludes, which is the point his attorney is contesting. Protests have already gathered in Austin. Immigration enforcement polls as one of the administration's weaker issues heading into November.

Houthis widen the war on Saudi Arabia and the Red Sea chokepoints

Facts

  • Saudi Arabia says it intercepted a Houthi ballistic missile fired at Riyadh at dawn Saturday, the first time the capital has been targeted in this round of fighting.
  • The Houthis claimed strikes on "sensitive" sites in Riyadh and on an Aramco facility at the Red Sea port of Yanbu, using cruise and ballistic missiles plus drones.
  • Black smoke was seen from the fuel storage area at King Khalid International Airport and flights were disrupted, though Riyadh reported no casualties or damage from the intercepted missile.
  • Coalition forces say attempts on Bisha, Taif, Farasan and Yanbu were thwarted. Earlier attacks on Sept. 8 set fires at oil facilities and wounded 73 people.
  • Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree framed the attacks as retaliation for more than 700 Saudi airstrikes in Yemen.
  • Between Sept. 11 and 15 Houthi forces took Mayun, Zuqar and the Hanish islands, tightening their grip on Bab el-Mandeb — the alternative route if Hormuz stays constrained.

Left view

Al Jazeera and The Irish Times treat this as predictable spillover from the U.S.–Iran war: a conflict Washington framed as contained is now pulling in a Gulf ally's civilian infrastructure and a second maritime chokepoint. The Guardian's angle has been the humanitarian cost of the parallel Saudi air campaign in Yemen.

Right view

Fox News and the WSJ editorial page read the same facts as evidence Iran's proxy network has not been degraded — that a missile reaching Riyadh airspace after seven months of strikes argues for more pressure, not a negotiated off-ramp. Coverage on the right is leading here; mainstream U.S. coverage of the Yemen front has been comparatively thin.

Watch for

War-risk insurance premiums on Gulf and Red Sea transits, and whether Aramco keeps rerouting exports to Persian Gulf terminals — the move that pushed Brent below $105 late last week. With both Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb compromised, analysts see limited slack if a single terminal goes offline.

The Fed's first hike since 2023 puts Warsh on a collision course with Trump

Facts

  • The FOMC voted unanimously on Sept. 16 to raise the federal funds rate 25 basis points to a 3.75%–4.00% target range, the first increase in more than three years.
  • Chair Kevin Warsh said inflation has been too high for too long, and that the action supports a timelier return to the 2% goal.
  • Headline CPI held at 3.4% year over year in August; core was 2.4%. The statement described activity as expanding at a solid pace.
  • Sixteen of 18 officials pencilled in at least one more 25-basis-point hike this year; four see two more.
  • Trump publicly attacked the decision and renewed his call for cuts, while notably sparing Warsh by name.

Left view

NPR and Vox-style coverage focuses on incidence: this is a supply shock from war-driven energy prices, and raising rates cools demand and hiring without adding a barrel of oil. The worry is a Fed tightening into an affordability squeeze households are already feeling at the pump and the meat counter.

Right view

Bloomberg and the WSJ report the hike as an assertion of independence, and the WSJ editorial board's long-running position is that credibility on inflation is worth more than a short-term growth cost. Reason-adjacent commentary adds that the Fed should not be asked to accommodate a war-driven price shock. Trump administration officials counter that higher rates will worsen energy costs for consumers.

Watch for

The December meeting, and whether the 10-year Treasury — which touched 5% earlier this month — keeps climbing. Warsh's caveat is the one to track: the Fed can't stop an oil shock, but it will lean against it broadening into second-round effects. Every escalation headline out of the Gulf now feeds directly into that calculation.

DOJ's beef-price antitrust probe expands to eight big grocers

Facts

  • The Justice Department's Antitrust Division has sent information demands to Kroger, Publix, Walmart, Albertsons, Aldi, Ahold Delhaize USA, Costco and Amazon.
  • The letters, dated in July and reported in early September, seek retail beef prices, costs, margins, purchasing practices and internal market analyses.
  • It expands a May investigation into meatpackers Cargill, Tyson, JBS and National Beef, which together control roughly 85% of U.S. beef processing.
  • Tyson settled a related class action earlier this year for $82.5 million after grocers alleged supply restriction to inflate prices.
  • BLS data show beef prices rising considerably faster than overall inflation and faster than other meat, poultry and egg categories.

Left view

Mother Jones and Guardian-style coverage treats this as a concentration story with a long paper trail — four packers, eight retailers, and a settled price-fixing suit already on the books. The argument is that consolidation, not input costs, is doing the work at the register.

Right view

Reason and National Review-aligned economists are skeptical that antitrust is the right instrument, pointing to the smallest U.S. cattle herd in decades, multi-year drought in the plains, and high feed and financing costs. The Washington Examiner's framing is that an election-year probe of grocers is messaging aimed at an affordability problem the administration's own energy and tariff policy contributed to.

Watch for

Whether the voluntary letters convert into civil investigative demands or subpoenas, which would signal DOJ sees a case rather than a data-gathering exercise. Grocery prices are polling as a top-tier midterm issue, so expect both parties to work this file through November regardless of where the legal theory lands.

Six weeks to the midterms: Democrats lead the generic ballot, the Senate map doesn't cooperate

Facts

  • Election Day is Nov. 3. All 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats are on the ballot. Primaries wrapped up Sept. 15.
  • Democrats lead the generic congressional ballot by roughly 6–7 points on average; one tracker had it 50.4% to 43.2% as of Sept. 20.
  • Republicans hold 220 House seats against a 218-seat majority, so Democrats need a net gain in the low single digits.
  • In the Senate, Democrats hold 47 and need 51. Of the seats up, 23 are held by Democrats or independents who caucus with them and 11 by Republicans.
  • Sixty-one representatives and two delegates are retiring — 37 Republicans and 26 Democrats.
  • A continuing resolution signed Sept. 2 funds the government at current levels through Dec. 11, pushing the shutdown fight past the election.

Left view

Democracy Now! and Guardian coverage emphasizes the ballot-access fight running underneath the polling: a federal judge has warned that mail-in voting restrictions would produce major disenfranchisement, and DOJ has told states they must report undocumented immigrants or lose TANF funds. The argument is that the topline lead may not survive the rules.

Right view

Silver Bulletin and right-leaning analysts note that a 6–7 point generic lead is a national number that does not redistrict the map, and that the Senate class up this year is the most favorable Republican terrain of the cycle. The counterargument on the right is that the Iran war, not the economy, is the drag — a point the Fox poll showing 60% calling the war a mistake reinforces.

Watch for

Whether gas at $4.48 and the Fed's hiking cycle keep pushing affordability to the top of the issue set, which historically punishes the incumbent party. Also watch the December funding cliff: whichever party wins will be negotiating it in a lame duck with a new balance of power already known.
