AI rout: Nasdaq −5.5% into a correction as investors bet the blue wave slows the data-center boom

Facts

  • Thursday was Wall Street's worst day since 2025. The S&P 500 fell 4.36%, the Nasdaq 5.52% (now more than 10% off its October record), and the Dow 1,835 points (−3.44%) to 51,521. That wiped out Wednesday's post-midterm rally. The VIX jumped about 60% to near 27.
  • Chips led the selloff. Micron and AMD each fell about 17%, Marvell 16% and Nvidia 9%, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index had its worst day since April 2025. Roughly $2–2.5 trillion in market value was erased.
  • The trigger was a Democratic push to curb data centers. Bernie Sanders called for a national moratorium, and Texas Gov.-elect Gina Hinojosa promised a pause on new approvals. Local ballot measures restricting data centers passed almost everywhere they appeared (The Guardian counted ~40 jurisdictions in 10 states). UBS estimated about 30% of planned 2028 data-center capacity is at risk.
  • The selloff spread overnight: Japan's Nikkei fell ~6% into a bear market and Korea's Kospi dropped more than 6%. U.S. futures are mixed this morning, with Nasdaq-100 up ~1.4% and the Dow down ~0.9%.

Left view

Progressives and outlets such as The Guardian read the ballot results as a voter revolt over power bills, water use and Big Tech's influence on local zoning. Democrats including Sanders, Reps. Ansari and Casar, and Ohio Senator-elect Sherrod Brown treat curbing the buildout as a mandate, not a market risk. NPR framed the rout as fear of an "AI crackdown" rather than a verdict on the economy.

Right view

Trump blamed the "Dumocrats" on Truth Social and told voters they had only themselves to blame for the "crash." Fox Business and the Washington Examiner led with his comments. The Examiner's own reporting, though, put the drop down to AI stocks rather than the election itself. Conservative commentators argue moratoriums would hand AI leadership to China and push up energy costs in other ways.

Watch for

The October jobs report lands at 8:30 ET, with consensus at roughly 55–65K jobs and 4.2% unemployment. A weak number could deepen the slide. Quilter's Stuart Clark pointed to how concentrated the market is in a handful of tech names, which makes swings like this more likely. Bloomberg reports some strategists see the backlash as making an outright AI investment bust less likely, because it slows overbuilding. Fed-hike odds for December fell from ~83% to ~70%.

Mississippi Senate: Colom leads Hyde-Smith by ~1,100 votes, thousands of ballots to go, and there's no recount law

Facts

  • Democrat Scott Colom led Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith 464,926 to 463,788 (+1,138) as of late Wednesday, with about 96% of the expected vote counted.
  • Still outstanding: about 3,600 mail ballots, which can arrive through Nov. 10, and an unknown number of affidavit (provisional) ballots. Estimates range from 15,000 (Colom's campaign) to 18,000 (unconfirmed reports). Counties must certify by Nov. 13.
  • Mississippi has no formal recount. After certification, the loser can request a "ballot box exam" and then go to court. The NRSC has demanded officials preserve all affidavit ballots, and the state GOP chair vowed to "fight like hell."
  • If Colom wins, he would be Mississippi's first Democratic senator elected since 1982 and its first Black senator since Reconstruction.

Left view

Mississippi Free Press reports that early affidavit batches are breaking heavily for Colom (69–29 in Lafayette County), and his campaign is already projecting victory. Democrats warn against GOP efforts to throw out provisional ballots.

Right view

Hyde-Smith's campaign says it's confident once "every legally cast vote" is verified, and Gov. Tate Reeves predicts she'll win. Republicans are focused on ballot integrity and curing rules. Magnolia Tribune notes the reports of 18,000 outstanding affidavit ballots are unconfirmed.

Watch for

The affidavit-curing process and any court fight over it. The Washington Post notes the state's lack of a recount mechanism makes litigation more likely than a re-tally. The seat doesn't decide control, but it sets the size of the new Democratic Senate majority.

Newsom moves to empty California's death row, commuting all 548 sentences on his way out

Facts

  • Gov. Gavin Newsom said Thursday he will commute every death sentence in the state, 548 in all, to life without parole. Advocates call it the largest mass commutation in U.S. history.
  • For anyone with more than one felony conviction, the state constitution requires the California Supreme Court to sign off on clemency.
  • California hasn't executed anyone since 2006. Newsom imposed a moratorium in 2019 and dismantled San Quentin's chamber. Voters rejected repeal in 2016 (Prop. 62) and passed Prop. 66 to speed up executions.

Left view

The ACLU praised the move and called capital punishment racist and error-prone. It is also suing to strike down the state's death-penalty statute. Axios and KQED frame the move partly through Newsom's 2028 ambitions.

Right view

Fox News led with victims' families. Marc Klaas, whose daughter Polly's killer is on death row, called it a gift to murderers. San Luis Obispo DA Dan Dow called it an insult to victims, and LA DA Nathan Hochman noted that only voters or the Legislature can actually abolish the death penalty.

Watch for

How fast the state Supreme Court acts on the repeat-felon cases, prosecutors' legal challenges, and whether it becomes a 2028 attack line. Legally, new death sentences can still be handed down.
