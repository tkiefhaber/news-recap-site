The Big Stories Today

Nvidia beats and raises, board rules out Huang's return, and futures bounce ahead of Anthropic's IPO pricing

Facts

  • Fiscal Q3 revenue was $115.8B, up 103% from a year earlier, against about $109B expected. Adjusted EPS was $2.68 versus $2.47 expected, and data-center revenue was $107.6B (Yahoo Finance).
  • Nvidia guided Q4 revenue to about $130B, plus or minus 3%, versus roughly $125B expected. It also announced a $150B buyback and said its cloud backlog is about $2.5T. The 71–72% gross-margin guide is below last quarter's 74.9% (Yahoo Finance, MarketSpeaker).
  • Board chair Harvey Jones said the board does not intend to reinstate Jensen Huang, who resigned Nov. 12. A search for a permanent CEO is open to inside and outside candidates. Interim CEO Colette Kress said Nvidia has stopped shipping to China. She also disclosed $118B in guarantees to "neocloud" partners and receivables of $81B.
  • Shares closed down 0.4% Tuesday at $155.46, then rose about 3.7% premarket. Nasdaq-100 futures are up about 1.2%. CoreWeave rose 6.5%, Nebius 8.1% and Marvell 4.4% (Reuters via Finwire).

Center-left and wire coverage (NPR, Al Jazeera) keeps the focus on governance and China. Lawmakers, led by Democrats like Sen. Maria Cantwell, are still asking whether Commerce under Howard Lutnick let advanced chips reach Beijing. Al Jazeera notes that Trump has questioned the espionage allegations around Huang and said he'd be open to Huang returning, which critics read as politics overriding export controls.

The right is split along administration lines. Trump has signaled openness to Huang's return. Treasury Secretary Bessent has pushed for an outside CEO and blamed Commerce for lax enforcement. Bessent's camp also has the backing of Republican China hawks like Sen. Tim Scott, who wants Lutnick and Huang to testify Dec. 3. Pro-market voices point to the beat as proof that AI demand is intact despite the scandal.

Watch for

Morningstar's Brian Colello called the print "business as usual" and expects it to calm transition jitters. The open questions are leadership (who and when) and balance-sheet risk from the neocloud guarantees and rising receivables. Anthropic prices its IPO tonight and trades Thursday. Bloomberg reports demand near 7x the shares on offer, with pricing at or above the top of the $70–78 range. The WSJ reports it could go above $80, a valuation of roughly $1.4T–1.6T. A strong debut would extend today's AI relief rally, and a sloppy one would revive bubble talk.

Xi greets Trump on the tarmac in Shenzhen after Manila deals; the Trump–Xi–Putin meeting is Thursday

Facts

  • In an unusual gesture, Xi Jinping met Trump at the foot of Air Force One in Shenzhen early Wednesday. Xi said he wants a "constructive, strategically stable" relationship. Putin was met by a lower-ranking official, Cai Qi (NYT, NBC, CNN via NewsCord).
  • In Manila, the Quad (U.S., Japan, India, Australia) announced new critical-minerals deals and a 2027 maritime patrol-aircraft exercise. It also pledged more than $85M toward AI access for Indo-Pacific partners and voiced concern about "economic coercion" without naming China (ABC Australia, Philstar).
  • Trump and Thai PM Anutin signed a joint trade statement. Thailand would drop tariffs on U.S. goods and buy about $2.6B more in U.S. farm products, with a formal deal expected within weeks. After meeting Marcos, Trump also announced a "very big" Philippines trade deal but gave no details. Manila wants the 12.5% U.S. tariff on its exports cut (ABC News, Philstar).
  • The first-ever three-way meeting with Xi and Putin is Thursday. The White House says the focus is denuclearization and Iran. U.S. officials say Ukraine likely isn't on the agenda, even though Trump has said he'd raise it. The U.S.–China trade truce runs through Jan. 10, 2027 (Fox News, Al Jazeera).

NPR's coverage puts the Nvidia fallout and the wave of U.S. tech outages next to the trip and reports the outages disrupted it. Al Jazeera quotes Brookings' Ryan Hass, who says a three-way meeting would recast Putin as a major player rather than a pariah. It also cites GMF analysts warning that Trump's delay of a $14B Taiwan arms package invites Beijing to expect more concessions. Critics also flag that Ukraine has been left off the agenda.

Right view

Fox News frames the trip as a "historic," high-stakes swing while noting low expectations for concrete results. It highlights Trump's openness to a Kim Jong Un drop-in. Rep. John Moolenaar (R), chair of the House China committee, told Fox he expects no nuclear-reduction deal because China "has resisted" such agreements, though he still sees value in the talks. National Review argues the summit should be used to renew a U.S.-led order rather than carve up spheres of influence.

Watch for

Crisis Group's Ali Wyne and Teneo's Gabriel Wildau expect atmosphere, not breakthroughs. The market-relevant items are any extension of the Jan. 10 truce, rare-earth flows (U.S. imports from China are still about 75% below pre-restriction levels, per CSIS), soybean purchases, and any Taiwan or chip-control language. Xi is also pushing a global AI governance body at APEC as a counterweight to the U.S. Trump has claimed the U.S. will "catch" China on rare earths within 10 months.

Iran widens its Gulf attacks to a U.S. base in Kuwait after a U.S. strike on Qeshm, while a reported "dirty bomb" plot rattles Washington

Facts

  • On Wednesday, Iran fired on Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait and hit at U.S. facilities in Bahrain several times. Kuwait and Bahrain say they intercepted the drones and report no immediate damage. Kuwait's foreign ministry called the attacks "criminal" (AP).
  • The attacks followed a U.S. fighter strike Tuesday on an IRGC missile-launch site on Qeshm Island. That strike answered Iran's attack that morning on a U.S. radar site in Bahrain. Separately, ADNOC says one crew member is missing and 11 were injured after an attack on its tanker in the Strait of Hormuz (AP, Gulf News).
  • CNN, citing seven officials, reports that U.S. intelligence shows Iran developing options to strike senior administration officials in Washington with a dirty bomb or chemical weapon. The officials described it as contingency planning with no sign of an imminent attack, and they disagree with each other on Iran's capability. The White House says there are "no credible threats to the homeland," Trump says he takes it "very seriously," and Iran calls the report baseless (Al Jazeera, CNBC).
  • Elsewhere in the region, Saudi Arabia intercepted two Houthi cruise missiles aimed at Riyadh, and a coalition air raid in Yemen reportedly killed at least 17 people (AP).

Senate Democrats are pressing to cut off war funding (see shutdown story below).

The Washington Examiner editorial board argues that Iran's lashing out shows the blockade is working and gives Trump leverage for a deal. In National Review, Noah Rothman says the blockade is squeezing Tehran and is also exposing rifts over Vice President Vance's earlier outreach.

Crisis Group's Hamidreza Azizi warns that piled-up incidents raise the risk of miscalculation. Any U.S. casualty would be the trigger. NSA's classified network goes down amid a string of U.S. tech outages, and lawmakers in both parties demand answers

Facts

  • NSANet, the agency's highly classified internal network, suffered an outage Tuesday. CNN broke the story, citing five sources. The NSA said the network saw "temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance." The WSJ and AP tied it to a software update or patching. Sources say there's no sign of a cyberattack, and backup networks kept communications with the traveling president intact (CNN, Fox News).
  • It caps about five days of unrelated-looking failures: last week's multi-day Microsoft 365/Outlook outage, Friday's FAA nationwide ground stop, a Chase software bug Monday that also hit other banks, plus Apple, Gmail and Marriott disruptions (Fox News live, Sinclair).
  • DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin says the outages are unrelated, and Trump said he isn't concerned. FBI cyber teams are assisting the FAA and contractor L3Harris (Fox News, Sinclair).
  • The outage news helped push stocks to session lows Tuesday. The Dow was down more than 1,000 points at one point (MarketSpeaker).

Left view

House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries says it's "time for the administration to come up to the Hill and explain themselves." In CNN's coverage, former FBI deputy director Andrew McCabe says the combined disruptions are hard to dismiss, especially with officials weighing the reported Iranian plot.

Right view

Some on the right don't buy the official line. Sen. Josh Hawley (R-Mo.) says the incidents don't look like coincidence and that "we need answers," made public.

Watch for

Expect oversight letters and hearing requests, which Democrats can force once they take control of both chambers in January. The FAA/L3Harris review is ongoing. Lame-duck shutdown fight takes shape: Democrats eye the Dec. 11 deadline to cut off Iran war funds

Facts

  • Government funding expires Dec. 11, and none of the 12 annual spending bills has been enacted. Less than four weeks remain before the new Democratic-controlled Congress is sworn in on Jan. 3 (HNGN, The Fiscal Times).
  • Senate Democrats including Patty Murray, Tim Kaine, Bernie Sanders and Chris Van Hollen say they'll oppose any stopgap that lets the administration keep spending on the Iran war, now in its ninth month. Kaine is drafting language to block those funds (The Hill).
  • Trump wants Republicans to pass a $350B defense package on party lines through reconciliation, covering munitions, critical minerals and Golden Dome. Majority Leader Thune says the votes aren't there, and the House Freedom Caucus wants at least $1T in offsetting cuts (HNGN, The Hill).

Left view

The Hill and progressive outlets frame the midterm results as a mandate to end the war and the spending deadline as the main leverage before January. Sen. Richard Blumenthal says a short-term bill must come with action to end the conflict. Reason, from the libertarian side, also argues that Democrats can use the power of the purse to stop the war now.

Right view

The Washington Examiner reports that even the party's "staunchest" war critics won't vote to defund it. Republicans argue that an abrupt cutoff endangers U.S. personnel without a coordinated drawdown. GOP senators including Rounds, Tillis and Curtis have each raised concerns about the reconciliation push, and some House Republicans want a tougher Russia line before backing it.

Watch for

The likeliest path is a short stopgap into the new Congress. The fight is over whether it carries war-funding conditions. A shutdown would land as markets already price a December Fed hike, adding data delays (as in 2025) to a jittery tape. The defense policy bill ($1.15T request) and about 25 pending judicial confirmations are also competing for floor time.
