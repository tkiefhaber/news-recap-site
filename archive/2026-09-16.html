The Big Stories Today

Fed decides today, and Wall Street expects Warsh to hike into Trump's objections

Facts

  • The FOMC announces its decision at 2 p.m. ET today, closing a two-day meeting. Futures markets put the odds of a quarter-point increase above 90%, per CNBC.
  • That would lift the target range to 3.75%–4.00% and mark the first Fed rate increase since 2023. The rate has sat at 3.50%–3.75% since December 2025.
  • Chair Kevin Warsh set up the move at Jackson Hole on Aug. 28, warning that underlying inflation is not slowing. CPI ran 3.4% in the twelve months through July; the Fed's preferred PCE gauge ran 3.7%.
  • Trump, Vice President Vance, the Treasury secretary and a senior White House economic counselor have all publicly urged the Fed not to raise rates over the past two weeks — an unusually broad pressure campaign. Trump said he would not have picked Warsh if he wanted hikes.
  • Warsh holds a press conference after the decision.

Left view

NPR and NBC News frame this as the test of whether the Fed still works: Trump hand-picked Warsh expecting a dove, and the institution's credibility now rests on Warsh doing the unpopular thing anyway. NPR's coverage notes the awkward position — Warsh's independence has to be demonstrated by defying the president who appointed him, which is a thin kind of independence. The Guardian and others tie the inflation problem back to administration policy itself: tariffs and an energy shock driven by a war the White House is prosecuting.

Right view

The Washington Times and Fox Business coverage cast the hike as the Fed cleaning up after years of accommodation, with Warsh's hawkishness the reason conservatives wanted him in the first place. Trump's own line — that the ruling class at the Fed is strangling growth before the midterms — gets amplified on the populist right, but institutionalist conservatives at outlets like the WSJ editorial page have argued that a Fed that caves to a sitting president is worth less than one that hikes.

Watch for

Economists quoted by CNBC and Fortune say the real signal is the dot plot and Warsh's press conference, not the 25 basis points. If he leaves the door open to a second hike, the 10-year could push past 5.10% and mortgage rates follow within days. If he frames it as a one-off insurance move against the energy shock, expect a relief rally in equities. The political tail risk analysts keep flagging: a public Trump attack on Warsh, or renewed talk of removing a governor, which is what drove the Lisa Cook fight last year. Rate-sensitive sectors — housing, regional banks, small caps — move first.

The 10-year sits at 5% — and the AI borrowing binge is part of why

Facts

  • The 10-year Treasury yield touched 5.04% Tuesday, the highest since 2007, and closed the New York session at 5.00%, per Bloomberg.
  • Stocks fell for the sixth time in seven sessions. The S&P 500 lost 0.45% to 7,585.73; the Dow fell 328.09 points to 52,093.11; the Nasdaq dropped 0.78% to 25,981.57.
  • Three forces are pushing yields up at once: the energy shock from the Middle East, the expected Fed hike, and a flood of long-dated corporate issuance from AI companies.
  • Hyperscalers have issued roughly $132B–$220B of AI-related debt so far in 2026 by various counts, including a multitranche deal near $53B — among the largest corporate bond sales on record — and a rare 100-year bond. Full-year estimates for the broader AI ecosystem run $300B–$570B.
  • The Bank for International Settlements named an AI capex bust one of its top three global financial stability risks in its 2026 annual report.

Left view

Vox and The Atlantic have run the argument that this is a privately funded bubble with publicly borne costs: a handful of firms are crowding out the bond market and bidding up the cost of every mortgage and car loan in the country to build data centers whose revenue case is still unproven. The Guardian adds the energy angle — the same buildout is straining grids and raising utility bills in states hosting the centers.

Right view

The WSJ editorial page and National Review treat the yield move as a sovereign-debt story first: investors are demanding more to hold US paper because Washington keeps borrowing, and blaming AI firms for market-priced capital is backwards. Reason and The Dispatch have argued the AI capex is genuine productive investment, and that a 5% risk-free rate is closer to historical normal than the decade of near-zero that preceded it.

Watch for

Vanguard and JPMorgan strategists flag the same tell: whether new AI deals still clear at reasonable spreads. Bond investors have started demanding wider concessions, and a failed or heavily discounted mega-deal would be the first real crack. On the consumer side, 30-year mortgages tracking a 5% 10-year put housing affordability back at 2023 lows — watch existing-home sales and homebuilder guidance. And if yields stay here into Q4, expect renewed pressure on Treasury's issuance mix.

Houthis complete control of Bab el-Mandeb; Brent above $107

Facts

  • Houthi forces have seized Perim (Mayun) Island, the port of Mokha, and the Greater and Lesser Hanish islands, giving them effective control of both sides of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, per NPR, NBC News and PBS.
  • Roughly 12% of global trade and about 7% of global petroleum supply normally transit the strait.
  • The group declared a naval blockade of Saudi shipping in the Red Sea on July 20. A Houthi attack on a cargo ship in the strait last month killed six — the first fatalities from Red Sea shipping attacks in over a year.
  • A Saudi oil pipeline struck in an attack is out of service; repairs are estimated at three to five weeks. Saudi Arabia had been routing exports through the Red Sea as an alternative to the Strait of Hormuz, where Iran has been targeting vessels.
  • Brent settled around $107.46 Tuesday. Trump has described the US naval response as a "wall of steel"; the Houthis dispute his characterization of the situation.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian frame this as the predictable cost of an open-ended war with Iran: the US has spent months striking Iranian assets and now faces a proxy that has taken territory rather than been degraded. The Christian Science Monitor's reporting emphasizes that naval escort operations protect ships but cannot dislodge a force holding the shoreline, and that there is no stated endgame. MSNBC commentary ties the gas price at the pump directly to the administration's escalation choices heading into the midterms.

Right view

Fox News and the New York Post argue the opposite causal story: years of accommodating Iran and treating the Houthis as a nuisance rather than a state-backed military let this happen, and the answer is heavier strikes, not withdrawal. The Free Press and National Review have pushed for re-designating and directly targeting Houthi leadership, and criticize the 2025 Oman-brokered ceasefire that shielded American ships but left Israeli-linked shipping exposed — which, they argue, taught the Houthis that attacks pay.

Watch for

Energy analysts cited by CNBC and CSIS say the near-term question is insurance: war-risk premiums for Red Sea transits are the mechanism that actually closes a strait, and a further spike pushes carriers back around the Cape, adding 10–14 days and cost to Asia–Europe freight. Watch Saudi export loadings and whether Riyadh restarts the damaged pipeline on schedule. A move above $110 Brent feeds straight into US pump prices with a two-to-three-week lag, which lands in late October — weeks before the midterms. Also watch whether the administration expands the naval mission, which would require either a new authorization fight or another executive-authority argument in Congress.

Supreme Court blocks Trump's mail-ballot rules for the midterms

Facts

  • The Supreme Court on Monday refused to lift a lower-court block on new USPS rules governing mail ballots, leaving existing procedures in place for the Nov. 3 midterms.
  • The rules, which followed a Trump executive order, would have required states to hand the Postal Service lists of voters' names and addresses and to use specific barcodes and envelope designs before ballots could be delivered.
  • The order was unsigned and three sentences long, saying the administration is unlikely to succeed on the merits. Justice Kavanaugh wrote separately that even if the rule might fall within USPS authority, applying it this cycle would be unlawful because election officials lack time to implement it.
  • Justices Alito and Thomas dissented. Justice Barrett joined the majority.
  • Trump called it a "big loss for Republicans, and America itself," and praised Alito and Thomas. Separately, the Court heard argument this week on whether states may count mail ballots arriving after Election Day.

Left view

The Washington Post and NPR report this as a guardrail holding: an attempt to rewrite ballot rules months before an election, through the Postal Service rather than Congress or the states, was stopped by a Court with a conservative majority. Democracy Now! and the Brennan Center-aligned commentary emphasize the voter-list handover, which they characterize as a federal database of mail voters assembled without statutory authority.

Right view

Fox News documented a real split on the right: populist commentators attacked Barrett personally — one called her the biggest judicial disappointment since Souter — while the Daily Caller and others reported the ruling straight as a timing loss rather than a merits loss, noting Kavanaugh explicitly left the door open for the rule in a later cycle. Election-integrity groups including the Honest Elections Project are focused on the separate late-arriving-ballot case, where conservatives think they have the better argument.

Watch for

The late-ballot case is the one with real consequences: a ruling that federal law requires receipt by Election Day would invalidate deadlines in roughly a dozen states and could decide close House races. Analysts expect a decision before November given the timing. Also watch whether the administration reissues a narrower rule for 2028, and whether the Barrett backlash shapes how the White House talks about the Court through the campaign.

House kills Al Green's impeachment push, with 64 Democrats helping

Facts

  • The House voted 232–147 on Tuesday to table H.Res. 1486, Rep. Al Green's privileged resolution to impeach President Trump. Forty-seven members voted present.
  • Sixty-four Democrats joined Republicans to table, including the 47 voting present. Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries voted present.
  • Green's articles centered on the killing of two US citizens by federal immigration officers earlier this year.
  • This is the latest in a series of privileged impeachment resolutions Green has forced to the floor this Congress; each has been tabled.

Left view

Axios reported the story primarily as a Democratic leadership problem — Jeffries and most of the caucus want the midterms fought on cost of living, not impeachment, and a floor vote that fails makes the party look divided. Progressive outlets including Mother Jones argue the underlying grievance is serious enough that leadership's procedural dodge is itself the story, and that the deaths at issue have not received a real congressional inquiry.

Right view

Just the News and the Washington Examiner led with the Democratic defections, framing the 64 votes as evidence that even Trump's opponents regard impeachment as theater. Republican members including Rep. Walberg issued statements calling the resolution a distraction from appropriations work. The broader right-leaning argument is that repeated failed impeachment votes cheapen the tool and help Trump.

Watch for

Practically nothing changes in the near term — the resolution is dead and leadership on both sides wants it to stay dead. The number to watch is whether Green files again before November, which would force vulnerable Democrats in swing districts into a second uncomfortable vote. The immigration-enforcement deaths remain live as a litigation and oversight matter regardless of the impeachment vehicle.

Judge blocks DHS limits on student, academic and journalist visas

Facts

  • US District Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV in Massachusetts temporarily blocked a DHS rule capping the duration of several visa categories.
  • The rule would have capped F student visas and J exchange visas at four years, and cut I visas for foreign journalists — currently open-ended in practice — to a maximum of 240 days.
  • Saylor called the government's national-security justification "exceptionally weak" and said DHS gave no reasonable explanation for the journalist cap specifically.
  • The block is temporary while a suit by a coalition of educational organizations and unions proceeds.

Left view

The Guardian and Democracy Now! frame the journalist provision as press-freedom-adjacent: a 240-day cap makes it structurally impossible for foreign correspondents to cover a full US election cycle, which they argue is the point. Higher-education groups quoted by NPR emphasize the enrollment and research consequences — international students are a significant share of graduate STEM programs and of university revenue.

Right view

Right-leaning coverage of this one is thin so far, which is itself worth noting. The general immigration-restrictionist case, made at the Washington Examiner and by groups like the Center for Immigration Studies, is that open-ended visa durations invite overstays and that fixed terms with renewal are normal in most countries. There is also a recurring conservative complaint about single district judges issuing nationwide blocks on federal immigration rules — expect that procedural argument on appeal.

Watch for

An emergency appeal to the First Circuit is the near-certain next step, and this administration has shown a pattern of going to the Supreme Court's emergency docket quickly. Universities are already into the spring admissions cycle, so the practical question is whether the block holds through the application window. If the rule is eventually revived, the immediate effect falls on students already mid-program who would need to reapply.
