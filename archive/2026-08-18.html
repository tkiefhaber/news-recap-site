The Big Stories Today

Another ship struck in the Strait of Hormuz as Iran hardens its reopening demands

Facts

  • The UK Maritime Trade Operations centre reported a bulk carrier hit by a projectile in the strait, the latest in a string of attacks. The UAE foreign ministry said a vessel affiliated with state oil company ADNOC was attacked while transiting late last week.
  • Iran's Supreme National Security Council says the waterway will not reopen until the US "corrects its behavior," adding demands that include an end to military threats and sanctions plus compensation. Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi framed the strait as Iranian territory to open or close at Tehran's discretion.
  • Roughly a fifth of global seaborne oil moved through the strait before the war. Traffic remains near a standstill and countries have been drawing down stockpiles.
  • Brent crude has traded near $90 a barrel, up roughly 24% from levels before the US–Israel–Iran fighting began in late February.

Left view

NPR and Al Jazeera have centered the household cost: pump and grocery prices climbing while oil majors book windfall profits, feeding Democratic proposals for a windfall-profits tax rebated to lower-income Americans. The Guardian and MSNBC frame the closure as the predictable cost of a war Congress never authorized — the House has now passed two war-powers rebukes, and a Senate version failed 47–49. DNC chair Ken Martin's line, echoed across left outlets, is that the administration is downplaying an affordability crisis it created.

Right view

The sharpest criticism this week is coming from the right, not the left. The Wall Street Journal editorial board has repeatedly hit Trump for trading away leverage without securing the strait — arguing he has twice announced a reopening and twice given something up, while Iran keeps escalating and adding demands. National Review and The Free Press make a similar hawkish case for enforcement over concessions. Fox News and the White House counter that the pressure campaign is working and that Iran's economy cannot outlast a US-led effort to refill global stocks and wait Tehran out.

Watch for

Energy analysts quoted by CNBC and Reuters say markets have not fully priced a permanent closure — confidence in a near-term deal is eroding but not gone, so headlines still move crude several dollars a day. A Brent break above $95–$100 would push the national gas average toward $4.40–$4.50 within weeks given the lag from crude to pump. That path also complicates the Fed: futures have swung from expecting 2026 cuts to pricing possible hikes. Watch insurance rates for Gulf transits and whether more Gulf-state-flagged vessels are targeted, which would widen the conflict beyond US–Iran.

Ukraine launches one of the war's largest drone barrages; Russia hits back at Danube port

Facts

  • Ukraine launched roughly 822 drones at Russian targets, with more than 600 directed at the Moscow region — described by Russian state agency TASS as the largest drone attack on the capital in two years.
  • Russian officials reported at least six to seven people killed and dozens injured. Moscow's defense ministry claimed a record 1,478 Ukrainian drones downed over 24 hours; Mayor Sergei Sobyanin said 180 were shot down over the capital region overnight.
  • A large fire consumed a distribution warehouse belonging to Russian e-commerce giant Wildberries near Moscow. Ukraine's military said it also struck a missile-fuel production facility in Rostov region.
  • Russia retaliated against a Ukrainian Danube river port. Separately, a Spanish F/A-18 operating with NATO shot down a Russian drone in Romanian airspace near Galați after it crossed from Moldova.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian emphasize that Ukraine's deep-strike capability has matured to the point of imposing real economic cost inside Russia — logistics hubs, refineries, fuel plants — and treat that as evidence continued Western support is producing leverage rather than stalemate. The Atlantic's framing is that Russian air defenses are being saturated, and that the civilian deaths in Russia are a consequence Moscow invited.

Right view

Coverage on the right splits. The Wall Street Journal news pages and National Review highlight the strikes as proof that pressure works and that supplying long-range capability is cheaper than a frozen conflict. Restraint-minded outlets — Reason, and parts of The Free Press — focus instead on the NATO-airspace incident over Romania, arguing each intercept by an allied jet edges the alliance closer to direct involvement with no plan for what follows.

Watch for

Analysts expect Russia to respond against Ukrainian port and energy infrastructure, which historically pushes European gas and grain futures. The Romania incident is the one to watch for escalation risk: repeated Article 4 consultations would be the tell. Also watch whether the Wildberries and Rostov strikes dent Russian domestic fuel supply — refinery outages there have previously forced export curbs that tighten global diesel.

Kushner meets Hamas leadership in Egypt as Gaza disarmament talks reach a decision point

Facts

  • Jared Kushner met Hamas chief Khalil al-Hayya in El-Alamein, Egypt, alongside a Board of Peace delegation, in a push to advance the next phase of Trump's 20-point Gaza plan.
  • Kushner and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu agreed that "demilitarization" should begin with a Hamas weapons handover supervised by an American military general, according to a source cited by NBC News.
  • Hamas has said it will begin disarming only if Netanyahu publicly commits to the plan's terms. Netanyahu has said publicly there will be no Israeli withdrawals until Hamas is fully disarmed; a Board of Peace source told Axios he has shown more flexibility privately.
  • IDF strikes in Gaza have continued during the talks. Axios reported Kushner urging Netanyahu to test Hamas's willingness rather than assume bad faith.

Left view

The Guardian and MSNBC question whether a US general supervising a weapons handover is a workable mechanism or a way to defer the hard question of Palestinian governance, and press on the humanitarian toll of strikes continuing mid-negotiation. Vox frames the sequencing dispute — disarm first vs. withdraw first — as the same deadlock that has collapsed prior rounds, with civilians absorbing the delay.

Right view

Fox News has run the argument that Hamas is the "ultimate spoiler," pocketing concessions while never intending to disarm, and that Netanyahu's no-withdrawal-first position is the only enforceable one. The Jerusalem Post and Washington Examiner treat the Cairo channel as vindication of the pressure-plus-dealmaking approach. The Free Press and National Review are more skeptical of the American-general mechanism, warning it puts US personnel in the middle of enforcement without a clear mandate.

Watch for

The near-term test is whether Netanyahu makes the public commitment Hamas has demanded — that is the gating item. Regional analysts expect Egypt and Qatar to press for a phased handover tied to specific Israeli pullback lines. A functioning first phase would ease some of the Gulf risk premium in oil; a collapse would likely be read alongside the Hormuz standoff as evidence the administration's regional track is stalling, which is how markets have been treating Middle East headlines this month.

Trump orders the Pentagon to sharply scale back joint exercises with South Korea

Facts

  • Trump directed the Pentagon to "substantially reduce" joint US–South Korea military exercises, announcing it hours before the drills were set to begin.
  • He cited the cost of the exercises, his "very good" relationship with Kim Jong Un, and a desire not to send a signal he called inappropriate and hostile toward North Korea. He also faulted Seoul for not supporting the US effort against Iran.
  • Sen. Mark Kelly (D-Ariz.) and other lawmakers criticized the move, arguing the drills are central to defending South Korea. Defense analysts warned it lowers coalition readiness and slows Seoul's path to taking on more of its own defense.
  • The Washington Post reported the decision has rattled the foreign-policy establishment in both capitals.

Left view

The Washington Post and NBC News frame it as unilateral concession to Pyongyang with nothing received in return, and as punishment of an ally for declining to join a war Congress never authorized. NPR and The Atlantic connect it to a broader pattern of transactional alliance management that they argue erodes deterrence in the Pacific while China watches.

Right view

This one splits the right unusually cleanly. Restraint-oriented conservatives at Reason and parts of The American Conservative back it as overdue burden-shifting — Seoul is wealthy and should carry more of its own defense. Traditional hawks at National Review, the Washington Examiner and the WSJ editorial page call it a readiness cost paid for nothing, and note the stated rationale (South Korea's non-participation in Iran) links two unrelated theaters in a way allies will read as unreliability.

Watch for

Watch South Korea's defense budget and any renewed domestic debate over indigenous nuclear capability — polling there has trended that direction whenever US commitment is questioned. Analysts also expect Japan to seek firmer written assurances. Near-term market effect is limited, but defense-sector analysts flag it as a positive for South Korean and Japanese defense primes and a question mark for US force-posture funding in the next NDAA.

Justice Department watchdog opens review of DEA tactic that let fentanyl reach the street

Facts

  • DOJ Inspector General Don Berthiaume announced a review of a practice in which the DEA learned of fentanyl deliveries in advance through wiretaps but declined to seize them, in order to build larger trafficking cases.
  • The review covers the department's actions over the past two years and may examine whether the tactic was used in cities beyond Albuquerque.
  • It follows an Associated Press investigation finding DEA agents repeatedly monitored but did not intercept major fentanyl shipments in New Mexico between 2023 and 2025.
  • It is among Berthiaume's first official acts; he was sworn in last Friday.

Left view

NPR and the Guardian frame this as the enforcement-first drug strategy failing on its own terms — federal agents choosing prosecutions over preventing overdose deaths in a specific, traceable set of communities. Mother Jones and Vox have used cases like this to argue for shifting resources toward treatment and harm reduction rather than case-building.

Right view

The New York Post and Washington Examiner treat it primarily as a federal-agency accountability story — bureaucratic incentives rewarding big headline busts over public safety. Reason goes further, citing it as evidence the drug war's structure predictably produces this trade-off. Notably, there is little partisan defense of the tactic itself; the disagreement is about what it proves, not whether it happened.

Watch for

Expect congressional letters from Judiciary Committee members on both sides — Grassley and Durbin have jointly pressed DEA oversight before. The practical question is whether the review expands geographically; if it does, it becomes a much larger story and could constrain wiretap-based case strategy nationally. Timeline for IG reviews of this scope typically runs six to twelve months.

Fallout continues from the executive order rewriting the childhood vaccine schedule

Facts

  • The "Gold Standard Childhood Vaccine Recommendations" order, signed Aug. 10, calls for cutting universally recommended childhood shots from 17 to 11 and splitting the MMR vaccine into three separate injections.
  • Trump tied the order to concerns about a vaccine–autism link, a hypothesis contradicted by large-scale studies.
  • The order removes the universal recommendation for flu vaccination and for COVID and bacterial meningitis shots, according to Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand.
  • Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-La.), a physician, publicly called the order wrong, saying the president lacks the expertise to make the change and that vaccines are safe, effective, and do not cause autism.

Left view

ABC News, CNN and NPR lead with physician organizations: the American College of Physicians' Dr. Jan K. Carney called it part of a pattern of changing vaccine guidance by fiat rather than through transparent scientific review. Left outlets emphasize the practical stakes heading into fall — school requirements, insurance coverage tied to recommendation status, and flu season.

Right view

The right is genuinely divided here, and it is worth noting the most quoted critic is a Republican senator. Cassidy's objection has been amplified by The Dispatch, National Review and the WSJ editorial page as an example of executive overreach into clinical judgment. Fox News and other pro-administration outlets frame it as restoring parental choice and forcing overdue scrutiny of a schedule they say expanded without sufficient public debate.

Watch for

The operative question is implementation: recommendation status drives insurance coverage under the ACA preventive-services rules and feeds into state school-entry requirements, so the practical effect depends on whether ACIP and CDC formally adopt the changes. Public-health analysts expect state-level divergence, with several states writing their own schedules. Vaccine-maker analysts at Bloomberg and BioPharma Dive are watching flu and MMR volume guidance for the fall as the first measurable signal.
