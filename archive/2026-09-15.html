The Big Stories Today

10-year Treasury hits 5% as the Fed opens a meeting markets expect to end in a rate hike

Facts

  • The 10-year Treasury yield touched 5.0% on Monday before easing back — its highest since October 2023, and a level otherwise unseen since 2007. It traded near 5.04% early Tuesday.
  • The FOMC's two-day meeting begins today, with the decision and Chair Kevin Warsh's press conference Wednesday afternoon. Futures put the odds of a 25bp hike around 90%. The target range has sat at 3.50%–3.75% since a divided 9–3 hold in July.
  • It would be the Fed's first increase since 2023. August CPI came in at +0.4% for the month and 3.4% year over year, with core at +0.3%; energy did most of the work. Goldman Sachs flipped its call from hold to hike after that print.
  • Stocks closed lower Monday on a chip selloff, and futures were down again Tuesday morning.
  • The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate rose to 6.76% last week, up from 6.15% at the start of the year.

Left view

NPR and CNN Business frame the yield move as the bill coming due for an energy shock the administration's Iran policy helped create — households face higher mortgage, auto and card costs for a war they didn't vote for. Commentators on the left also note the political trap: the Fed is being asked to raise rates into a midterm year, and the White House spent the past 18 months arguing rates should be lower.

Right view

The Wall Street Journal editorial page and Washington Times coverage put the emphasis on inflation being above target since the Biden years and argue Warsh's credibility depends on acting rather than talking. Analysts at CNBC framed the same point more bluntly: having warned about prices for months, a hold now would revive questions about who is actually setting Fed policy.

Watch for

The dot plot lands with the decision Wednesday and is the real signal — one hike or a series. Bloomberg reported strategist Brian Barrow, who called 5% early, expects the selloff to run further. If yields hold above 5%, expect knock-on pressure in mortgages, regional bank balance sheets and highly levered corporate credit, plus a stronger dollar that squeezes emerging-market borrowers. Watch also whether Trump attacks the decision publicly; he has pressured the Fed repeatedly.

Trump rejects the AI industry's own call for a slowdown

Facts

  • Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published a roughly 3,800-word essay Saturday arguing the race to build frontier AI needs to slow, with international coordination and new government rules, because safety work is falling behind capability.
  • OpenAI's Sam Altman said companies need to pace the frontier; Elon Musk said Amodei was right; Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis called the direction correct.
  • Trump rejected the idea on Truth Social Monday, writing that the only guardrail AI needs is a strong, smart president, and later warning against killing the golden goose. He compared the warnings to past climate alarm and called the push against AI data centers a conspiracy.
  • The Washington Post reported that Anthropic, OpenAI and Google have discussed creating a new joint AI safety body.
  • Congress is not moving quickly. A Republican proposal for a 10-year moratorium on state AI regulation remains live; Amodei has called that approach too blunt.

Left view

NPR and CNN frame this as the president siding with growth over catastrophic-risk warnings coming from the builders themselves — the unusual fact being that the people asking for brakes are the ones with the most to lose. Left-leaning commentary ties it to the departure of the US AI Safety Institute's head and argues the administration has hollowed out federal capacity to evaluate the models it is cheering on.

Right view

Fox News casts Trump as defending American competitiveness against China, on the logic that a US slowdown is a Chinese speedup. But the right is split: Reason argues the executives asking for international regulation could simply slow down on their own and don't need Washington to do it, and Townhall warns that federal rules would hand incumbents a government-protected moat. The libertarian critique and the nationalist one land in the same place — no new federal AI regime.

Watch for

Whether the voluntary safety body materializes with real commitments or stays a talking point; whether the state-preemption moratorium gets attached to a moving vehicle in Congress before the midterms; and whether AI capex sentiment cracks — Monday's chip selloff showed the debate now moves semiconductor prices, not just op-ed pages. Data-center siting fights at the state and local level are the near-term flashpoint.

Supreme Court blocks Trump's mail-ballot rules for the midterms

Facts

  • The Supreme Court on Monday declined to lift lower-court injunctions against Trump's March executive order directing the Postal Service to impose uniform ballot and envelope requirements in federal elections.
  • The order would also have required states to hand USPS lists of mail and absentee voters 60 days before Election Day, and would have let the Postal Service decline to deliver ballots that did not conform.
  • Justice Brett Kavanaugh wrote that applying the rule to the 2026 elections would be arbitrary and capricious, because state and local election officials lack time to implement it.
  • Roughly two dozen Democratic-led states sued in April; both cases went to the same federal judge in Boston, who enjoined the order. A second judge issued a separate block on Sept. 3.
  • The ruling is about timing for this cycle, not a final judgment on the order's legality. An earlier decision in August had let other parts of the order take effect.

Left view

NPR and CNN report it as a significant defeat for an effort that would have given the Postal Service unprecedented power to withhold millions of ballots, and note the practical relief for election administrators who have been planning under uncertainty since spring. Democratic state AGs are presenting it as proof the courts still function as a check.

Right view

Coverage on the right emphasizes that the Court ruled on timing and implementability, not on the merits of uniform federal ballot standards, and that the administration retains the parts of the order already cleared in August. Conservative election-integrity groups argue the underlying goal — consistent ballot handling across states — survives and returns after November.

Watch for

Election officials now finalize mail-ballot procedures under existing state law, which removes a major operational unknown roughly seven weeks out. The merits litigation continues and could reach the Court after the midterms. Expect the administration to look for narrower administrative routes at USPS in the meantime, and expect mail-ballot rules to remain a live campaign theme in close Senate races.

EPA repeals power plant climate rules — and moves to bar future ones

Facts

  • EPA announced Monday it is repealing the Biden-era greenhouse gas standards for coal- and gas-fired power plants, which required certain coal plants to cut emissions 90% by 2032 and new gas plants to offset most of theirs.
  • In a separate but linked action, the agency proposes to find that the Clean Air Act does not authorize EPA to regulate power-plant greenhouse gases for climate purposes at all — a step intended to constrain future administrations.
  • EPA estimates the rescission saves consumers $310 billion.
  • The Natural Resources Defense Council said within hours it will sue to block the repeal.

Left view

NPR and the Washington Post lead on the second action as the more consequential one: repealing a rule is ordinary policy reversal, but declaring the authority itself nonexistent is an attempt to take the question off the table permanently. Environmental groups dispute the $310 billion figure as counting compliance costs while ignoring health and climate damages.

Right view

The Daily Signal and allied outlets frame it as a legal correction — the agency conceding it exceeded statutory authority rather than making a policy choice — and tie it to electricity reliability and price, an argument with extra force while gas and power demand rise alongside AI data-center buildout. The savings estimate is presented as relief for ratepayers.

Watch for

Litigation is certain and the authority question is squarely aimed at the Supreme Court's major-questions line of cases; a ruling endorsing EPA's reading would outlast this administration. Near term, watch utility capital plans and coal retirement schedules, several of which were built around the 2032 deadline, and watch whether states with their own carbon rules move to fill the gap.

Russian drone hits Warsaw-bound train two kilometers from Poland

Facts

  • A Russian strike drone hit the locomotive of a Warsaw-bound passenger train in Ukraine's Volyn region Sunday, about two kilometers from the Polish border near the Yahodyn crossing.
  • The train carried 206 passengers. There were no casualties; a monitoring team's advance warning is credited.
  • Passengers included EU security advisers and former UK Prime Minister Boris Johnson. Former CIA Director David Petraeus was reportedly on another train at the station.
  • Polish authorities identified the weapons as jet-powered Geran-5 drones. Ukraine and Poland called the strike an escalation.
  • NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte pledged additional support for Kyiv. Allies committed €70 billion in military aid for 2026 at the Ankara summit and have pledged to match it in 2027; over $10 billion has flowed through PURL and US foreign military sales in the past year.

Left view

The Guardian and France 24 present the strike as deliberate signaling at a NATO border rather than a stray, pointing to the Yahodyn rail gateway's role as the main EU–Ukraine supply artery and to who was aboard. The argument is that incremental probing works unless allies make the cost explicit, and that European air defense commitments have lagged the rhetoric.

Right view

Right-leaning coverage is split. Hawks at National Review-style outlets read it as confirmation that Moscow escalates when it senses hesitancy and push for tighter Article 4 consultations. Restraint-minded outlets argue the near-miss is precisely why the US should press harder for a negotiated end rather than deepen exposure, and note that a strike with no casualties is being used to justify open-ended commitments.

Watch for

Whether Poland invokes Article 4 consultations again, and whether allies extend air-defense coverage over western Ukrainian rail corridors — a step that edges toward direct involvement. Insurance and freight rates on the Ukraine–EU rail route are the practical tell. Also watch for movement on the stalled US-brokered proposal that envoys carried between Moscow and Kyiv earlier this month.

Pennsylvania measles outbreak nears 700 cases with a third death under review

Facts

  • Pennsylvania's health department confirmed 693 measles cases across 38 counties as of Monday, up from 624 a week earlier and 460 earlier in the outbreak.
  • More than 130 people have been hospitalized. The state has confirmed two measles-associated deaths this year; a third — an adult woman with underlying health problems — is under review. Two infants died last month of related complications.
  • Four vaccinated people have contracted the disease: three adults and one child. Two MMR doses are about 97% effective, and breakthrough cases are typically milder.
  • The department has run more than 100 pop-up vaccination clinics, including in churches, community centers and homes.

Left view

NPR and CNN tie the outbreak's size to falling MMR coverage and to federal messaging that has softened on vaccine recommendations, arguing that state health departments are absorbing the consequences of a national retreat. The breakthrough cases are presented carefully — evidence of how much virus is circulating, not of vaccine failure.

Right view

Right-leaning outlets have given the outbreak lighter coverage, focusing on local response and on the breakthrough infections as reason for candor about vaccine limits rather than mandates. Some argue the deaths involved people with serious underlying conditions and that the policy response should be targeted protection of the vulnerable, not broad restrictions. Mainstream and left coverage is clearly leading on this story.

Watch for

Whether the case curve bends after the vaccination push — measles has a roughly 10–14 day incubation, so this week's numbers reflect exposures from Labor Day weekend. Watch for spread into neighboring states, school exclusion policies as the academic year gets underway, and whether the third death is confirmed, which would make this Pennsylvania's deadliest measles year in decades.

Saudi Arabia threatens a firm response to Houthi attacks as Gulf risk premium holds

Facts

  • Saudi Arabia warned it will react firmly to Yemen's Houthis after ballistic missile and drone attacks wounded 13 civilians in the kingdom's south.
  • Saudi Arabia shut a major crude pipeline after drone attacks — a route specifically built to bypass the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Brent crude rose as much as 7.1% last week to $108, its highest settlement since May 19 and its biggest one-day move in six weeks. It has held above $108.
  • Iran's security chief Ali Larijani has ruled out negotiations with Washington until conditions are met; Trump said on Sept. 1 he is not trying to force Iran back to talks.
  • China's foreign ministry separately warned against a space arms race, urging Washington to stop expanding military capabilities in orbit.

Left view

The Guardian and NPR argue the strike-for-strike cycle has produced no diplomatic opening and a large economic bill, and that the administration's refusal to pursue talks leaves the conflict without an off-ramp. The Yemen front is presented as the predictable widening of a war framed at the outset as narrow.

Right view

Fox News and the Washington Examiner frame Saudi resolve as evidence that regional partners are carrying more of the burden, and treat Larijani's refusal as proof Tehran was never a serious negotiating partner. The counterargument from restraint-minded conservatives at outlets like The American Conservative is that energy prices are now an American domestic problem with a midterm attached.

Watch for

S&P Global's crude research head Jim Burkhard describes a market adjusting to unresolved conflict and persistent maritime risk rather than returning to calm. Watch whether Saudi retaliation hits Yemeni port infrastructure, which would tighten Bab al-Mandab further; whether the shut pipeline restarts; and diesel, which at a record $6.06 a gallon is the freight-cost channel that pushes energy inflation into everything else.
