The Big Stories Today

Trump says the Iran war ends "after the election" as Saudis press for strikes on the Houthis

Facts

  • Speaking Saturday, Trump said the war with Iran — now in its seventh month — will end "very soon," and most likely just after the November 3 midterms. He said Tehran is stalling to complicate the election, and that he has no regrets about starting it.
  • Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has urged Trump to take direct military action against the Houthis, who on Friday seized Perim (Mayun) island at the mouth of the Bab el-Mandeb and now hold most of the Yemeni coastline along the strait.
  • Saudi Arabia's East–West pipeline remains shut after a drone strike Thursday in the Riyadh and Medina regions; Riyadh and Baghdad both say the drones came from Iraq, where Iranian-backed militias operate.
  • Gulf Cooperation Council diplomats are due to meet their Iranian counterpart in Oman on Monday on a temporary arrangement for Hormuz shipping. Iran says the strait stays closed until Washington meets terms from the lapsed June interim deal.
  • Reuters and Bloomberg report Riyadh is rerouting crude through the Suez Canal — longer and costlier for Asian buyers. WTI settled at $100.05 Friday, Brent at $104.61.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian frame the Houthi advance as the clearest evidence yet that a blockade-plus-sanctions strategy has not worked: seven months in, Iran has more leverage over global shipping than it did in February, not less. Vox and MSNBC focus on the election timing — the argument being that if the administration believes the war can be ended "immediately after" the midterms, it is fair to ask why it cannot be ended immediately before, and voters are paying $4.31 a gallon in the gap.

Right view

Fox News is leading with the Saudi request for US action and treating the Houthi gains as a case for escalating rather than folding. The National Interest argues the Perim seizure should force a rethink — that naval blockade without ground-denial has ceded a chokepoint to Tehran's proxy. The Free Press and Washington Examiner have pushed the harder line that a negotiated Hormuz arrangement now would ratify Iranian control of the waterway.

Watch for

Monday's Oman meeting is the near-term price signal: energy desks at Bloomberg and Reuters put a workable transit arrangement as worth roughly $8–12 off Brent, while a US strike on Houthi positions pushes it toward $115–120. Also watch whether Saudi's East–West pipeline restarts — it is the only meaningful route bypassing both chokepoints, and traders read its status as a proxy for Gulf export capacity. The White House has internally discussed the war lasting past January 2029, which is not the timeline Trump is offering publicly.

The Fed meets Wednesday and markets expect a rate hike — Warsh's first

Facts

  • August CPI, released Friday, rose 0.4% on the month for a 3.4% annual rate, matching estimates. Core eased to 2.4%.
  • Gasoline did more than a third of the monthly damage, up 27.4% year over year; fuel oil was up 52%.
  • CME FedWatch put the odds of a quarter-point hike at roughly 90% after the print, up from about 70% the day before; Polymarket had it near 79%.
  • It would be the first hike of the cycle and the first policy move under Chair Kevin Warsh, who warned at Jackson Hole in late August that inflation remains too high.
  • The FOMC concludes Wednesday. Bank of America has penciled in three increases from here.

Left view

NPR and The Atlantic note that the inflation the Fed would be tightening into is largely an oil shock imported from a war the administration chose — squeezing households with higher borrowing costs on top of $4.31 gas, while core inflation is actually falling. Some economists quoted by CNBC go further, arguing there is no empirical basis for a hike and that Warsh is talking up inflation so he can claim credit when energy prices normalize.

Right view

The WSJ editorial board and National Review have backed Warsh's hawkishness as a credibility restoration after years of a Fed they see as behind the curve, and welcome his stated preference for a "quieter" central bank that does less signaling and less mission creep. Analysts cited by CNBC flagged that this posture puts the Fed at odds with a Treasury and a White House that want cheap money into an election.

Watch for

Whether Trump attacks the decision publicly — he has argued energy prices, not policy, are the inflation problem, and a hike three weeks before early voting is a political flashpoint. Rate-sensitive sectors (housing, regional banks, small caps) are the trade; the 2-year yield is the cleanest read on whether markets think Warsh is done after one. A hold instead of a hike would be a genuine surprise and likely a sharp equity rally.

BRICS adopts a New Delhi declaration built around routing trade off the dollar

Facts

  • The 18th BRICS summit ran September 12–13 in New Delhi. Modi said the 45-page New Delhi Declaration was adopted unanimously.
  • The declaration commits members to expand trade settled in their own national currencies and to link domestic payment and messaging systems — India's UPI, China's CIPS, Russia's SPFS — so transactions can clear outside SWIFT.
  • It condemned unilateral tariff and non-tariff measures as inconsistent with WTO rules, and warned about rising nuclear-confrontation risk.
  • Xi Jinping's first India visit in seven years. Putin, Iran's Pezeshkian, Ramaphosa, Prabowo, el-Sisi, Anwar, Abu Dhabi's MBZ and Abiy Ahmed also attended.
  • No common BRICS currency was endorsed; India has resisted that. Trump has threatened tariffs up to 100% on the bloc, which the White House says rests on executive-order authority.

Left view

The Guardian and Al Jazeera present this as the predictable cost of using the dollar and tariffs as coercive instruments: the more Washington weaponizes financial plumbing, the more countries build alternate plumbing. The Modi–Putin–Xi photo is read as a diplomatic failure for an administration that spent the year pressuring India on Russian oil and tariffs simultaneously.

Right view

The Wall Street Journal and The Dispatch are more measured, noting that interoperable national-currency settlement is not a reserve-currency challenge and that BRICS remains internally divided — India wants a US trade deal and has no interest in a Chinese-led monetary bloc. The Washington Examiner casts the summit as an authoritarian photo op given Iran's presence while it blockades a shipping lane, and supports the tariff threat as leverage.

Watch for

Whether Trump actually applies the 100% tariff and to whom — applying it to India would blow up a trade deal New Delhi is still seeking. Economists broadly expect BRICS Pay to matter at the margins first (Russia–India and Iran–China energy flows, sanctions evasion) rather than in dollar reserve share, which has moved slowly for a decade. Watch the rupee and the yuan's share of trade invoicing for the real signal, not the communiqué.

Mail ballots are going out while the courts are still fighting over the USPS rule

Facts

  • USPS finalized its ballot-mail rule on August 26 under Executive Order 14399. It requires states to hand the federal government lists of every voter sent a mail or absentee ballot, and to use federally reviewed envelopes with unique barcodes. USPS says it will decline to deliver ballots in states that don't supply the list.
  • The Supreme Court in late August lifted a stay letting parts of the executive order proceed, but did not rule on the policy's legality.
  • On September 3 a federal appeals panel again refused to let USPS implement the plan. A district judge separately blocked it as ballots began going out.
  • DOJ has returned to the Supreme Court asking it to clear the way before November 3. Democratic-led states and voting-rights groups have filed a third round of challenges.
  • All 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats are on the ballot.

Left view

The Washington Post and NPR emphasize the operational chaos: election officials are printing and mailing ballots under rules that may change by court order in October, and the deliver-or-not provision means a state's dispute with USPS could strand real voters' ballots. Votebeat's reporting stresses that the practical question is timing — even a government win may land too late to implement cleanly, which is its own risk.

Right view

Fox News and the House Oversight Republicans frame barcoded envelopes and voter lists as basic chain-of-custody — the kind of tracking already used for other accountable mail — and argue confidence in mail voting is low because it is the least auditable method. Thirteen Republican state attorneys general, led by Alabama, backed the rule in comments. The right's counter to the chaos argument: the litigation, not the rule, is what's late.

Watch for

A Supreme Court order on DOJ's application is the thing to watch, and the Purcell principle — the Court's reluctance to change election rules close to an election — cuts against implementation this cycle even if the government ultimately prevails on the merits. Election administrators are warning about ballot-return deadlines in states with tight receipt windows; expect litigation over individual rejected ballots in November regardless of how the rule fight ends.

Facts

  • At his Irish golf course Saturday and in a meeting with Taoiseach Micheál Martin in Dublin, Trump said reunification of the Republic and Northern Ireland would be "fantastic" and that it will happen eventually.
  • US policy since the Good Friday Agreement has been deliberately neutral on Northern Ireland's constitutional future.
  • Sinn Féin's Mary Lou McDonald said the comments show the unity conversation has shifted and called on Dublin to start preparing.
  • UK Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch compared the remarks to Trump's talk about Canada and Greenland, calling them unhelpful loose talk.
  • New British PM Andy Burnham said weeks ago that a border poll is off the table.

Left view

The Guardian and PBS NewsHour report the substance carefully — that whatever one thinks of unity, an American president freelancing on another country's constitutional settlement removes a guardrail that has held for thirty years, and that the Good Friday framework depends on Washington being seen as an honest broker by both communities. Irish commentators on the nationalist side welcome it; unionist commentators read it as a threat.

Right view

The Daily Wire and NY Post are covering it largely as Trump being Trump — an offhand remark at a golf course, not a policy shift, and one that costs the US nothing. National Review has been more critical of this genre of comment, arguing that presidential asides about allies' borders have real diplomatic costs even when unserious, a line consistent with its position on the Canada and Greenland remarks.

Watch for

Whether the State Department walks it back. The mechanical constraint is unchanged: under the Good Friday Agreement, a border poll is the UK Northern Ireland Secretary's call and requires evidence a majority would vote for change — polling has not shown that. Watch Stormont's reaction this week and whether the DUP uses it to harden its position; analysts see little near-term market or policy effect, but it complicates the UK–US relationship at a moment when Burnham is new in the job.

Facts

  • September 10 marked one year since Kirk, 31, was shot and killed at Utah Valley University in Orem while speaking on a Turning Point USA campus tour.
  • Tyler Robinson, 22, faces aggravated murder charges. A judge ruled weeks before the anniversary that the case can proceed as a capital trial.
  • In the year since, employees at airlines, universities and media companies were fired or suspended over posts about the killing; NPR has tracked a wave of resulting lawsuits, and at least one arrest over an online post.
  • Vice President Vance urged listeners to contact employers of people celebrating the killing; Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy publicly pressed airlines to fire rather than suspend.
  • Reporting this week describes a durable pattern of neighbor-on-neighbor doxing that outlasted the immediate aftermath.

Left view

NPR and The Atlantic focus on the speech-retaliation machinery the killing set in motion — cabinet officials pressuring private employers over employees' political speech is, in this framing, the government doing indirectly what the First Amendment forbids directly, and the resulting lawsuits are the test case. The Guardian adds that political violence in the US is not one-directional and that the year's response flattened that.

Right view

The Washington Times and Townhall mark the anniversary around Kirk's legacy and Turning Point's growth, and argue the left never reckoned with the celebration that followed his murder. Breitbart is running the sharpest version, cataloguing anniversary commentary it reads as continued contempt. The Free Press and National Review have taken the harder internal line — that conservatives who cheered firings cannot now claim the free-speech mantle.

Watch for

Robinson's capital trial is the next real news event and will re-run all of this at higher volume; legal analysts expect pretrial motions over venue and the death-penalty notice into next year. The wrongful-termination and First Amendment retaliation suits NPR has tracked are the ones with precedential stakes — a ruling that official jawboning of employers is state action would reach well beyond this case.

August tied the hottest month ever recorded as El Niño strengthens early

Facts

  • Copernicus reports August 2026 tied July 2023 as the warmest month on record globally, at about 1.65°C above the preindustrial average.
  • It was the first month since November 2025 to exceed 1.5°C.
  • Average sea surface temperature outside the polar oceans hit a record high; central and eastern tropical Pacific waters saw record warmth and severe marine heat waves.
  • Western Europe recorded its warmest summer on record, passing 2003.
  • Scientists attribute the spike to continued fossil-fuel warming plus an El Niño that intensified faster and earlier in the year than is typical.

Left view

The Guardian and Scientific American connect the record to policy: the US is seven months into an oil-price shock that has made fossil production politically untouchable, and climate spending has been deprioritized. UN officials quoted by UN News frame 1.5°C breaches becoming routine as the headline, not the tie for hottest month.

Right view

Reason and the WSJ editorial board typically emphasize the El Niño component — that a strong, early El Niño mechanically lifts global mean temperature and that single-month records overstate the trend signal. The broader right-of-center argument this year has been that energy security comes first while a war constrains supply, and that adaptation spending outperforms emissions targets on cost.

Watch for

Forecasters expect this El Niño to peak in late 2026, which historically means the following calendar year runs hotter still — 2027 is the year to watch for an annual record. Nearer term, record Atlantic and Pacific sea surface temperatures raise late-season hurricane and typhoon intensity risk, which reinsurers price through the fourth quarter. Watch European gas and power markets if a warm autumn follows the record summer.
