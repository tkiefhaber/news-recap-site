The Big Stories Today

White House starts enforcing Trump's ban on CNN, MS NOW and Politico

Facts

  • Trump announced Friday on social media that CNN, MS NOW and Politico were barred from the White House “effective immediately,” accusing the three of reporting what he called fake news.
  • Enforcement began Saturday morning. CNN senior White House reporter Betsy Klein, MS NOW correspondent Akayla Gardner and Politico's Cheyenne Haslett were each turned away from the grounds; Klein's credential was deactivated and Haslett's badge was confiscated. Gardner cleared two security gates before being told her badge had been disabled.
  • Trump suggested the list could grow to include other outlets.
  • The move follows the administration's 2025 restrictions on the Associated Press, which sued; that litigation is still ongoing.
  • Politico said it will keep covering this White House and defend its First Amendment rights. MS NOW declined to comment on Trump's initial threat.

Left view

NPR, The Washington Post and Al Jazeera are covering this as a direct test of press freedom rather than a personnel dispute, leaning on constitutional scholars: NBC News quoted Jameel Jaffer of Columbia's Knight First Amendment Institute calling the move pretty clearly unconstitutional and predicting a challenge would succeed, and CNN quoted Bruce Brown of the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press saying a ban like this would be flatly unconstitutional. Salon frames it as an escalation in a running campaign against unfriendly coverage, with the AP case as the template.

Right view

Conservative coverage has been notably thin on this one — mainstream and left-leaning outlets are leading it, and the loudest right-of-center voices so far are echoing Trump's own framing that the three outlets have forfeited access through slanted coverage rather than defending the constitutional question. It is worth watching the libertarian right — Reason and The Dispatch have opposed credential revocations under presidents of both parties — and the WSJ editorial board, which criticized the AP ban in 2025, for whether they break from the White House here.

Watch for

Legal analysts expect suits within days. The district court ruled against the administration in the AP case on viewpoint-discrimination grounds, which gives the three outlets a live precedent, though the administration has argued the White House complex is not a public forum and access is discretionary. Watch whether the White House Correspondents' Association responds collectively, whether more outlets are added, and whether the pool system itself is restructured — the administration already took control of pool rotations from the WHCA last year.

Greenland deal lands — and Greenlanders read it very differently than Trump does

Facts

  • Trump announced Friday an agreement with Denmark and Greenland granting the US permanent access, basing and overflight rights on the island, after months of threatening to take the Arctic territory by force from a NATO ally.
  • The deal bars non-NATO military bases, blocks China and Russia from establishing any presence, and limits adversary investment. Greenland remains under Danish sovereignty.
  • Trump said the pact gives the US permanent control over security and “all other needs” in Greenland, and that his administration would immediately begin building a larger military presence.
  • Danish PM Mette Frederiksen's office said all three governments will sign next week during the UN General Assembly, but the Danish and Greenlandic parliaments still have to act before it takes effect. Her statement stressed that the agreement recognizes both peoples' right to self-determination.
  • Greenland PM Jens-Frederik Nielsen said the deal recognizes Greenland's interests. Full text was not released; it is unclear how “adversary” gets defined going forward.
  • The security pact follows a separate rare-earth magnet mining deal announced earlier.

Left view

NPR and The Philadelphia Inquirer led with Greenlanders' unease rather than the signing — one resident quoted by the Inquirer said the way Trump describes it, it sounds like he is being handed a monopoly over the island. The Guardian-side framing is that an agreement extracted after months of annexation threats is coercion dressed up as diplomacy, and that the sovereignty language is a face-saving wrapper around permanent US basing rights. The Conversation argued the deal papers over a deeper collapse of trust between Washington and Copenhagen.

Right view

Fox News called it a historic win securing permanent American military rights, and one Fox guest compared it to the Alaska purchase. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the deal permanently guarantees US security interests in the Arctic at zero cost to the American taxpayer. RedState and PJ Media treated the China-and-Russia exclusion clause as the real prize and evidence that the pressure campaign worked. Notably, a Fox News segment interviewing Greenlanders on the street produced considerably cooler reactions than the DC framing.

Watch for

The signing on the UNGA margins this week is the easy part; ratification in Copenhagen and Nuuk is not. Greenland's parliament has an independence-minded majority, and analysts expect the fight there to center on whether US access survives a future Greenlandic independence — which appears to be exactly what Washington negotiated for. Also watch the undefined “adversary” language, Moscow's and Beijing's responses, and whether Arctic basing spending shows up in the next defense request. Mining and rare-earth analysts expect follow-on magnet and critical-minerals announcements.

Russia holds Duma elections inside occupied Ukraine for the first time

Facts

  • Russia's parliamentary election is being held for the first time in the four Ukrainian regions Moscow illegally annexed after the 2022 invasion, along with Crimea, annexed in 2014.
  • Nationwide voting runs Friday through Sunday. In the occupied territories and some Russian border areas, balloting actually began in late August; officials cited security.
  • Ukrainian rights activists say election commission members went door to door escorted by Russian troops and security agents to press residents to vote.
  • Kyiv denounced the vote as illegal. European Commission spokesperson Christian Wigand called it another blatant violation of international law.
  • Context: Russian forces netted roughly five square miles of Ukrainian territory between Aug. 4 and Sept. 1, per Russia Matters' tracking, and Ukraine's agriculture minister estimates strikes have destroyed about 90% of the country's food-logistics infrastructure.

Left view

NPR and The Guardian emphasize the coercion reporting — armed escorts accompanying ballot collection — and treat the exercise as theater designed to manufacture a paper trail of consent. The broader left-of-center argument is that any settlement that freezes current lines effectively ratifies this, and that Western non-recognition has to be stated loudly now rather than negotiated away later.

Right view

Hawkish conservatives at National Review and the WSJ editorial board read the vote as Moscow bolting annexation into its own legal architecture precisely because it expects a negotiated freeze — making the case that pressure should increase, not ease. The restraint-minded right is less exercised, arguing the vote changes nothing on the ground and that the real question is whether Kyiv can hold lines through another winter given the logistics damage.

Watch for

Expect a formal EU non-recognition statement and likely sanctions designations aimed at officials who organized the vote. The bigger downstream question analysts are flagging: this hardens Russia's negotiating floor just as Ukraine diplomacy is supposed to surface at UNGA this week. Watch also the reported feud between rival Ukrainian intelligence services — a shootout in central Kyiv between competing units has exposed an internal power struggle that Western officials see as a real risk to command cohesion.

UN General Assembly opens with the Iran war, Ukraine and AI on the table

Facts

  • World leaders convene in New York this week. The high-level agenda is dominated by the US–Iran war and the Strait of Hormuz closure, Ukraine, and the governance of artificial intelligence.
  • Trump is scheduled to address the Assembly Tuesday, his second consecutive year at the rostrum, and is expected to frame US peace efforts around an America First view of the global order.
  • The Security Council is expected to hold high-level sessions on Ukraine, AI and the Middle East.
  • Secretary-General António Guterres finishes his term at the end of 2026, making this his last UNGA and putting the succession race in the background of every meeting.
  • The US–Denmark–Greenland agreement is slated to be signed on the margins this week.

Left view

The Guardian and Vox frame the week around American retreat from multilateral institutions — aid and UN funding cuts, withdrawal from agencies, and a Security Council that has been deadlocked on both major wars. The argument is that the UN's weakness in 2026 is substantially a product of deliberate US policy, and that the AI governance push from Guterres, aimed at giving the Global South a seat, is the last real item on his agenda.

Right view

National Review and The Free Press take the opposite lesson: the institution failed to stop either war, and UNGA week is largely a diplomatic trade show where the real work happens in bilateral meetings — the Greenland signing being this year's example. The Wall Street Journal editorial line has consistently argued US leverage comes from bilateral deals and hard power, not General Assembly speeches.

Watch for

The single market-moving item is any signal of Iran diplomacy. With Hormuz effectively closed, energy desks say even a credible hint at de-escalation talks is worth several dollars off Brent; conversely, a hard-line Trump speech Tuesday would likely firm crude. Also watch the Guterres succession jockeying, whether a Ukraine framework gets floated publicly, and whether any AI governance text emerges with US buy-in.

The Fed's first hike in three years starts biting — and the politics are ugly

Facts

  • The FOMC voted 12–0 on Sept. 16 to raise the target range a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00%, the first increase since July 2023.
  • The statement said inflation remains elevated. August CPI rose 0.4% on the month and 3.4% over 12 months. Gasoline jumped 3.9% in August and is up 27.4% year over year, accounting for more than a third of the monthly increase; the overall energy index is up 16.3% from a year ago. Shelter rose 0.3% on the month, 3.0% over the year.
  • The dot plot shows 16 of 18 participants expecting at least one more hike, four seeing two more, and no cuts penciled in through 2027.
  • The administration lobbied hard against the move — the president, vice president, Treasury secretary and senior economic advisers all pushed for no hike or a cut. Trump called FOMC members “clowns,” said rates should be 1% or less, and said he had discussed the vote with Chair Kevin Warsh beforehand.
  • The decision came roughly two months before the November midterms.

Left view

CNN and Marketplace framed it as the Fed defying the White House, and Democrats moved immediately to tie higher prices and higher borrowing costs to Trump — arguing the energy shock driving the inflation number is a direct consequence of the Iran war and that tariffs are compounding it. House Democrats have also warned that rate hikes into a softening labor market risk avoidable job losses, a line they used through 2022–23 as well.

Right view

Fox Business and the WSJ editorial page credited Warsh with protecting Fed independence, and pointed out that the unanimous 12–0 vote undercuts the White House claim that Warsh was boxed in by holdover appointees. The supply-side argument on the right is that this is an energy-supply problem, not a demand problem, and the fix is Hormuz and domestic production rather than a rate path — which is roughly where Trump and the hawkish right actually agree, if for different reasons.

Watch for

Economists broadly expect another 25bp at the next meeting unless crude retreats. The Dallas Fed's own working paper on the Iran war's inflation pass-through is being cited on both sides. Concrete downstream effects to watch: the 10-year hovering near 5% pulling 30-year mortgage quotes higher, corporate refinancing pushed into 2027, and a consumer squeezed by a $4.48 national gas average heading into heating season, with European gas futures at three-year highs.

Impeachment resolutions fly in both directions as the midterm map hardens

Facts

  • Rep. Suzanne Bonamici (D-Ore.) introduced H.Res.1391 to impeach Education Secretary Linda McMahon, alleging a willful and systemic refusal to comply with the law and false statements to Congress over her effort to wind down the department.
  • Senate Democrats are pressing in parallel for an inspector general investigation and calling for her removal.
  • Days earlier, Rep. Thomas Massie (R-Ky.) introduced articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth — making two Cabinet secretaries targeted in as many days, from opposite directions.
  • Sixty-one House members and two non-voting delegates — 26 Democrats and 37 Republicans — have announced retirements this cycle; 29 of them are running for other offices.
  • Generic congressional ballot averages put Democrats roughly seven points ahead as of Sept. 19, around 50.5% to 43.2%, a cycle high for the party.

Left view

MSNBC and Vox treat the McMahon resolution as overdue accountability: the Education Department was created by statute and an executive can't dissolve it unilaterally, so the constitutional question is separation of powers rather than policy. The affordability numbers — 27% gas inflation, a rate hike, wide voter dissatisfaction on prices — are the actual midterm argument, and the impeachment push is framed as keeping the legal record clean rather than as a realistic removal effort.

Right view

National Review and the Washington Examiner note that neither resolution goes anywhere in a Republican House and read both as messaging exercises. The more interesting story on the right is Massie: a Republican impeaching a Republican Defense Secretary reflects a real GOP split over war powers and the Iran campaign, not a bipartisan turn against the administration. The 37 Republican retirements are the number conservative strategists are actually worried about.

Watch for

Neither resolution will get a floor vote unless offered as privileged, which would force leadership into an uncomfortable tabling motion — that's the procedural thing to watch this week. Election analysts caution that a D+7 generic ballot in September historically overstates the eventual margin, and that redistricting and the Senate map blunt the translation into seats. The Iran war's trajectory and the gas price line through October are the two variables most forecasters say could still move it.
