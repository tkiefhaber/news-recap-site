Midterms, 2 days out: 51 million ballots cast, Democrats hold a high-single-digit generic lead, Senate still a coin flip

Facts

  • More than 51 million Americans have already voted ahead of Tuesday, Nov. 3. All 435 House seats, 35 Senate seats and 36 governorships are on the ballot. Democrats need a net gain of three House seats and four Senate seats to flip both chambers.
  • The RealClearPolling average has Democrats ahead 51.5% to 43.1% on the generic ballot. The final PBS News/NPR/Marist poll (Oct. 19-20) had Democrats up 52-44 among likely voters, Trump's approval at 40%, and cost of living (34%) narrowly ahead of preserving democracy (33%) as the top issue.
  • Senate control likely turns on Michigan, Texas, Kansas, Iowa, North Carolina and Alaska. A Washington Post-Schar School poll released Saturday has Democrat Adam Hamilton up 52-43 over GOP Sen. Roger Marshall in Kansas, which hasn't elected a Democratic senator since 1932. Emerson has that race at 50-47.
  • Trump holds in-person rallies in Iowa and Kansas today after canceling both and then reversing course Friday. In Michigan, Democrat Abdul El-Sayed campaigns in Detroit and Southfield against Republican Mike Rogers in a race strategist Celinda Lake calls "really close."

PBS NewsHour frames the race as a referendum driven by disapproval of Trump, with that disapproval pushing early turnout. Coverage summarized by Al Jazeera ties voters' cost-of-living anger to tariffs and the Iran war. Its story also notes rising Islamophobic attacks on El-Sayed.

Right view

The Washington Examiner's live coverage stresses GOP skepticism of the polls showing Democrats competitive in Kansas and Texas. It points to past polling misses in red-state Senate races. The Federalist argues the Senate polls may be overstating Democrats, and Senate Majority Leader John Thune has predicted Republicans will outperform the polls and keep the chamber.

Watch for

Pollster Natalie Jackson told Al Jazeera that Democrats are fairly likely to take the House but the Senate is unclear, and that polls may undercount Republicans. Indiana and Kentucky close first Tuesday evening, and close races (Alaska above all) may take days. Markets are treating a split Congress as the base case. A Democratic House would make tariff and war-funding fights the 2027 storyline.

Facts

  • An AP review documents Trump repeatedly linking federal help to Republican victories. In North Carolina, he said he'd settle the remaining Camp Lejeune veterans' claims if Republicans win Congress, adding "if we don't win, there's no money." In September he tied Hurricane Helene recovery money to Senate candidate Michael Whatley's election, then said he was joking.
  • In Kansas, Trump said he'd make year-round E15 ethanol sales permanent with Sen. Roger Marshall but not if Democrat Adam Hamilton wins. He also suggested a reelected Sen. Jon Ossoff (D-Ga.) and Ohio Democratic governor candidate Amy Acton would get less help.
  • CNN counts transactional promises of this kind in roughly half the states with key Senate contests.
  • The White House says Trump simply wants a GOP majority to keep delivering on taxes, drug prices and immigration.

Coverage collected by NewsCord, including MS NOW and NYT syndication, ties it to a broader pattern of using federal power for retribution.

Right view

Right-leaning coverage of this story is thin. The main pushback comes from the White House and GOP candidates rather than conservative outlets. Spokesman Davis Ingle said Democrats have no agenda to improve Americans' lives. Whatley didn't disavow the Camp Lejeune remarks, saying only that caring for affected veterans is a priority.

Watch for

Whether the remarks move suburban and veteran-heavy districts in North Carolina and Georgia. Whether any post-election aid decisions (Helene, Camp Lejeune, E15) track the results. That would likely bring Democratic oversight demands if they win the House.

Iran: Doha talks continue as Rubio says no strike decision yet and Khamenei orders forces to be ready

Facts

  • Iranian negotiators met Qatari mediators in Doha on Saturday, after more than 12 hours of talks in Tehran on Friday that a source told Reuters were coordinated with Washington. Tehran rejected Qatar's seven-day trust-building plan. Its own plan, which Trump has rejected, demands lifting the US blockade of Iranian ports, unfreezing assets and approving an Iranian-backed Hormuz route.
  • Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Trump hasn't decided on new strikes, prefers diplomacy, and that "talks have actually never stopped." Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi said Iran is studying a letter from VP JD Vance. Trump has downplayed the letter as being about medical supplies.
  • Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei ordered the armed forces to prepare a "geographically unrestricted" response to any attack. Security chief Mohsen Rezaei said Tehran is debating whether to lift its ban on nuclear weapons if fighting resumes, though no decision has been made.
  • Axios reports a US official saying Trump may "do something bold" if Republicans do badly Tuesday. A White House official denied that elections factor in. Per Axios, Hegseth and Rubio doubt a deal can be reached, while Vance, Witkoff and Kushner favor more talks.

Left view

International and left-leaning coverage (Al-Monitor/Reuters, Middle East Monitor relaying Axios) focuses on the electoral timing. Its argument: Washington is holding fire for domestic political reasons and using the threat of a post-election strike as leverage. That story also highlights Trump urging Israelis to keep Netanyahu in office so the two can "finish the job."

Right view

Little fresh right-leaning commentary surfaced today. The hawkish case is mostly coming from inside the administration. Per Axios, Hegseth and Rubio favor further weakening Iran's leadership over a deal. Rubio has previously said Trump won't allow "fake negotiations" to be used as a delay tactic.

Watch for

Allies were warned strikes could start as early as Nov. 4. With WTI above $100 and Brent well above that, analysts see any renewed strikes or Hormuz incidents pushing crude higher fast, which would undercut the winter-blend relief at the pump. Rezaei's nuclear-ban comment is the escalation tail risk to watch.

Alaska Senate: DOJ asks a court to set aside every ballot cast for the "second Dan Sullivan"

Facts

  • The Justice Department wants all ballots for challenger Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. segregated. Its suit would delay certification if GOP Sen. Dan Sullivan loses and votes for the second Sullivan could have changed the result. The ballot lists both men, the incumbent with GOP and incumbent tags and the challenger with no party.
  • The New York Times reported that Democrat Mary Peltola's campaign worked with operatives to recruit the second Sullivan; the campaign denies it. A state judge restored his candidacy in June after the Division of Elections tried to disqualify him.
  • Peltola, the DSCC and the State of Alaska have all moved to dismiss. They argue the federal law DOJ cites covers voter registration, not ballot design. A federal judge has fast-tracked the case.

Left view

Democracy Docket and NBC News lead with the Democrats' filing, which calls DOJ's bid "wholly baseless." They cast the suit as a pre-planned way to block an Alaska result if the GOP loses.

Right view

Fox News leads with the "alleged spoiler plot" but concedes DOJ faces a "major legal hurdle." Heritage's Don Palmer says statistics alone won't prove confusion. Cato's Walter Olson thinks the state is likely to prevail, and Notre Dame's Derek Muller calls the needed analysis "impossible to measure after the fact."

Watch for

A ruling on the motions to dismiss could come before or right after Tuesday. Alaska's ranked-choice count runs for weeks, so if Senate control comes down to Alaska, this case could keep the chamber undecided into December.

Israel: thousands rally for an Eisenkot–Arab party deal to unseat Netanyahu

Facts

  • Final results leave Netanyahu's bloc with 53 of 120 Knesset seats and Gadi Eisenkot's bloc with 48, so neither can govern alone. Arab-led parties (Ra'am and the Joint List) won 19 seats on about 840,000 votes, a 64% jump from 2022.
  • Eisenkot had ruled out relying on Arab parties, but adding their 19 seats would give him 67. Thousands rallied in Tel Aviv urging him to make that deal, and Netanyahu supporters counter-protested.
  • Netanyahu accused Arab parties of "stealing" the election with Qatari money. Qatar called the charge disinformation.

Left view

Al Jazeera frames the record Arab turnout as the story of the election. It notes that right-wing circles have called the turnout "electoral terrorism," and treats the Tel Aviv rally as a "last chance for real change."

Right view

The Jerusalem Post's coverage focuses on Netanyahu's bloc leading in the count and on the coalition math. Likud and its allies argue a government relying on Arab parties would be illegitimate, a line amplified at the counter-protest.

Watch for

PBS NewsHour lays out the paths: a minority government, a unity deal, or a quick repeat election. The outcome shapes the Iran war. Trump has openly urged Israelis to keep Netanyahu, and an Eisenkot-led government could be more open to the Qatari track.

Ukraine: US clears a $2.1B sale of 500 Patriot missiles, but Zelensky warns delivery could take years

Facts

  • The State Department approved a potential $2.1 billion sale of 500 PAC-3 Patriot interceptors, with Lockheed Martin as prime contractor. Ukraine pays using money from Canada, Germany, Norway and other allies, and the department says no US funds are involved and US readiness won't suffer.
  • Zelensky thanked Trump but warned that delivery could take years. He asked the US and Europe to send Patriots from their own stockpiles now and refill them later from Ukraine's order. He says Russia has hit Ukraine's energy grid more than 30 times in two weeks.
  • The sale comes days after Trump waived new Russia sanctions just before they were set to take effect.

Left view

CNN stresses how scarce Patriot interceptors are and how long the production line is. It argues an approval that doesn't arrive before winter does little for Ukraine's grid right now, especially alongside the sanctions waiver.

Right view

No distinct right-leaning take surfaced today. Pro-administration framing emphasizes that Ukraine and Europe are paying and US taxpayers aren't. That's consistent with Trump's "they buy, we sell" approach to Kyiv.

Watch for

Whether European allies release stockpiled interceptors before peak winter strikes. More business for Lockheed's PAC-3 line, and pressure to expand production. Congressional pressure on the sanctions waiver if Democrats win the House.
